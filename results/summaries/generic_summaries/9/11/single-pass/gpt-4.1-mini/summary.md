# Education in Poland
Polish education begins with compulsory primary education starting at age six or seven, lasting eight years after a 2017 reform that abolished the three-year junior high school (gimnazjum). Upper secondary education includes general secondary schools (liceum) lasting four years leading to the Matura exam for university entry, and vocational technical schools (technikum) of five years offering vocational training alongside the Matura. Tertiary education follows the Bologna Process, typically with a three-year bachelor's and two-year master's degree; doctoral studies last about three years. Foreign languages, mainly English and German, are widely taught. Historical reforms of 1999 and 2017 restructured the education system to improve academic performance, though the 2017 reform faced criticism for a nationalistic focus. A 2006 "zero tolerance" policy was introduced to address school violence.

# Education in Pakistan
Pakistan’s education consists of pre-school, primary (years 1–5), middle (years 6–8), secondary (years 9–10), and higher secondary (years 11–12), with compulsory education ending typically at age 16. The system blends national curricula with alternatives like GCE O/A Levels and vocational training via Technical and Vocational Education Training (TVET). Tertiary education includes universities offering bachelor's, master's, MPhil, and PhD degrees, with professional degrees in engineering, medicine, and law. Gender disparity remains a significant issue, with female literacy and enrollment lagging behind males due to socio-cultural barriers and infrastructure challenges, although some progress has been made. COVID-19 prompted distance learning initiatives like Teleschool, but access remains uneven. The education quality faces challenges including teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and insufficient resources. Madrassas play a role, especially among poor families, but their influence and curriculum have been sources of concern. Notable Pakistani scholars have made international contributions in diverse academic fields.

# Education in the Czech Republic
Compulsory education spans nine grades in elementary schools divided into two stages, followed by multiple types of secondary education: vocational schools, professional schools (including lyceums), grammar schools, and special/practical schools for students with disabilities. A universal leaving exam called "maturita" is required for tertiary entry. Higher education includes public and private universities and higher professional schools offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees with various academic titles. The school year runs September to June, divided into two semesters with standard holidays. Special education integrates developmentally disabled students with tailored curricula or practical training.

# Education in Russia
Russian education comprises general (preschool to secondary) and professional education, with compulsory nine-year schooling and optional upper secondary education. Primary education starts at age 6 or 7; secondary divides into elementary, middle, and senior classes, with vocational training offered at specialized schools alongside normal schools. The Unified State Examination standardizes university admissions. Higher education includes bachelor’s (4 years), specialist (5-6 years), and master’s (2 years) degrees, with postgraduate Candidate of Sciences and Doctor of Sciences degrees reflecting research achievements. Disability education is provided through specialized institutions or integrated settings. Recent reforms align higher education with the Bologna Process. Science outreach activities and patriotic education programs have been introduced amid government controls and criticisms.

# Education in Canada
Education is primarily a provincial responsibility, with compulsory schooling beginning between ages five and seven and ending by 16–18 depending on province. Canada offers diverse educational pathways, including publicly funded primary, secondary (usually K–12), post-secondary universities, and vocational colleges. The country boasts high tertiary education enrollment and expenditure (~5.2% of GDP). Constitutional provisions protect certain religious and language minority education rights. Assessment agencies coordinate data collection via OECD programs like PISA. Admission criteria vary, with prestigious universities having competitive cutoffs. Private schools represent about 6% enrollment and often show higher achievement partly due to socio-economic factors. The federal government plays limited direct educational roles.

# Education in South Korea
South Korea’s education system, strongly influenced by Confucianism, includes six years of elementary school (ages 6-12), three years of middle school, and three years of high school, with compulsory education through middle school. High school offers general academic and vocational tracks; Meister Schools provide specialized vocational training modeled on German systems to combat stigmatization of trades. Higher education consists of universities and colleges awarding bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. University admissions heavily rely on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT). English education is emphasized from early stages, though proficiency has historically lagged behind spending. The system faces criticism for intense competition, social stigma against vocational education, excessive tutoring, and student stress. Reforms aim to balance academic pressure and improve vocational training.

# Education in Bangladesh
Bangladesh’s education system comprises pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with instruction offered in Bengali and English. National curriculum is standardized, with limited subject choice until secondary levels when students select streams (arts, commerce, science). Education management involves the Ministry of Education and National Curriculum and Textbook Board. Secondary certificates (SSC and HSC) are administered regionally. Tertiary education includes public and private universities, with varied emphasis. Madrasas operate parallel education with governmental oversight of Alia madrasas. Challenges include teacher absenteeism, infrastructure deficits, gender disparities (largely reduced at primary and secondary levels), and quality concerns. NGO non-formal education supplements gaps. Literacy stands at about 75% (2022 census).

# Education in the Netherlands
The Dutch system mandates education from age five to eighteen with partial education required up to sixteen. Primary education lasts eight grades; secondary education is divided into VMBO (vocational), HAVO (general secondary), and VWO (pre-university), with specialized tracks and profiles introduced in upper years. Tertiary education includes HBO (universities of applied sciences) and WO (research universities) aligned with the Bologna Process. School selection at age 12 is informed by aptitude testing, though early tracking has been criticized for misplacement risks and socioeconomic bias. School types include public, special (religious), and general-special, all government funded. A broad network of international schools exists. Education quality indicators have declined somewhat in recent PISA assessments.

# Education in Saudi Arabia
Saudi education, rooted in Islamic tradition, is free for all and governed mainly by the Ministry of Education and the Technical and Vocational Training Corporation. It comprises pre-primary (kindergarten), compulsory primary (6 years), intermediate (3 years), secondary (3 years), technical, and tertiary education. Multiple reforms aim to reduce rote learning and bolster skills relevant to the modern economy. Female education has expanded significantly since the 1960s; women now comprise a majority of university students. Gifted education programs exist alongside special education and inclusive practices for disabilities, though inclusivity faces challenges. The country hosts private and community international schools regulated differently. Literacy rates have improved, though regional disparities and gender gaps persist. Criticisms focus on religious content overemphasis, insufficient critical thinking, and intolerance in curricula, with ongoing reforms attempting to modernize education.

# Education in Japan
Japan’s education system, formal since the 6th century, underwent major modernization post-Meiji Restoration (1868), aligning with Western models. Compulsory education covers six years primary and three years lower secondary, with upper secondary attendance nearly universal. Higher education includes universities and colleges offering bachelor’s to doctoral degrees. The academic year starts in April and schools emphasize moral education alongside academics. English is a compulsory subject, supported by the JET program. Extracurricular “shadow education” (cram schools) is widespread due to competitive university entrance exams. Criticisms include excessive academic pressure, limited free time, and insufficient nurturing of independent thinking. Special needs education has progressed towards inclusion. International schools serve expatriates but do not fulfill compulsory education requirements.

# Education in New Zealand
New Zealand offers free primary (Years 1–8) and secondary (Years 9–13) education from age five, with compulsory attendance between 6 and 16 years. The schooling system includes state, state-integrated (often faith-based), and private schools. Indigenous Māori language education has seen revival through immersion and bilingual programs since the late 20th century. The national curricula (NZC and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa) cover eight learning areas, with senior students often working towards the National Certificate of Educational Achievement (NCEA). Tertiary education is regulated within a unified qualifications framework, funded primarily by government subsidies and student fees. Challenges include disparities in Māori and Pacific Island educational outcomes, and bullying is a persistent problem addressed through policy and funding.

# Education in Mexico
Mexico’s basic education is divided into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (secundaria, grades 7–9), and high school (preparatoria, grades 10–12). Primary and secundaria are compulsory, with recent reforms extending compulsory education to preparatoria. Secondary education offers academic and vocational tracks. The country has pursued educational integration and inclusive education for students with disabilities, though challenges in accessibility and teacher preparation persist. The education system faces criticism regarding infrastructure, low secondary completion, and union influence. Higher education follows a US-style model with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Intercultural Universities target indigenous inclusion. Literacy rates have risen significantly. Mexico hosts over 150 international schools following various curricula. Historical development reflects colonial and post-independence influences, secularization, and modernization efforts.
