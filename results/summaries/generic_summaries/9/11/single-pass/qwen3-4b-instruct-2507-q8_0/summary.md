**Education in Poland**  
Poland's education system evolved significantly with the 2016 "Law on School Education," which expanded primary school to 8 years (replacing the previous 6-year primary + 3-year gimnazjum model) and abolished gimnazjum. Compulsory education ends at age 18. Primary school (grades 1–8) includes core subjects like Polish, math, and foreign languages. Students must attend kindergarten before starting primary school. Upper secondary education includes general (liceum), vocational (technikum, zasadnicza szkoła zawodowa), and profiled programs. A numeric grading system (2–5) is used, with 5.0 being the highest. Foreign language education requires two languages, with exams influencing secondary school admissions. Historically, the system emphasized a Polish and Eurocentric curriculum, removing non-aligned movements and promoting nationalism, which critics argue undermines historical inclusivity.

**Education in Pakistan**  
Pakistan's education system is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary education (ages 5–14) includes core subjects like Urdu, English, and science, with a focus on religious education. Secondary education (ages 14–18) leads to the SSC and HSSC, with streams like pre-medical, pre-engineering, and commerce. Tertiary education includes bachelor’s, master’s, and vocational degrees. Gender disparity persists, with lower female enrollment and performance, especially in rural areas. The system faces challenges in quality, teacher training, and relevance. Literacy rates have improved, reaching 60.65% in 2023, but remain below regional averages. Religious education is deeply embedded, with madrassas providing free food and shelter, though often criticized for promoting extremist ideologies. The curriculum includes controversial content, such as negative portrayals of Jews and Israel, and promotes ideological narratives.

**Education in the Czech Republic**  
Czech education is structured into elementary (grades 1–9) and secondary (grades 10–13). Elementary school has two stages, with a focus on core subjects and foreign languages. Secondary education includes grammar schools (general curriculum), vocational schools, and lyceums (academic focus). Students must pass the maturita exam for university admission. The maturita includes compulsory Czech and world literature, along with a foreign language. Tertiary education includes public and private universities, with degrees like bachelor’s, master’s, and doctorates. The system emphasizes academic rigor and standardized testing. Special and practical schools serve students with developmental disabilities, though integration remains a challenge. The education system is well-funded and structured, with a strong focus on academic excellence and national curriculum standards.

**Education in Russia**  
Russia’s education system is divided into general and professional tracks. General education includes primary (grades 1–4), basic (5–9), and secondary (10–11) levels, with a nine-year compulsory program. Secondary education includes vocational and academic streams, with a standardized Unified State Examination (USE) replacing traditional university entrance exams. The system has transitioned toward the Bologna model, introducing bachelor’s and master’s degrees. Religious education is integrated into the national curriculum, with strong influence from state and religious institutions. The system faces criticism for excessive focus on rote learning, lack of teacher training, and political indoctrination. The government promotes patriotic education, including "Important Conversations" lessons, which have drawn criticism for their militaristic tone. The education system is heavily centralized, with strong state oversight and a focus on national identity.

**Education in Canada**  
Canada's education system is provincial, with no national curriculum. Compulsory education starts at age 5 and ends at 18. The system includes kindergarten through Grade 12, with a strong emphasis on equity and inclusion. Secondary education includes high school (grades 10–12) and offers pathways to university or vocational training. The education system promotes diversity, with programs in Native studies, antiracism, and indigenous cultures. The government supports equitable access through policies like Section 15(2), which allows for targeted programs for disadvantaged groups. The system is well-funded, with a focus on equity, teacher development, and student well-being. Vocational education is available through colleges and career institutions. Criticism includes concerns over academic pressure, teacher autonomy, and the impact of standardized testing on student well-being.

**Education in South Korea**  
South Korea's education system emphasizes academic excellence, with a rigorous curriculum and high-stakes entrance exams. Primary education (grades 1–6) includes core subjects, with English introduced from grade 1. Secondary education includes science, foreign language, and arts tracks, with students taking the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) for university admissions. The system is highly competitive, with a strong focus on university entrance. Vocational education has been modernized through "Meister Schools," which offer skilled trades and high employment rates. However, the system is criticized for high student stress, excessive night study, and gender disparities in access. The education system is highly effective in producing skilled graduates but faces criticism for its focus on rote learning and lack of creativity. The government has introduced reforms to promote a more balanced, competency-based approach.

**Education in Bangladesh**  
Bangladesh's education system is structured into pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary education (grades 1–8) is compulsory and includes core subjects in Bengali and English. Secondary education (grades 9–12) leads to the SSC and HSC, with streams in arts, commerce, and science. Tertiary education includes public, private, and international universities. The system faces challenges in quality, teacher training, and access, especially in rural areas. Gender parity exists in primary and secondary education, but disparities persist in higher education. The government has invested in expanding access and improving infrastructure. Literacy has improved to 74.66% (2022), but remains below regional averages. The system emphasizes religious education, with madrassas playing a significant role in rural areas. The government has introduced reforms to improve curriculum quality and reduce drop-out rates.

**Education in the Netherlands**  
The Netherlands has a well-structured, decentralized education system with compulsory education from age 5 to 16. Primary education (grades 1–8) is followed by secondary education (grades 9–12), with pathways into vocational (VMBO), academic (HAVO/VWO), or higher education. The system emphasizes inclusivity, with special schools for students with disabilities and a strong focus on student well-being. The Cito test is used to guide secondary school placement. The system is highly effective in outcomes, with strong performance in international assessments. Criticisms include early placement decisions based on IQ tests, which may disadvantage gifted students, and concerns about academic pressure. The government promotes equity and student autonomy, with strong teacher training and support systems. The system is well-funded and responsive to societal needs.

**Education in Saudi Arabia**  
Saudi Arabia's education system is state-run and religiously oriented, with a strong emphasis on Islamic studies. Primary education (grades 1–6) is compulsory and includes Arabic, Islamic education, and mathematics. Secondary education (grades 7–12) offers general and vocational tracks, with a strong focus on religious instruction. Higher education includes universities and private institutions, with a growing number of female students. The system faces criticism for its focus on rote learning and lack of innovation. Reforms, such as the Tatweer program, aim to modernize the curriculum and promote critical thinking. The government has introduced reforms to improve teacher training and expand access to education. The system is highly effective in achieving basic literacy and is expanding access to higher education, particularly for women. However, challenges remain in fostering creativity and innovation.

**Education in Japan**  
Japan's education system is highly structured and rigorous, with a strong emphasis on academic achievement. Primary and secondary education (grades 1–13) is compulsory, with a focus on core subjects like Japanese, mathematics, science, and foreign languages. The system is known for its high academic standards and intense competition for university entrance. Students often attend private cram schools (juku) to prepare for exams. The curriculum emphasizes discipline, respect, and conformity. The system has faced criticism for high student stress, bullying, and a lack of creativity. Reforms have been introduced to reduce academic pressure and promote well-being. The government has implemented policies to improve student mental health and reduce bullying. The system is highly effective in producing skilled graduates but remains under pressure to modernize and promote innovation.

**Education in New Zealand**  
New Zealand's education system is free and compulsory from age 5 to 18. It includes early childhood education, primary (grades 1–8), and secondary (grades 9–13) education. The national curriculum (NZC) and Māori-medium curriculum (Te Marautanga o Aotearoa) emphasize holistic development, with a strong focus on Māori language and culture. The system promotes inclusivity, with a strong commitment to educational integration for students with disabilities. The government supports teacher autonomy and student well-being. The system has faced criticism for declining academic performance in mathematics and science, with reforms introduced to improve teaching methods. The government has invested in teacher training and curriculum development. The system is well-funded and responsive to societal needs, with a strong focus on equity and student well-being.

**Education in Mexico**  
Mexico's education system is structured into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (grades 7–9), and high school (grades 10–12) levels. The system is under the control of the Ministry of Public Education (SEP), with a strong emphasis on public access and equity. Education is compulsory from age 6 to 18. The system faces challenges in quality, infrastructure, and access, especially in rural areas. The government has implemented reforms to improve teacher training, curriculum, and student outcomes. The system includes vocational training (Conalep) and intercultural universities to serve indigenous populations. The system is highly effective in increasing school attendance and literacy, with a literacy rate of 94.86% (2018). However, challenges remain in addressing gender disparities and improving outcomes in higher education. The system is well-funded and responsive to societal needs, with a strong focus on equity and inclusion.
