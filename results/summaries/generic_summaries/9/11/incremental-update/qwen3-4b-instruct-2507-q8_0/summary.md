## **Education in Poland, Pakistan, the Czech Republic, Russia, Canada, South Korea, Bangladesh, the Netherlands, Saudi Arabia, Japan, New Zealand, and Mexico**

Poland features a compulsory education system from age 6 to 18, with a modernized primary structure (grades 1–8) and a Bologna-aligned tertiary system emphasizing academic equity and national/European history over ideological content. Pakistan has a strong English-based foundation with high primary completion (67.5%), but faces systemic challenges including low graduate rates (6%), gender disparities, and ideological bias in madrasa curricula, particularly in negative portrayals of Jews and Israel. The Czech Republic offers rigorous, nine-year elementary education followed by secondary tracks with the Maturita exam for university entry; public tertiary education is free, with strong teacher-student ratios and academic structure. Russia provides nine-year compulsory education with standardized university admissions via USE, a Bologna-adopted tertiary system, and a strong focus on foundational skills, though post-2021 "patriotic lessons" raise concerns about academic freedom and indoctrination. Minority language education (e.g., Tatar, Bashkir) exists in over 6,800 schools but remains unevenly supported.

Canada has a decentralized, provincial system with compulsory education from age 5–7 to 18 in some provinces, strong tertiary funding, and growing emphasis on Indigenous and vocational pathways. South Korea emphasizes rigorous academics and near-universal secondary completion (98%), with specialized high school tracks and vocational "Meister Schools" improving employment outcomes; however, intense competition, hagwons, and student well-being issues persist. Bangladesh operates a multi-sectoral system with declining teacher attendance (15.5%) and low education expenditure (~2% of GDP), though gender parity exists and literacy has improved from 51.77% to 74.66%. The dual madrasah system (Qawmi and Alia) provides Islamic education, with Alia aligning to general curricula. The Netherlands features a highly equitable, government-funded system with compulsory education from age 5 to 18, including flexible pathways like the *brugklas* and structured secondary tracks (VMBO, HAVO, VWO) leading to vocational (MBO), applied (HBO), or research (WO) higher education. The system emphasizes student support, equity, and adaptability, though early academic tracking and declining international rankings remain concerns.

Saudi Arabia’s education system is rooted in Islamic values, with compulsory primary and secondary education and strong female enrollment (60% of university students), supported by women’s universities like PNU. Gifted and special education programs are expanding, with over 6,000 students reached through the Summer Enrichment Program and 91 dedicated centers; however, teacher shortages and negative school climates persist. Reforms under *Tatweer* promote critical thinking and secularism, removing anti-Semitic content from textbooks. Japan maintains a disciplined, high-performing system with compulsory education through grade nine and strong STEM outcomes, though academic stress, bullying, and the prevalence of cram schools (*juku*) raise well-being concerns. The system includes inclusive support for students with disabilities and a strong emphasis on lifelong learning. New Zealand offers free primary and secondary education (ages 5–19), with a national curriculum and growing Māori language education through *kōhanga reo* and *kura kaupapa Māori*. Despite progress, challenges include poor math performance, persistent achievement gaps for Māori and Pacific students, and systemic bullying. Mexico’s education system evolved from colonial Catholic control to a secular, state-run model under Benito Juárez and later reforms under Cárdenas and Torres Bodet, with a 2013 reform shifting control to federal and state governments. Indigenous access remains limited, prompting Intercultural Universities. The system has improved literacy and attendance (97.5% by 2020), though disparities in rural and Indigenous communities persist. Notably, Mexico’s universities are gaining international recognition: the National Autonomous University of Mexico ranks 103rd globally (QS), and private institutions like IPADE and EGADE are among the top 10 preferred by international recruiters.
