# Comparative Overview of Global Education Systems

## South Asia

In Pakistan, education comprises primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a low literacy rate and significant regional disparities. Challenges include underfunding, outdated curricula, teacher shortages, rote learning, social inequalities, and limited access for females, particularly in rural areas. Efforts to improve access are ongoing, but poverty and institutional weaknesses persist. Bangladesh has seen marked improvements in literacy rates, rising from 51.77% in 2011 to 74.66% in 2022, and has achieved gender parity in primary and secondary education. Despite government initiatives—such as free schooling and stipends—rural regions continue to struggle with attendance, dropout rates, resource constraints, and teacher absenteeism. Higher education growth and quality remain limited, especially outside urban centers.

## Russia and East Asia

Russia’s centralized system is standardized, with compulsory schooling, specialization options, and reforms inspired by the Bologna Process. Debate surrounds employability, constant government control, and concerns over censorship and support for minority languages. In East Asia, Japan and South Korea both feature rigorous, standardized education systems with a high emphasis on academic achievement. Japan offers broad curriculum options and nearly universal senior high attendance, but the competitive environment generates stress and stifles independent thinking. South Korea is highly competitive with a strong private tutoring culture, significant academic pressure, and social stigma against vocational pathways, despite attempts to reform student welfare and career readiness.

## Central and Western Europe

Poland and the Czech Republic have compulsory education emphasizing discipline and academic rigor, with Poland promoting foreign language learning and Czech universities offering free tuition in Czech. School violence is addressed proactively, and systems support special needs students. The Netherlands ensures compulsory education from age 5 and features diverse secondary tracks and autonomy for schools, with funding parity between public and religious institutions. Reforms aim to boost inclusivity and address performance gaps, though socioeconomic disparities and declining outcomes remain concerns.

## North America

Canada’s education system is determined at the provincial level, featuring diverse curricula and compulsory schooling. Notable features include Quebec’s CEGEP, varied religious school funding, and a prominent postsecondary sector. The system faces challenges in tuition inflation, equity, and a lack of national accreditation, with ongoing debates over regional policy and grade inflation.

## Latin America, Middle East, and Oceania

Mexico’s education evolved from religious origins to a centralized, compulsory system emphasizing national unity and free textbooks. Primary attendance rates are high, but completion rates dwindle in later years due to rural infrastructure issues. Reforms have focused on inclusion for indigenous and special needs students, with expansion in vocational options and improved rankings for its leading universities. Saudi Arabia’s state-funded education system has modernized through Vision 2030, increasing female participation and technological integration, yet faces continued challenges in vocational relevance, teacher quality, and regional gender disparities. New Zealand provides free, compulsory schooling rooted in Māori and European traditions, with national curricula for English- and Māori-medium education. While support exists for low-decile schools, achievement gaps persist for Māori and Pacific Island students, and declining performance in core subjects and the absence of a national anti-bullying policy are notable criticisms.
