**Education in Pakistan** covers a comprehensive overview of the formal, non-formal, and informal education systems, including primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. The national system is inspired by the English model, with pre-school (Play Group, Nursery, Kindergarten) followed by junior (Years 1–5) and middle (Years 6–8) schools. Students study a set curriculum in arts, computer studies, general science, modern languages, mathematics, religious education (Islamic Studies), social studies, and Pakistan Studies. The system faces challenges such as low primary completion rates (67.5%), gender disparities, outdated curricula, and teacher shortages. Gender inequality persists, with lower female enrollment and performance, especially in rural areas, and limited female representation in leadership and STEM fields. The education system suffers from declining quality due to outdated content, poor infrastructure, and lack of teacher training. Despite reforms like the 2009 National Education Policy aiming to increase spending to 7% of GDP, public expenditure remains low at around 2%, with significant regional disparities. Alternative qualifications such as GCE, IGCSE, and AP exist, and technical education (e.g., DAE) offers vocational pathways. Madrassas, while widespread, are criticized for promoting religious extremism and rote memorization. Literacy rates are improving but remain below South Asian averages, with women lagging behind men. Notable alumni include Nobel laureate Abdus Salam and other prominent scientists. The system is also influenced by religion, with curricula often emphasizing Islamic content, and a controversial history of promoting anti-minority narratives in textbooks. Education expenditure, teacher education, and curriculum relevance are ongoing challenges, with reforms aimed at improving equity, quality, and access.
