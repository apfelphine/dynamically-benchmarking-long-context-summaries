# Education in Pakistan  
Pakistan's education system is influenced by the English model and is divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, along with non-formal and madrassa education. Primary education covers years 1–5 with curricula including sciences, languages, social studies, and religious education; about 66% complete this stage. Secondary education spans years 9–12, culminating in SSC and HSSC certificates, with academic and vocational streams available. Alternative qualifications like GCE O/A Levels and AP are also offered. Tertiary education includes bachelor's (pass and honours), master's, MPhil, and PhD programs with professional accreditation councils. Non-formal education includes vocational training and apprenticeships, while madrassas focus on Islamic education and serve many poor families. 

Gender disparity is a significant issue, with females facing lower enrollment and literacy rates partly due to social, cultural, and infrastructural barriers, although recent progress includes government mandates for primary education as a legal right. Women’s participation in STEM fields faces systemic challenges despite high enrollment in medical schools. Teacher education is under-resourced with an emphasis on memorization over application, impacting education quality. Technical and vocational education aims to develop labor skills but faces challenges in infrastructure and teaching quality. Distance learning gained prominence during COVID-19 via televised lessons and open universities, improving rural and female access. Pakistan plans to increase education expenditure from around 2% toward 7% of GDP, though infrastructural deficits persist. Notable Pakistani scholars include Nobel laureate Abdus Salam and scientist Atta-ur-Rahman. Education is heavily entwined with religion, with madrasas influential especially in poorer areas, and some curriculum content criticized for ideological bias. The literacy rate stands at about 60%, with substantial regional and gender disparities.

# Education in Russia  
Russia's education system comprises general and professional education. General education includes preschool, primary (4 years), basic general (5 years), and secondary general education (2 years), with compulsory nine-year schooling. Professional education ranges from vocational training to higher education including bachelor's (4 years), specialist (5–6 years), master's (2 years), and postgraduate degrees (Candidate and Doctor of Sciences). The system is transitioning towards the Bologna Process structure but maintains traditional models. Vocational schools and technicums offer qualifications equivalent to secondary education plus vocational skills. Unified State Examinations standardize university admissions, replacing inconsistent regional tests.

Special education is available for physical and mental disabilities with specialized schools and integration attempts. Postgraduate scientific degrees are distinct from academic titles, emphasizing research achievement. The government introduced laws regulating outreach activities related to education, and increased patriotic education has been implemented following recent geopolitical events. Education in minority languages persists, though some have sharply declined. Moscow State University and other federal universities hold prominent status. Russia’s university system faces challenges balancing consolidation and regional access.

# Education in Poland  
Poland’s education system consists of compulsory primary school (grades 1–8), junior high school (until 2019), and upper secondary education. In 2017, reforms restructured education by extending primary school to 8 years and abolishing junior high schools. Upper secondary education includes general secondary schools leading to the Matura exam and vocational/technical schools providing trade skills plus potential university access. Higher education follows the Bologna process with bachelor's, master's, and doctoral programs. A numeric grading system from 2 (fail) to 5 (excellent) is used at the university level. Foreign languages, primarily English and German, are taught widely. Poland has a long educational history, with early universities dating to 1364 and the world’s first state ministry of education founded in 1773. Recent reforms emphasize a Eurocentric curriculum, with debates on inclusivity and nationalism. Anti-violence measures in schools have been implemented to reduce bullying and harassment.

# Education in South Korea  
South Korea’s education, historically influenced by Confucianism, is highly competitive and structured. It includes 6 years of elementary, 3 years of middle, and 3 years of high school education. The system offers general and vocational tracks, with specialized “Meister Schools” modeled after German vocational education to revive job-oriented training. High school enrollment is near universal (about 98%), and approximately 69% of students complete tertiary education, heavily emphasizing university degrees. English education is widespread but has faced criticism for effectiveness. South Korea’s educational system faces challenges including high pressure on students, social stigma against vocational education, and mismatches between majors and jobs. Government policies aim to improve vocational training’s image and provide more balanced curriculum options. Heavy reliance on private tutoring ("hagwons") exists, though after-hours operations are regulated to reduce student stress.

# Education in the Czech Republic  
Czech education includes elementary school (9 grades split into two stages), secondary school types (vocational, professional, lyceum, grammar), and tertiary education. The maturita exam, compulsory for secondary graduates, is required for university admission. Vocational schools offer certificates, while professional and grammar schools lead to maturita. Universities offer bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees with various academic titles in specific fields. Special and practical schools cater to students with developmental challenges, often integrating students into mainstream education. The school year runs September to June, with multiple holidays. Historically, multiple education reforms have streamlined the system, balancing academic and vocational training.

# Education in the Netherlands  
Dutch education is administered by the Ministry of Education in coordination with municipal governments. Compulsory education starts at age 5 and continues until 18 or diploma attainment. The system includes elementary school (ages 4–12), culminating in a national test (Cito test) guiding secondary education placement into VMBO (vocational), HAVO (general), or VWO (pre-university) tracks. Secondary education offers differentiated curricula with specialization profiles. Higher education is divided into vocational universities (HBO) and research universities (WO), aligned with the Bologna Process awarding bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Vocational education is robust, spanning multiple levels and apprenticeship routes. Early childhood education, religious and special schools exist alongside public schools. The system has been criticized for early tracking potentially misallocating students. The Dutch education calendar includes regional staggered holidays to manage vacation times.

# Education in Canada  
Education in Canada is provincially managed with no federal oversight except in limited federal institutions. Compulsory schooling generally starts by age 5–7 and ends between 16–18. The system includes kindergarten, elementary, intermediate, and secondary (high school) levels, followed by tertiary education. Post-secondary includes universities awarding bachelor's, master's, and doctorates, and vocational colleges offering certificates and diplomas. Private and religious schools exist with varied funding; in some provinces public funding supports Catholic schools. Canada spends about 5.2% of GDP on education, with high tertiary enrollment. Admissions to universities rely on transcripts, standardized tests, and extracurriculars; vocational schools have lower entry barriers. Indigenous and minority education programs exist, but educational inequality remains a concern. Section 15(2) of the Canadian Charter allows differential treatment for disadvantaged groups to address equity. Some provinces include data collection on racial groups to address achievement gaps.

# Education in Japan  
Japan’s education system, rooted in Chinese and Confucian traditions, underwent modernization after the Meiji Restoration incorporating Western models. It consists of six years of elementary, three years of lower secondary (compulsory), and three years of upper secondary education, followed by higher education (universities, junior colleges). Despite compulsory lower secondary schooling, over 98% attend upper secondary. Emphasis on entrance exams fuels a shadow education system of cram schools (juku). Higher education includes four-year universities and professional schools; some six-year programs exist for medicine, law. Grading uses a five-step scale. Reforms have addressed inclusion of disabilities and promoted more holistic education. The system faces criticisms of high pressure on students, rote learning, bullying, and lack of creativity fostering. International schools teach in English but do not fulfill compulsory education. University rankings include prestigious institutions like University of Tokyo.

# Education in Bangladesh  
Bangladesh’s education system has four levels: pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (classes 9–12), and tertiary. Students follow either Bengali or English medium curricula, with government agencies overseeing curriculum, examination (PEC, JSC, SSC, HSC), and textbook distribution. Approximately 60% of primary teaching positions are for women. Secondary education is managed by regional boards conducting public exams. Madrasah education exists as parallel religious instruction, with Qawmi (private) and Alia (government subsidized) systems. Technical and vocational education prepares students for applied science and technology jobs. Non-formal education caters to school dropouts. Literacy rose from about 52% in 2011 to 75% in 2022. Challenges include overcrowding, poverty, rote learning, and quality disparities. Gender disparities persist primarily in rural areas but have decreased. Refugee education addresses Rohingya children in camps.

# Education in Mexico  
Mexico’s basic education is divided into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and high school (10–12). Primary and junior high are compulsory; high school was recently made compulsory. Curriculum may include bilingual education. Secondary education includes general, vocational, and technical tracks. Higher education follows the Bologna process with bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. Indigenous populations remain underrepresented in tertiary education, addressed through Intercultural Universities. Educational integration for special needs students is progressing but faces challenges in training and infrastructure. Quality issues include low secondary attendance and rural infrastructure deficits. The National Autonomous University of Mexico ranks as the top national university. The teachers' union historically dominated education but has been challenged by reform efforts.

# Education in Saudi Arabia  
Saudi Arabia’s education stems from Islamic roots, with early Quranic schools leading to modern institutions managed mainly by the Ministry of Education. Education is free and includes pre-primary, primary, intermediate, secondary, and higher education, with heavy religious content especially in early grades. Recent reforms introduced English language instruction from grade 1 and merged multiple Islamic subjects to streamline schooling. Higher education expanded since 1957, now including 24 government universities with growing female enrollment—women constitute approximately 60% of university students. Special education and gifted programs exist but face resource and cultural challenges. Private and international schools operate under varying regulations, often gender-segregated. Literacy has improved but regional disparities and critiques of rote learning, lack of innovation, and religious intolerance persist. The government is implementing modernization programs emphasizing critical thinking and vocational skills.

# Education in New Zealand  
New Zealand’s education, influenced by Māori traditions and European settlers, provides free and compulsory education from age 6 to 16 (up to 19 in practice). The system includes early childhood education, primary (Years 1–8), intermediate (Years 7–8 in some cases), and secondary education (Years 9–13). The national curriculum focuses on eight learning areas and includes Māori-medium education. Schools include state, state-integrated (religious or special character), and private institutions. Funding is primarily government-based with parental donations common. Tertiary education offers various qualifications and is funded through government grants, loans, and fees. Challenges include declining student performance in math and science, disparities in Māori and Pacific Islander achievement, and bullying concerns. Maori language revitalization is a significant focus, with immersion schools and curricula. The school year runs January to December with regional holidays.
