**Updated Summary of Key Educational Systems and Global Trends:**

Pakistan’s education system faces systemic challenges in literacy (60.65%), gender equity, rural access, and infrastructure, with persistent disparities by region and gender. While it has produced globally recognized scientists and scholars, structural weaknesses—such as outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and ideological content in madrassas—undermine quality and inclusivity. Gender gaps remain stark, with women’s literacy at 42% compared to 67% for men, and limited access to STEM and medical fields due to cultural and legal barriers. Inclusive policies, such as the 2018 Transgender Persons Act, represent progress, but patriarchal norms and socioeconomic constraints persist.

Canada operates under a decentralized model with provincial responsibility, offering flexibility in curriculum and program structure. Publicly funded primary and secondary education extends to ages 19–21, with alternative pathways for students unable to attend conventional schools. Post-secondary education includes colleges (offering diplomas, certificates, and associate degrees) and universities (offering bachelor’s, master’s, professional, and doctoral degrees), with vocational training supported through apprenticeships and technical programs. Approximately 6% of grade ten students attend private schools, primarily in Quebec, where higher academic achievement is attributed to strong parental and peer expectations. Private universities, now permitted in several provinces since 1999 (e.g., Trinity Western, Quest University), raise equity concerns, especially for low-income students. Faith-based schools receive variable funding: Ontario’s Catholic system is fully funded and constitutionally protected, though criticized by the UN for discrimination; other provinces provide partial funding or support through non-religious charter schools (e.g., Alberta). The system spans early childhood through tertiary levels, with regional variation in structure and governance.

Japan maintains a long-standing tradition rooted in early Chinese-influenced courtly learning, evolving through the Edo period and Meiji Restoration into a modern, Western-aligned system. Compulsory education spans elementary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and senior high (10–12), with a drop-out rate of less than 2% and over 98% of students advancing to upper secondary. Public high schools are tuition-free, while private schools are more expensive. English became compulsory at the elementary level in 2011. The system emphasizes academic excellence, with over 48,000 *juku* (cram schools) active, 86% of college-bound students participating in extracurricular preparation. This intense *shadow education* has led to high rates of bullying (612,496 cases in 2019), suicide, and psychological harm, particularly due to verbal bullying and limited free time. Despite progress in inclusive education—such as the 2006 integration of students with disabilities—systemic pressures on students remain severe. The national curriculum and grading system (using traditional terms like *shū* and *fuka*) are supported by policies like the Basic Act on Education and CRPD.

Bangladesh operates a three-tiered system (pre-primary to tertiary) with no formal middle school, managed by the National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB). Students follow one of three streams (Arts, Commerce, Science) after class 9. All academic certificates are based on standardized exams, with classroom performance not counted. Key features include 60% of primary teaching positions reserved for women, a dual madrasah system (Qawmi and Alia), and a growing refugee education crisis, especially for Rohingya children. Despite challenges—such as poor teacher attendance (15.5%), high drop-out rates, and outdated curricula—gender parity has been achieved in primary and secondary education, with literacy rising from 51.77% (2011) to 74.66% (2022). The system includes international, religious, and technical/vocational institutions, with 44 government and 101 private universities offering programs in engineering, medicine, and agriculture.

Mexico’s education system has deep historical roots in pre-Columbian and colonial structures, including elite *calmecac* and *cuicacalli* institutions and Catholic-led colonial education. Post-independence, secular, free, and compulsory education was established, with centralization under the SEP promoting nationalist curricula. Despite progress in attendance (reaching 97.5% by 2020), only 45% of students complete secondary school and 25% enroll in post-secondary education. A 2013 reform shifted control from the teachers’ union (SNTE) to the government, sparking ongoing tensions. Higher education follows a US-style model (*Licenciatura*, *Maestría*, *Doctorado*), with only 23% of 23–35-year-olds holding degrees, indicating a significant access and completion gap. Intercultural Universities were established to address indigenous enrollment disparities. The system includes 151 international schools and a growing emphasis on bilingualism and cultural inclusion.

Saudi Arabia’s education system is rooted in Islamic traditions, with early *kuttabs* linked to mosques. Primary education is compulsory and includes Arabic, Islamic studies, math, and science, with English introduced from first grade. Religious instruction dominates, and the system has faced criticism for rote learning, intolerance toward non-Muslims, and anti-Semitic/anti-women content in textbooks. However, significant progress has been made in girls' education: women now constitute 60% of university students, and the government established the General Presidency for Girls’ Education and Princess Nora University—the largest women-only university globally. The 2030 Vision and the $2 billion Tatweer reform program aim to modernize education by promoting critical thinking, secularism, and vocational training. Private schools are regulated, with a shift in 2018 to centralized educational buildings causing backlash and reversal. International schools (203 as of 2015) follow national regulations and enforce gender segregation.

New Zealand’s education system is free, compulsory, and secular, from age 5 to 19, structured across 13 year levels (Years 1–13). Early childhood education is available in centre-based or parent-led settings (e.g., *Kohanga Reo*, *Playcentre*), guided by the *Te Whāriki* curriculum. The national curriculum (NZC or *Te Marautanga o Aotearoa*) is followed in state and state-integrated schools, with private schools following equivalent curricula. Schools are categorized by funding: state (free, majority), state-integrated (retain cultural/religious character, charge fees), and private (fee-based, ~4% of students). Alternative options include home schooling and former charter schools, now fully integrated. Most students complete the NCEA by Year 13, with some pursuing Cambridge or IB qualifications. Māori language education has seen a revival since the 1980s, with *kōhanga reo* and *kura kaupapa Māori* recognized under the Education Act 1990; however, enrolment in immersive programs has declined, and most Māori students still receive instruction without language immersion. Enrolment schemes in state schools—now limited to 700 of 2550 schools—prioritize local students via home zones, sparking criticism over fairness and property value inflation. **Māori language schools (kōhanga and kura kaupapa) demonstrate better academic and social outcomes for Māori students**, reinforcing the value of culturally responsive and language-immersive education. School days run from 8:00–9:00 am to 3:00 pm, spanning four terms with breaks in between. Manual training schools, established in the 1890s, were later integrated into mainstream curricula. Tertiary education is regulated by the New Zealand Qualifications Framework and includes both formal and informal learning. Funding for primary and secondary schools is per-student, with additional support for low-decile and smaller schools; private schools rely on tuition fees and partial government subsidies. Special needs students receive ongoing funding (ORS). Tertiary funding combines government grants and student fees, with criticism over rising costs and student debt. Students access loans and allowances, with interest on loans abolished for New Zealand residents in 2006. Educational standards declined after the 1995 TIMSS results, with reforms like the Numeracy Development Project failing to improve outcomes due to confusion and neglect of foundational skills. **New Zealand faces ongoing challenges in basic numeracy, with 9-year-olds ranking poorly in international assessments and declining proficiency in arithmetic. While a 2012 report ranked New Zealand 8th globally, its validity is questioned due to testing errors. Māori and Pacific students lag behind Pākehā students in academic performance, and school bullying—particularly cyber and physical forms—is widespread, with serious incidents prompting calls for stronger anti-bullying policies that remain inconsistently implemented.**

**Cross-national priorities for improvement remain consistent**:  
- Equitable access and inclusive curricula that promote gender, ethnic, and linguistic diversity.  
- Modernization of curricula to include critical thinking, soft skills, and scientific literacy.  
- Teacher development and practical training to improve pedagogical quality.  
- Reduction of ideological content, especially in religious and nationalistic education.  
- Support for vocational education, which remains underfunded and stigmatized.  
- Transparency in data collection and policy implementation, particularly in addressing achievement gaps and ensuring culturally responsive education.  

These cross-national patterns underscore the need for balanced, human-centered education reforms that prioritize equity, well-being, and global relevance.
