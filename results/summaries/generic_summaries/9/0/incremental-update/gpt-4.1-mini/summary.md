# Updated Working Summary of Education in Pakistan, Russia, Poland, South Korea, Czech Republic, the Netherlands, Canada, Japan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, and New Zealand

## Pakistan

Pakistan’s British-influenced education system spans primary to tertiary levels, facing low literacy (60.65% in 2023), gender, regional, and rural-urban disparities. Challenges include poverty, feudal norms, teacher absenteeism, weak quality, and low funding (~2% GDP). Madrassas often lack scientific curricula and may promote extremist views; female STEMM education faces obstacles. Secondary education culminates in regionally administered academic or vocational exams. Technical and vocational education aims to upskill but suffers from quality issues and outdated curricula. Distance and non-formal education address digital divides. Pakistan hosts 439 international English-medium schools; many students study abroad, especially in China. Notable scholars include Nobel laureate Abdus Salam.

## Russia

Russia’s education ranges preschool to postgraduate levels with Bologna bachelor-master structures. Early education relies on a single teacher, progressing to subject specialists. After nine years, students select academic or vocational tracks; male vocational students may face conscription risks. Unified State Exams standardize university admissions; over 50% of adults hold tertiary degrees. Special education serves 20% of children inclusively or segregated. Minority languages are preserved. A 2021 law restricts non-formal outreach. Patriotic lessons emphasize traditional values amid militarization. From 2024, foreign students must pass Russian language exams.

## Poland

Poland mandates education from ages 6/7 with 6+3+3 or 8+4/5 structures, featuring a nationalist history curriculum revising communist-era terms. Upper secondary divides into general, vocational/technical, and special needs schools. Tertiary education follows Bologna with bachelor (3 years), master (2 years), and doctoral (3 years) degrees, assessed on a 2.0–5.0 scale with exam retakes. Students study two foreign languages, mainly English; reforms mandate external exams. Anti-violence policies and disciplined civil-servant teachers promote stability. Poland’s educational tradition dates to the 12th century, with Jagiellonian University (1364).

## South Korea

South Korea’s education blends Confucian meritocracy with Western models, spanning infant to tertiary levels; literacy rose from 22% (1945) to over 90% (1980s). Secondary completion is ~98%, with general and specialized tracks. From 2025, credit systems will allow course selection by interest. Vocational high schools enroll 20%, including stigmatized Meister Schools guaranteeing employment. “Employment First, College Later” policies align education with labor markets. Intense private cram schools (hagwons) exacerbate inequalities. University admission hinges on the CSAT. Prestigious institutions include “SKY,” KAIST, and POSTECH. English education emphasizes oral skills and is largely privately funded. Challenges include vocational stigma, high academic pressure, inequality, and job mismatches.

## Czech Republic

The Czech school year runs September–June in two semesters. Elementary education spans nine grades initially taught by one teacher (grades 1–5), adding subjects and two foreign languages (grades 6–9). Secondary education requires entrance tests and offers vocational, professional, lyceum, and grammar tracks culminating in maturita exams for university entry. Special and practical schools serve developmentally disabled and underperforming students, increasing inclusive integration. Tertiary education includes higher professional schools and universities with Bologna bachelor (3 years), master (1–3 years), and doctoral degrees. Public universities offer free Czech-taught education under age 26; private schools charge tuition.

## Netherlands

Dutch compulsory education starts at age 5, partially until 18; schools are mainly public or special (often religious), mostly government-funded; private schools are rare. Elementary (ages 4–12) ends with the Cito Eindtoets guiding secondary placement. Secondary features a three-year basisvorming followed by VMBO (4 years vocational-theoretical), HAVO (5 years applied sciences), or VWO (6 years research universities), including classical languages. HAVO and VWO share a common curriculum before specialization via four profiles. Early tracking draws criticism for inequality and misplacement. Dutch literacy and numeracy rankings declined since 2006. Vocational MBO (1–4 years), HBO (applied bachelor), and WO (academic bachelor to PhD) follow Bologna standards. The system evolved through 19th–20th century reforms to establish equal funding and streamline secondary education.

## Canada

Canada’s provincially managed system (1871–1943) varies regionally. Kindergarten starts around age 5; compulsory attendance lasts until 16–18. Secondary ends at Grade 12 (Grade 11 in Quebec) with regional adaptations; public religious and language schools coexist constitutionally with secular boards. Private schools enroll about 6%, mainly in Quebec, often with better outcomes. Private universities include faith-based and non-denominational schools; some provinces permit private degree-granting institutions. Ontario fully funds Catholic schools; Alberta funds charter but not religious schools. Education divides into early childhood (3–6), elementary (1–6), intermediate (7–9), and secondary (10–12); Quebec uniquely uses CEGEP bridging. Post-secondary includes community colleges and universities offering bachelor to doctoral degrees; admissions consider grades, extracurricular activities, and references. Skilled trades require practical training and Red Seal exams. Canada invests about 5.2% GDP in education. Rising post-secondary tuition coincides with policies easing permanent residency for international students. About 51% of young adults hold tertiary qualifications; secondary diploma attainment varies by gender.

## Japan

Japan’s education merges Chinese and Western influences from elementary to higher education with integrated special education. Compulsory education covers Grades 1–9; upper secondary attendance is 98.8% though not mandatory. Curriculum includes Japanese, math, science, compulsory English, social studies, arts, physical and moral education, informatics, and home economics. Public and private schools coexist; 55% of high schools are private. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, technology colleges, and special training colleges with over 2.8 million students. Admission relies on competitive exams driving cram schools (juku). Annual costs average ¥1.4 million with financial aid available. Since the 1990s, Japan advanced special needs education with Resource Rooms and inclusive policies. Academic pressure causes psychological issues and bullying, which are increasing. English-language international schools do not fulfill compulsory requirements. Legal frameworks guarantee equal access for disabled students with tailored supports.

## Bangladesh

Bangladesh’s system covers pre-primary to tertiary levels, guided by the National Curriculum and Textbook Board. Bengali and English are mandatory; students select Arts, Commerce, or Science streams from grades 9 and 11. National exams occur at grades 5, 8 (JSC discontinued 2023), 10, and 12 based on written/practical tests without homework or participation credit. Primary education falls under the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education; secondary is regionally managed. Public universities are subsidized with competitive admissions; private universities must build permanent campuses within 12 years. Technical and vocational education offers diplomas leading to engineering. Alternative streams include international English-medium schools and Islamic Madrasas (Qawmi – private; Alia – government-subsidized with integrated curricula). Religious education includes Islam, Hinduism, etc., with informal teaching for some non-Muslims. Refugee education for Rohingya children is limited but expanding under UNICEF. NGOs like BRAC provide non-formal education for vulnerable groups. Grading uses a GPA system with optional fourth-subject extension. Government promotes compulsory primary education, free girls’ schooling, stipends, and free textbooks, but challenges include teacher absenteeism (15.5%), poverty, rural dropout, and low learning outcomes. Literacy rose from 51.77% (2011) to 74.66% (2022). Higher education is criticized for rote learning and weak outcome orientation.

## Mexico

Mexico’s education evolved from prehispanic and colonial religious schooling to secular, state-controlled systems. Post-independence 19th-century reforms established secular public education with normal schools and Universidad Nacional de México. The 20th century saw centralization under the Ministry of Education, socialist-nationalist curricula, and indigenous assimilation efforts amid church tensions. Reforms expanded access with teacher training and free textbooks. The powerful teachers’ union (SNTE) faced corruption. Social unrest prompted reforms including mass media education and technical institutes like Conalep. Economic crises delayed progress, but 1990s policies advanced decentralization and merit pay. Enrollment surged to 32 million by 2011, with near-universal attendance and 95% literacy by 2018 despite rural and indigenous disparities. Post-1940 policies emphasized free textbooks adorned with Mexican art. The National Autonomous University of Mexico ranks globally (103rd) and leads nationally; private business schools such as IPADE and EGADE are internationally noted.

## Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia’s education rapidly evolved since the mid-20th century from mosque-linked Islamic Kuttabs to a formal system promoting literacy, Islamic values, science, and arts. Primary education is compulsory; pre-primary non-mandatory. Intermediate and secondary offer general and vocational tracks. Higher education expanded to 24 public universities, private/community colleges, and distance learning offering diplomas to doctorates. Female university enrollment surpasses males; prominent women-only institutions include Princess Nora University. Female labor participation remains low.

Special education serves severe disabilities in specialized institutions and milder cases inclusively; teacher training and resources are limited. Special education teachers receive targeted training and higher pay. Assistive technology aids learning, but parental involvement and school support vary; many facilities lack accessibility.

Private schools supplement public education, regulated and supported with funding and textbooks. Substandard private schools have been phased out. Vocational programs see higher private enrollment.

Expatriate education includes about 203 international schools offering English-medium curricula, including British and Philippine community schools. Private international schools comply with Saudi regulations such as gender segregation; community schools have some regulatory leniency.

As of 2007, adult literacy was 85%, youth 98.1%, with regional and gender disparities. Religious institutions historically contributed to literacy despite low primary enrollment. The King Abdullah Project (US$2.4bn) aims to train 400,000 teachers and foster intellectual and creative skills. The system faces criticism for rote learning, religious dominance, lack of marketable skills, intolerance, and extremist content, though recent reforms reduced anti-Semitic and anti-women language. The Tatweer program focuses on modernizing education toward critical thinking and vocational skills.

## New Zealand

New Zealand’s education is rooted in Māori traditional oral knowledge before European-style schooling began in 1815, evolving into a national secular system based on the Canterbury model. Early childhood education includes centre-based and whānau-led services under the Te Whāriki curriculum.

Primary (Years 1–8) and secondary (Years 9–13) education is free and compulsory between ages 6 and 16, typically spanning 13 year levels with flexible intermediate schools. The academic year runs late January to mid-December in four terms, with school days from about 8–9 am to 3 pm. Schools include state (85%), state-integrated (religious, ~10%), and private (~4%), with charter schools integrated by 2018. Homeschooling accounts for less than 2%. Schools vary by year-level coverage and include types such as middle, senior, correspondence, special needs, and teen parent units.

Geographic enrolment zones, abolished in 1991 and reintroduced in 2000, manage overcrowding but contribute to inflated property prices near popular schools and require proof of address to prevent falsification.

Māori language education was suppressed after 1900 but revived from the 1970s with kōhanga reo (1981), kura kaupapa Māori (1985), and Māori Language Act (1987). Māori instruction modes include Māori Medium (≥51% curriculum), Māori Language in English Medium (≤50%), or minimal/no Māori instruction. Currently, about 9.45% of Māori students are in Māori Medium (declining), 30.43% receive Māori Language in English Medium, and most receive little or no Māori language education. Efforts continue to revitalize Māori language.

Historically, manual training schools introduced practical skills but were mostly integrated into general curricula.

Tertiary education covers all post-school learning, regulated by the New Zealand Qualifications Framework, with funding from government subsidies and student fees supported by loans and allowances. Criticisms include high fees causing skill shortages and student emigration.

Primary and secondary schools receive government funding per student, adjusted for size and socio-economic factors, supplemented by voluntary donations, attendance dues (state-integrated schools), and private tuition. Teacher salaries are nationally fixed and paid by the Ministry of Education. Special needs students receive Ongoing Resource Scheme funding based on need. Student financial support includes allowances and loans; interest on student loans was abolished in 2006 for residents, easing burdens for current students but causing previous dissatisfaction.

International rankings in mathematics and science declined after 1995. The 2012 TIMSS math survey ranked New Zealand’s 9-year-olds 34th of 53 countries, with many struggling with basic arithmetic following new math teaching methods introduced in 2001. Renowned mathematician Sir Vaughan Jones criticized these approaches, advocating for stronger foundational skills. Despite this, the 2012 Pearson Learning Curve report ranked New Zealand 8th globally in education, though its accuracy is debated.

Māori and Pacific Island students exhibit marked academic achievement disparities compared to Pākehā peers, who rank much higher internationally, signaling a need for targeted improvement.

Bullying, including cyber and physical abuse, is a significant problem. New Zealand ranked second highest for bullying in a 2009 international study. Serious incidents prompted government investigations and a $60 million Positive Behaviour for Learning plan, yet effectiveness remains limited. Schools lack a unified bullying policy, resulting in inconsistent anti-bullying efforts.

## Comparative Overview

This synthesis overviews fourteen national education systems, detailing structures from early childhood through tertiary education, curricula, assessments, governance, inclusion policies, and challenges. Common themes include equity gaps (rural-urban, gender, ethnic), academic pressures, curricular reforms, vocational education stigma, resource constraints, language revitalization efforts, and modernization policies. Each system reflects unique historical, cultural, and socio-political contexts shaping priorities and reforms.
