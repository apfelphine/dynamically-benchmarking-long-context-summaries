### **Education in Russia**
Russia's education system includes general and professional education. General education spans from preschool (age 2–6) through secondary education (age 13–18), with a 9-year compulsory cycle. Professional education includes vocational training, vocational education (3 years), and higher education with degrees like bachelor's, specialist, master's, and postgraduate. The Unified State Examination (USE) is used for university admissions. Education for the disabled includes specialized schools and vocational training. The system is undergoing reforms toward the Bologna Process, with a shift from traditional five-year specialist degrees to a four-year bachelor's and two-year master's structure. Government initiatives include outreach programs and efforts to promote patriotism in schools.

### **Education in South Korea**
South Korea’s education system is rooted in Confucian values and emphasizes academic excellence. The structure includes pre-school, primary (grades 1–6), middle (grades 7–9), and high school (grades 10–12). A rigorous competitive environment exists, with students attending private cram schools (hagwons) and facing intense academic pressure. High-stakes entrance exams determine university admissions. Vocational education, including Meister Schools, is being developed to address the shortage of skilled trades. Despite societal stigma, vocational education is gaining recognition. The system has been criticized for excessive pressure, leading to high stress, mental health issues, and low student well-being.

### **Education in Pakistan**
Pakistan’s education system is structured into primary (grades 1–8), secondary (grades 9–12), and tertiary levels. Primary education includes a mix of compulsory subjects, with a strong emphasis on religious education. Secondary education is standardized through national board exams. The literacy rate has improved from 46.56% in 1998 to 60.65% in 2023, though rural and female literacy remains low. Gender disparity is significant, with women facing barriers in education and employment. The system is criticized for poor quality, outdated curricula, and lack of teacher training. The government has initiated reforms to improve access and equity, particularly in rural and marginalized communities.

### **Education in the Netherlands**
The Dutch education system is structured with compulsory education from age 5 to 18, including elementary (grades 1–8) and secondary education (VMBO, HAVO, VWO). Students are assessed annually, and those who do not meet expectations may be required to retake a year. The system emphasizes student autonomy and personal responsibility. The VWO (preparatory scientific education) and HAVO (higher general education) pathways lead to university or vocational training. The system has been criticized for early academic categorization and lack of equity in student outcomes. The Dutch system is known for its balanced approach, with a strong focus on social and personal development.

### **Education in Poland**
Poland's education system has undergone significant reforms. Primary education (grades 1–8) was extended from six to eight years, replacing the previous 6-year primary and 3-year middle school structure. Secondary education includes general (liceum) and vocational (technikum, zasadnicza szkoła zawodowa) tracks. The maturita exam is required for university admission. The education system emphasizes national identity and values, with curriculum changes in history and political education. The system has faced criticism for promoting nationalist and exclusionary narratives, particularly in history education. Vocational and technical education is being expanded to meet labor market needs.

### **Education in Japan**
Japan's education system is highly structured and emphasizes academic excellence. The curriculum includes core subjects like Japanese, mathematics, science, social studies, and foreign languages. Students are assessed through standardized exams, and the system is known for its rigorous entrance exams for university. A strong focus on rote learning and exam preparation leads to high academic performance but also significant student stress. The system includes a strong emphasis on extracurricular activities and "shadow education," such as cram schools (juku). The system has been criticized for promoting excessive academic pressure, leading to high rates of student anxiety, bullying, and suicide. Efforts have been made to promote inclusivity and reduce stress, but challenges remain.

### **Education in the Czech Republic**
The Czech education system includes a nine-year elementary school (grades 1–9), followed by secondary education leading to the maturita exam. The system emphasizes student autonomy and academic excellence. Vocational education is available through technical and professional schools. The maturita exam is required for university admission. The system has been criticized for lack of diversity in curricula and limited access to inclusive education. The government has introduced reforms to improve access and equity, particularly for students with special needs. The Czech system is known for its strong academic standards and focus on national identity.

### **Education in Mexico**
Mexico’s education system is divided into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (grades 7–9), and high school (grades 10–12). The system is publicly funded and compulsory until age 18. Education is decentralized, with significant variation between provinces. The curriculum includes a strong focus on social and civic education. The system has been criticized for low access to quality education, particularly in rural and marginalized communities. Efforts to improve access and equity are ongoing, with a focus on inclusive education and teacher training. The system is undergoing reforms to improve quality and reduce disparities.

### **Education in Canada**
Canada’s education system is decentralized, with provinces managing education. The system includes early childhood, primary, secondary, and tertiary education. Secondary education ends at grade 12, with some provinces offering Grade 11 as the final year. The system emphasizes equity, diversity, and inclusion, with a strong focus on Indigenous and multicultural education. Tertiary education includes universities and vocational institutions. The system is known for its high quality and international recognition. The system has been criticized for disparities in access and outcomes, particularly in rural and low-income areas. Efforts to improve equity and inclusion are ongoing.

### **Education in New Zealand**
New Zealand’s education system is free and compulsory from age 5 to 16. The system includes a strong emphasis on Māori language and culture, with Māori-medium education being increasingly available. The curriculum is based on the New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa (TMoA). The system has been criticized for low performance in international assessments, particularly in mathematics and science. Efforts to improve teaching quality and student outcomes are ongoing. The system is known for its strong focus on cultural inclusivity and student well-being.

### **Education in Bangladesh**
Bangladesh’s education system includes pre-primary, primary (grades 3–8), secondary (grades 9–12), and tertiary levels. The national curriculum is taught in Bengali or English, with students choosing between arts, commerce, or science streams. The system is heavily centralized, with standardized exams determining progression. The literacy rate has improved from 51.77% in 2011 to 74.66% in 2022. The system faces challenges in teacher quality, access, and equity, particularly in rural and marginalized communities. Efforts to improve access and equity are ongoing, with a focus on expanding education for girls and marginalized groups.

### **Education in Saudi Arabia**
Saudi Arabia’s education system is rooted in Islamic values, with religious education being a core component. The system includes pre-primary, primary (grades 1–6), intermediate (grades 7–9), and secondary (grades 10–12) education. Higher education includes government and private universities, with a growing emphasis on STEM and entrepreneurship. The system has been criticized for promoting rote learning and religious extremism, with limited emphasis on critical thinking and innovation. Efforts to modernize the system, including the Tatweer reform program, aim to reduce religious dominance and promote secular, vocational education. The government is also expanding access to private and international schools to improve quality and diversity.
