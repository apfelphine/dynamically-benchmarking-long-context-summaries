# Education in Russia  
Russia's education system is legally divided into general and professional education encompassing preschool, primary, basic, and secondary general levels, and professional training including vocational, higher (bachelor’s, specialist, master’s), and postgraduate education. Preschool enrollment rose from 67% in 1999 to 84% in 2005 but faces capacity challenges, especially in urban areas, with controversies around kindergarten admissions. Secondary education is compulsory for nine years, transitioning into an eleven-year program split into elementary, middle, and senior classes with a full national curriculum and Unified State Examination (USE) for university admissions, introduced in 2003 to standardize and reduce corruption. Education for disabled students is provided through specialized schools and inclusion efforts, but academic and social integration remain limited. Russia has a large higher education sector, with over 8 million tertiary students, undergoing reforms to align with the Bologna Process by adopting a bachelor-master degree structure. Postgraduate degrees follow the Soviet model with Candidate and Doctor of Sciences. The 2021 law on "outreach activity" aims to regulate science communication but faces criticism as prior censorship. In recent years, state-driven patriotic education has increased following the Ukraine invasion, with mandatory lessons criticized for militaristic content and suppression of dissent. Minority languages are taught in various schools, but recent laws restrict foreign students’ admission without Russian language proficiency.

# Education in South Korea  
South Korea’s education history is deeply rooted in Confucian values emphasizing meritocracy and social mobility through schooling, evolving from traditional village schools to a modern system influenced by Japanese occupation and post-war reforms aimed at democratization and expansion. The system comprises infant care, kindergarten, six years of elementary, three years of middle, and three years of high school, with high completion rates and strong government investment. High school offers academic and specialized tracks, including vocational and recently developed Meister schools modeled after German vocational education aimed at reducing stigma and enhancing skill training for trades. Higher education includes universities, industrial universities, and junior colleges, with fierce competition controlled mainly through the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT). English education is intensive, involving private tutoring (hagwons), with a historical focus shifting from grammar to oral proficiency, though social critiques highlight inequities and intense academic pressures. Criticism of the system includes stigma against vocational paths, health impacts from intense academic competition, and overemphasis on university entrance, despite reforms introducing credit systems and flexible course selection.

# Education in Pakistan  
Pakistan’s formal education spans pre-school, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, influenced by British system structures. Primary education covers years 1–5 with subject emphasis on sciences, languages, and religious studies. Secondary education (years 9–12) involves standardized testing (SSC and HSSC) with streams in pre-medical, engineering, commerce, and humanities. Tertiary education offers diverse degrees with accreditation councils for professional fields, though access remains low. Gender disparity is pronounced, with female enrollment and literacy rates significantly lagging, particularly in rural areas, and cultural and infrastructural barriers persist. Quality challenges include outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and insufficient training. Madrasas provide religious education and are prevalent among poorer communities. COVID-19 induced a rise in distance learning initiatives, though access remains unequal. Pakistan’s literacy rate has improved but remains below regional averages. Notable alumni include Nobel laureate Abdus Salam and others in sciences and economics. Government spending on education is low and historically inconsistent, affecting infrastructure and quality.

# Education in the Netherlands  
The Dutch education system, coordinated by the Ministry of Education, includes mandatory schooling from age 5 with flexible availability from age 4, and compulsory attendance until 18 or diploma attainment. It features publicly funded public, special (religious), and neutral schools, all regulated for quality. Elementary education spans eight grades (groups 1–8), culminating in a Cito test to advise secondary education placement. Secondary education offers tiered paths: VMBO (vocational, 4 years), HAVO (general, 5 years), and VWO (pre-university, 6 years), each with specialized curricula and profiles preparing for vocational or academic higher education. Vocational education includes MBO (middle-level applied education), followed by higher professional education (HBO) and scientific education (WO) at universities. Higher education follows the Bologna process with bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees, with strict accreditation and quality assurance. The system includes options for moving between education levels and emphasizes early tracking, which has both benefits and critiques regarding equity and student potential.

# Education in Poland  
Poland’s compulsory education starts typically at age 7 with an eight-year primary school, reintroduced in 2017 replacing the previous 6-year primary plus 3-year gimnazjum system. Secondary education includes general secondary schools (liceum) offering a four-year program culminating in Matura exam, technical schools (five years), and basic vocational schools (three years with options for further certification). The tertiary system aligns with the Bologna Process, offering three-year bachelor’s and two-year master’s programs, with some fields offering longer unified programs. University grading uses a 2.0 (fail) to 5.0 scale. Foreign languages are widely taught, with English being most common. Historically, Poland has a rich tradition in education, with Jagiellonian University established in 1364. Reforms in 1999 and 2017 reshaped structures and curricula, though recent reforms have been critiqued for nationalist emphases and diminished inclusivity. Measures to combat school violence have been implemented since 2006.

# Education in Japan  
Japan’s formal education began in the 6th century influenced by Chinese culture and Confucianism. Post-Meiji Restoration reforms in the late 19th century introduced Western education models and compulsory education. Post-WWII reforms established a system modeled on the American system: six years elementary, three years lower secondary (compulsory), three years upper secondary, and university education. The school year starts in April and is divided into terms. Primary education focuses on basic academics and moral education, with English introduced as compulsory in elementary levels since 2011. Secondary education is rigorous and competitive, with many students attending cram schools (juku) to prepare for university entrance exams. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, and vocational schools, with prestigious universities such as University of Tokyo and Kyoto University. The system has been criticized for academic pressure, limited critical thinking, bullying, and excessive reliance on entrance exams. Special education and inclusive education reforms have advanced since the 1990s.

# Education in the Czech Republic  
The Czech Republic’s education system includes a nine-grade elementary school divided into two stages, with increasing subject specialization and foreign language learning in the second stage. Secondary education includes vocational schools, professional lyceums, grammar schools (preparing for university), and specialized schools, all culminating in the maturita exam required for higher education. The tertiary system comprises universities and higher professional schools offering bachelor's, master's and doctoral degrees with various titles. Admission to secondary schools includes standardized entrance tests. Special and practical schools serve developmentally disabled students, with increasing integration into mainstream education. The school year runs from September to June, with several breaks. Private universities exist but are generally considered lower quality than public ones.

# Education in Mexico  
Mexico's basic education is structured into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (secundaria, grades 7–9), and high school (preparatoria, grades 10–12). Primary and secundaria are compulsory; preparatoria has recently become compulsory. Specialized programs include vocational tracks and telesecundaria (distance learning). Inclusive education for students with disabilities is mandated but faces challenges such as unclear roles and accessibility issues. Quality concerns include infrastructure deficits and low secondary and tertiary completion rates. Higher education follows the Bologna Process with licenciatura (bachelor’s), maestría (master’s), and doctorado (doctorate) degrees. Intercultural universities seek to increase indigenous enrollment. The National Autonomous University of Mexico is the top-ranked university. Education has a colonial history heavily influenced by the Catholic Church and liberal reforms promoting secular education.

# Education in Canada  
Canada's education system is provincially managed with federal involvement limited to military and Indigenous education. Compulsory schooling starts between ages 5 to 7 depending on province and usually concludes by grade 12 (age 17-18). The dual system includes elementary, secondary, and post-secondary education with extensive vocational colleges. University admission relies on senior secondary school grades and other factors, with competitive programs requiring high averages. The country boasts internationally recognized research universities. Private schools constitute about 6% of enrollment, often with higher academic attainment attributed to socio-economic factors. Publicly funded religious and language-based school systems exist due to constitutional protections. Recent education debates include identity-focused data collection and equity initiatives. Tuition and fees vary by province; student funding is partly through loans and allowances.

# Education in New Zealand  
New Zealand’s education system originated from Māori traditional knowledge transmission and was formalized under European influence with free, compulsory, and secular education established nationwide in 1878. Early childhood education includes kindergartens and whānau-led centers, while compulsory schooling spans from age 6 to 16 with most students attending to age 17 or beyond. The national curriculum applies to most schools, offering NCEA and other qualifications at senior levels. Schools are categorized as state, state-integrated (religious or special character), and private, with funding varying accordingly. Māori-medium education has been pursued actively since the late 20th century. Challenges include issues with equal access to popular schools, standardized assessment declines, and bullying. Tertiary education is regulated via a qualifications framework with government subsidy and student loan systems.

# Education in Bangladesh  
Bangladesh’s education is structured into pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with national curriculum offered in Bengali and English versions and streams in science, commerce, and arts at higher levels. The system includes government and private schools, madrasas (Alia and Qawmi), technical and vocational training, and international schools. Issues include gender disparity, quality concerns like outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and infrastructural deficits. Enrollment has increased notably but challenges persist in rural areas and among marginalized groups including refugees. The grading system uses GPA up to 5.0. Religious education is integrated into general curriculum with limited non-Muslim teacher availability. The government implements initiatives for inclusive education, literacy, and teacher reforms.

# Education in Saudi Arabia  
Saudi Arabia’s education has Islamic foundations, evolving from mosque-based schooling (kuttabs) to a modern system overseen by the Ministry of Education and other government bodies. Education is free and compulsory at primary (6 years) and intermediate (3 years) levels, with secondary education offering general and technical tracks. Higher education includes public universities and specialized institutions, with female enrollment surpassing males in recent years despite historical cultural restrictions. Private education complements government efforts; however, many private schools have faced regulatory scrutiny. The system is heavily religious with a focus on rote memorization, drawing criticism for failing to foster technical skills, innovation, and tolerance. Government reforms aim to modernize curricula and practices, including gifted education programs. Special education is growing but faces resource and inclusion challenges. International schools serve foreign nationals and expatriates. Literacy rates have improved but disparities exist across regions and genders.
