# Final Summary of Education Systems across Selected Countries Including Saudi Arabia

**Education in Russia**  
Russia’s system spans preschool to postgraduate education, including vocational tracks and Bologna Process degrees. Compulsory education lasts nine years, primarily taught in Russian, with options for secondary vocational schooling. Admission to universities depends on the Unified State Examination, now extended to international students. Recent policies emphasize increased state control, patriotic education, and militarization.

**Education in South Korea**  
South Korea employs a 6-4-3-4 system influenced by Confucian meritocracy and modern reforms. Secondary education offers general, specialized, and vocational tracks with a national ethos favoring employment before college despite social stigma. University entrance relies on the competitive CSAT exam centering on elite “SKY” universities. Private tutoring supports English learning. Challenges include exam pressure, authoritarian cultures, elitism, and social stigma.

**Education in Pakistan**  
Pakistan’s education features diverse academic and vocational streams in multiple languages. Primary attendance is about 66%, with significant gender, geographic, and wealth disparities. Secondary credentials include SSC and HSSC. Vocational education seeks to improve employability but faces infrastructure and teacher training gaps. Spending (~2% GDP) is below targets. Madrasas prioritize Quranic instruction shaped by Islamization, limiting science education. COVID-19 pushed distance learning despite connectivity challenges. Some universities rank regionally.

**Education in the Netherlands**  
In the Netherlands, education is free from ages 5–18, with early tracking via the Cito exam into vocational (VMBO), higher general (HAVO), or pre-university (VWO) paths, raising equity concerns and mirroring declining PISA scores. Secondary education leads to vocational schools, higher professional education (HBO), or research universities (WO), all accredited by NVAO. Student support and regionally varying holidays are notable.

**Education in Poland**  
Poland mandates eight years of primary education, followed by general (liceum), technical (technikum), or vocational tracks, including special education. English is the primary foreign language. Post-1999 reforms emphasized Eurocentric curricula and zero tolerance for school violence. Higher education follows Bologna guidelines with bachelor’s to doctoral degrees and teacher training.

**Education in Japan**  
Japan’s education, rooted in Confucian and Buddhist traditions and Meiji-era reforms, mandates nine years emphasizing core subjects, moral education, and English from 2011. Upper secondary offers academic, technical, and vocational tracks with significant private school attendance. Special needs education emphasizes inclusion. Cram schools supplement learning but raise concerns about stress and mental health.

**Education in the Czech Republic**  
Elementary education lasts nine grades, transitioning from teacher-led to specialized subjects. Secondary education requires entrance exams to vocational, professional, or grammar schools, culminating in the Maturita exam for university admission. Inclusive education integrates disabled and lower-achieving students. Higher education includes professional schools and universities awarding bachelor’s to doctoral degrees. Public tuition-free Czech-language programs coexist with private fee-based institutions.

**Education in Mexico**  
Mexico’s system spans nursery to preparatoria and higher degrees. Indigenous education was oral and specialized pre-colonially; colonial schooling was religious and elite-focused. Post-independence reforms established secular, free, compulsory primary education. Later reforms emphasized standards, teacher training, free textbooks, and unions amid challenges from union conflicts and decentralization. Enrollment reached 97.5% for ages 6–14 by 2020 with 95% literacy by 2018. Challenges remain in quality, equity, and infrastructure.

**Education in Canada**  
Canada’s provincially governed system mandates schooling ages 5–7 to 16–18, with optional kindergarten at age 5. Secondary includes International Baccalaureate and Advanced Placement programs; public funding may extend to ages 19–21. Public schools are administered by elected boards; Alberta allows independent charter schools. Vocational education is delivered by over 200 colleges and technical schools. Private schools serve about 6% of grade 10 students with generally higher outcomes. Private universities, some religiously affiliated, exist amid debates on funding and religious freedom. Quebec requires college diplomas (CEGEP) before university admission.

**Education in New Zealand**  
New Zealand blends Māori and European traditions. Formal schooling began in 1815; free, compulsory, secular education has been established since 1878. Early childhood education includes centre-based and whānau-led care. Primary covers Years 1–8; secondary Years 9–13; schooling is free ages 5–19 and compulsory ages 6–16. Schools include state (~85%), state-integrated (~10%, chiefly religious), and private (~4%). Enrollment prioritizes local residents, which impacts low socioeconomic groups and housing prices. Māori-language education has revived and outperforms mainstream; government funding supports schools per student, with extra funds for smaller or low-decile schools. Teachers receive centralized salaries. Challenges include high tertiary fees, skills shortages, achievement gaps, and bullying.

**Education in Bangladesh**  
Bangladesh’s education covers pre-primary to tertiary without middle school. Multiple boards (general, Qawmi, Alia Islamic) govern diverse school types. Technical and vocational education ranges from diplomas to four-year courses with higher degree pathways. Madrasah education splits into government-funded Alia and private Qawmi emphasizing Islamic studies. Non-formal and refugee education operate widely. The 0–5 GPA grading scale has a 33% pass threshold. Government initiatives promote compulsory primary education, free girls’ schooling through class 10, stipends, and free textbooks. Challenges include teacher absenteeism, poverty-related dropout rates, rote learning, and limited higher education capacity. Literacy rose from 51.8% (2011) to 74.7% (2022), with gender parity largely achieved.

**Education in Saudi Arabia**  
Rooted in Islamic tradition with formalization from 1926, Saudi Arabia’s education system is overseen chiefly by the Ministry of Education and the Supreme Committee for Educational Policy. Public spending reached 5.1% of GDP (2006). The 10-Year Plan (1425–1435 Hijri) focuses on expanding kindergarten, technical education, loyalty promotion, teacher quality, ICT use, and illiteracy eradication. Kindergarten enrollment remains low (~10.8% in 2022). Primary education is compulsory (ages 6–12), covering Arabic, Islamic studies, math, science, art, and English since 2021. Intermediate and secondary stages last three years each, offering general and technical tracks with high enrollment rates (~96% and 92%).

Higher education started with King Saud University (1957); 24 government universities offer four- to six-year programs mainly in humanities, social sciences, medicine, and engineering. Enrollment exceeded 636,000 in 2006 with women comprising 60% of university students though only 14.4% of the workforce, mostly in education and public sectors. Female legal graduates appeared circa 2008; the first licensed female lawyers in 2013. Princess Nora University is the world’s largest women-only university. Extensive scholarships support Saudis and women obtaining doctorates abroad.

Gifted education operates through 91 government centers targeting at least 1% of students per district, emphasizing STEM enrichment and innovation aligned with Saudi Vision 2030. Special education serves students with various disabilities, offering mainstreaming for mild cases and specialized institutions for severe disabilities, though teacher training and resource limitations challenge inclusion. Special education teachers earn higher salaries and number over 20 programs. Transition services involve business partnerships but suffer from limited parental involvement and coordination. Large, older schools face facility and climate difficulties.

Private education complements public schooling under government regulation, including free textbooks, financial aid, and director appointments. Many private schools historically operated in villas; government measures from 2004–2018 tried to enforce proper infrastructure, facing some resistance.

International education includes over 200 schools with foreign curricula (British, American, Filipino), mainly for expatriate children. Schools often follow Saudi rules requiring gender segregation, though community-based schools have lighter regulation.

Literacy rates (2007) were 85% adult overall with 98.1% youth literacy, though adult male literacy (89.1%) exceeds female (79.4%). Illiteracy varies regionally, highest in Jizan and lowest in Riyadh. Religious institutions contribute auxiliary literacy education.

The King Abdullah Project invested US$2.4 billion to develop education, training over 400,000 teachers, and promote extracurricular activities.

Saudi education faces criticism for rote learning, religious instruction fostering intolerance, and lacking skills for innovation and entrepreneurship, though textbook reforms reduce anti-Semitic and anti-women content. The Tatweer reform program, backed by US$2 billion, seeks to modernize curricula by encouraging critical thinking and vocational training. Local calls emphasize enhancing English proficiency and aligning skills with economic and global demands.

---

**Overview**  
These countries demonstrate varied education systems shaped by culture, history, language, and socioeconomics, with common structures including compulsory schooling phases, academic and vocational pathways, and multiple governance models. Persistent challenges involve equity, quality, infrastructure, and balancing tradition with modernization. Noteworthy advances include New Zealand’s Māori language education, South Korea’s meritocratic rigor, Mexico’s literacy gains, Canada’s pluralistic public/private mix, Bangladesh’s integrated multi-board framework, and Saudi Arabia’s rapid modernization expanding access for women, gifted, and disabled learners amidst ongoing reform efforts and evolving curricula.
