# **Master Summary: Education Systems in Russia, South Korea, the Netherlands, Japan, the Czech Republic, Mexico, Canada, New Zealand, Bangladesh, and Saudi Arabia (2024–2025)**

## **The Netherlands**  
The Netherlands operates a comprehensive, equitable, and student-centered education system under the Ministry of Education, featuring three main secondary pathways: **VMBO** (pre-vocational, 4 years), **HAVO** (5 years, leading to higher professional education), and **VWO** (6 years, leading to research universities). After secondary education, students may pursue **MBO** (vocational training, levels 1–4), **HBO** (universities of applied sciences), or **WO** (research universities). Since 2002, the system aligns with the Bologna Process, using the ECTS credit system (1 credit = 28 hours), with bachelor’s programs requiring 180 credits, master’s ranging from 60 to 180, and PhDs requiring original research and public defense. The **NVAO** accredits programs to ensure quality and funding eligibility. The system emphasizes inclusivity, flexibility, and vocational alignment, with strong support for students with special needs through the Resource Room System and inclusive education policies. However, early tracking via the Cito test misplaces up to 30% of gifted students in lower tracks, leading to reduced achievement and higher dropout rates. Parental income and involvement also influence outcomes, and PISA data show declining performance in reading, math, and science. Despite these challenges, the Dutch model stands in contrast to rigid or ideologically constrained systems, offering strong pathways for both academic and practical advancement.

## **South Korea**  
Rooted in Confucian values, South Korea’s education system emphasizes meritocracy, academic excellence, and social mobility through a 6-4-3-4 structure. With over 98% secondary completion and 93% adult literacy, it has reduced political indoctrination and introduced reforms such as banning mobile phones in class, abolishing corporal punishment (2011), and launching a credit-based system (2025) allowing student customization. High schools now offer general, science, foreign language, international, and art tracks, with highly competitive entrance exams. Despite strong academic performance, systemic issues persist: intense competition, over-reliance on university entrance exams, and high parental investment in private *hagwons* and overseas English programs lead to student stress, poor mental health, and neglect of physical education. A major shift is underway toward a competency-based model, with vocational education gaining traction through *Meister Schools*—apprenticeship-style programs in trades like auto mechanics and welding. Currently serving 20% of high school students, especially in rural areas, these programs offer strong job placement and higher earning potential, reflecting an “Employment First, College Later” philosophy to reduce youth unemployment. Vocational graduates may bypass the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), signaling growing acceptance of non-academic pathways.

## **Russia**  
Russia’s education system features a dual general and professional track. General education begins at preschool and includes nine-year compulsory schooling (eleven years in some regions), with rising enrollment despite limited access in large cities due to local regulations. Secondary education follows a standardized national curriculum with nationally recognized certificates and teacher-to-student ratios comparable to developed nations. The professional pathway includes vocational training (3–4 years), bachelor’s degrees (4 years), specialist programs (5–6 years for medicine/engineering), master’s (2 years), and postgraduate studies (2–4 years). University admissions are standardized via the **Unified State Examination (USE)**, reducing regional disparities, though the grading system remains controversial. The higher education system is transitioning toward the Bologna Process with a four-year bachelor’s and two-year master’s structure, though critics argue the reform is superficial with minimal curriculum changes and limited student mobility. Specialist diplomas are still considered equivalent to Western MSc/MA degrees, while the new master’s remains costly and underrecognized by employers. Postgraduate degrees (Candidate of Sciences and Doctor of Sciences) require original research and peer review, with the latter reserved for major scientific contributions. A 2021 law formalized "science outreach" initiatives, raising concerns about censorship. "Patriotic education" including mandatory "Important Conversations" lessons—criticized for their militaristic tone—is now widely enforced. Language education supports major ethnic languages (e.g., Tatar, Bashkir), but many indigenous languages (e.g., Yukaghir, Shor) lack formal recognition. In December 2024, a law required foreign citizens to pass a Russian language exam to enroll in schools, reinforcing linguistic and cultural integration.

## **Japan**  
Japan’s education system, evolved from Meiji-era reforms to a decentralized democratic model, includes six years of elementary school, three of junior high, and three of senior high (ages 6–18). Nearly 99% of students attend upper secondary education, which is not compulsory but widely pursued. Public schools are tuition-free; private schools account for about 55% of institutions. Core subjects include Japanese, math, science, social studies, physical education, and English (mandatory from elementary). Textbooks are centrally certified, and students are assessed using a traditional grading system (*shū to fuka*). A significant challenge is the intense pressure from private cram schools (*juku*), with over 86% of university-bound students participating. This has led to severe well-being issues, including bullying (over 612,000 cases in 2019), psychological harm, and suicide (10 of 317 suicides involved bullying). Since the 1990s, reforms such as the "Resource Room System" and full inclusive education (introduced in 2006) have integrated students with disabilities into mainstream classrooms through specialized support. The Basic Act on Education and the CRPD ensure inclusive access and vocational training for students with disabilities.

## **Czech Republic**  
The Czech education system includes a nine-grade elementary stage (grades 1–9), with students entering eight-year or six-year academies. Secondary education requires passing mandatory entrance exams in math, English, or Czech, with 12.3% of first-time test-takers failing in 2025. Vocational training (2–3 years) offers certificates or combined academic qualifications, while grammar schools prepare students for university. The **maturita** exam is required for university admission and includes mandatory subjects (Czech language and literature, and math or a foreign language), with other subjects determined by schools. Tertiary education is led by historic institutions such as Charles University (founded 1348) and Palacký University. Public universities offer free Czech-language study to first-time students under 26, while international students may study in English or Czech. Private universities charge €2,000–10,000 annually. Degrees range from 2 to 6 years: Bachelor’s (3 years, Bc.), Master’s (e.g., Mgr., Ing.), and PhD (e.g., PhDr.). Public universities are generally considered higher quality. The school year runs from September to June, with 62+ days of breaks. Inclusive education is growing, supported by specialized resources and policies, though teacher training and accessibility remain challenges.

## **Mexico**  
Mexico’s education system evolved from pre-Columbian and colonial roots to a modern, secular, state-controlled public system. Indigenous institutions like the *calmecac* and *cuicacalli* existed before Spanish colonization. Post-independence reforms under Juárez and Vasconcelos promoted national unity through Spanish-language instruction and a *mestizo* identity. The 1920s saw centralization, with expansions under Díaz and Cárdenas emphasizing science and worker empowerment. The current structure includes primary (grades 1–9), junior high (*secundaria*, grades 7–9), and high school (*preparatoria* or *bachillerato*, grades 10–12), with *preparatoria* now compulsory. Bilingual instruction is available, and *secundaria* offers vocational tracks such as *técnica* and *telesecundaria*. *Preparatoria* provides a three-year general education for university, while *bachillerato* prepares students for skilled employment. Inclusive education is legally mandated since 1993, with students with special needs integrated into regular classrooms via USAER support, though teacher training and accessibility remain challenges. Despite progress, disparities persist: only 45% complete secondary education and 25% reach higher education. Weaknesses in math, science, and reading are highlighted by OECD reports. A 2013 reform transferred control from the SNTE to government bodies, sparking ongoing tensions. Higher education follows a U.S.-style Bologna-aligned structure: 4-year *Licenciatura* (bachelor’s), 2-year *Maestría* (master’s), and 3-year *Doctorado* (PhD). Indigenous enrollment remains low (1–3%), prompting the creation of Intercultural Universities in 2004. Mexico hosts 151 international schools offering English-medium curricula.

## **Canada**  
Canada’s education system is decentralized, with provinces managing curriculum, funding, and school operations. Compulsory education began between 1871 and 1943, finalized in Quebec and Newfoundland in 1943. The standard K–12 structure begins at age 4–5 and ends at Grade 12 (except Quebec, where it ends at Grade 11). Full-day kindergarten is available in most provinces. Schools offer IB or AP programs, which can earn university credits. Students with suspensions may access drop-in programs, night school, or online classes. Tertiary education includes universities (offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees) and vocational institutions (offering diplomas, certificates, and associate degrees). Canada invests approximately 5.2% of GDP in education, with strong emphasis on equity, inclusion, and cultural programs such as Indigenous studies and antiracism. Religious and language-based schools are constitutionally protected (e.g., Ontario’s Catholic system is fully funded). Private schools and universities (e.g., Trinity Western, Quest) raise debates over access, cost, and religious exemptions. Canada ranks globally for university quality and research, attracting many international students. Provincial variations exist—e.g., British Columbia uses Middle Schools (K–7 or 6–8), Ontario includes early childhood programs like Maternelle and Jardin, and Quebec integrates secondary and post-secondary education—resulting in regional diversity despite national emphasis on local control.

## **New Zealand**  
New Zealand features a nationally structured system with standardized curricula: the New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) for English-medium education and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa for Māori-medium education. Compulsory schooling is free and mandatory from age 6 to 16, with primary education covering Years 1–8 and secondary education from Years 9–13. Early childhood education includes both teacher-led (e.g., Kindergarten) and parent-led (e.g., Kohanga Reo, Playcentre) models, regulated by the Ministry of Education and guided by Te Whāriki. State schools are free and secular, while state-integrated schools maintain cultural or religious identity with attendance fees, and private schools charge tuition. Charter schools were phased out in 2018. Māori-language education, rooted in pre-European wānanga traditions, has seen revival through kōhanga reo and kura kaupapa Māori schools, which offer immersive instruction and show higher NCEA Level 3 attainment—though enrolment has declined. Despite strong global rankings in some assessments, New Zealand’s performance in TIMSS—especially in math and numeracy—has declined, raising concerns about a shift toward alternative problem-solving over foundational skills. Persistent achievement gaps exist, with Māori and Pacific Island students ranking 34th and 44th globally in PISA, respectively. Bullying remains a serious issue, with New Zealand reporting the second-highest rates worldwide; although initiatives like the $60 million *Positive Behaviour for Learning* program exist, their impact has been limited. Teacher salaries are centrally managed, and schools receive per-student funding adjusted for socio-economic factors, though inflation and high tertiary costs threaten access and contribute to teacher shortages.

## **Bangladesh**  
Bangladesh operates a multi-tiered, government-managed education system. Primary education is overseen by the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education (MOPME) through the Directorate of Primary Education (DPE), which manages teacher recruitment, textbook distribution (over 34 million as of 2022), and school operations. The PEC (5th grade) and JSC (8th grade) exams are administered by DPE, though these will be discontinued from 2023. Secondary education is led by ten regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education (BISEs), which conduct the SSC (10th grade) and HSC (12th grade) exams. Government schools are managed by headmasters under zone supervision, while non-government schools are governed by School Management Committees (SMCs) or Governing Bodies (GBs). The system includes a dual madrasah structure: **Qawmi** (privately funded, 1.9%–2.2% enrollment) and **Alia** (government-subsidized, 8.4%–19% enrollment), with Alia schools offering Arabic alongside general education. Students can pursue religious degrees such as Alim, Fazil, and Kamil after qualifying exams. Religious education is part of the national curriculum for Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Christianity, though many non-Muslim students study independently. Tertiary education is regulated by the University Grants Commission (UGC), overseeing 43 public, 103 private, 2 international, 31 specialized colleges, and 2 special universities. Public universities are government-subsidized and admit students via competitive exams; private institutions allow more foreign students and teachers and offer master’s degrees (but not MPhil/PhD). Technical and vocational education is led by the Directorate of Technical Education (DTE) and BTEB, offering diplomas (1 month to 4 years) that lead to bachelor’s degrees through Engineering & Technology Universities. Alternative education includes international schools using curricula such as Cambridge, Edexcel, or IB, where students take IGCSEs or A-Levels accepted by local universities. Non-formal education, largely led by NGOs like BRAC, serves dropouts but rarely completes full primary cycles. Refugee education—especially for Rohingya children—remains a major challenge due to limited access and reliance on UNICEF-run centers. The system suffers from outdated curricula emphasizing rote learning, lack of industry-aligned training, absence of Outcome-Based Education (OBE), and limited access for marginalized groups. Despite progress in free primary education, girl’s education stipends, and free curriculum books, structural reforms and greater inclusivity are essential. Grading is based on a 33% pass mark, with a GPA of 5.0 (A+) considered ideal; a "Golden A+" refers to A+ in all subjects, with an optional fourth subject adding grade points if it exceeds 2.0. The system allocates between 0.94% and 2.2% of GDP to public education, reflecting limited financial commitment. Persistent challenges include poor teacher retention (15.5% absenteeism), gender disparities in rural areas, low school attendance, high dropout and repetition rates, and rising education costs.

## **Saudi Arabia**  
Saudi Arabia operates a free, Islamic-rooted education system supported by strong government investment—5.1% of GDP in 2006—focused on quality, modernization, and access. The curriculum includes Arabic, Islamic studies (averaging 9 periods per week), math, science, and art, with English introduced from Grade 1. Gross enrollment rates are high: 95.9% at the primary and 91.8% at the secondary levels (2007). Since 2021, curriculum reforms have merged Islamic subjects and expanded science education. Pre-primary enrollment stands at 3.66 million (10.8% gross enrollment rate in 2022). Higher education has grown substantially since 1957, with 24 government universities and a growing network of private and distance learning institutions. Programs last four years in humanities and social sciences, and five to six years in medicine, engineering, and pharmacy. Women now constitute 60% of university students, surpassing men in undergraduate enrollment by 2015, supported by scholarships and institutions like Princess Nora bint Abdul Rahman University (PNU). A national gifted education program, including the Summer Enrichment Program (SEP), targets at least 1% of students per district, promoting STEM and creativity—though access remains uneven. The system includes a "differentiated paradigm" for gifted students, integrating them into broader social programs, and offers inclusive education in cities like Riyadh, with 30 elementary, 18 middle, and 11 high schools supporting students with mild disabilities. Special education teachers earn 30% more and are in high demand, with over 20 university programs offering training. Assistive technology is widely used and effective, and school-business partnerships support transition planning. However, general education teachers often lack training in autism support, face heavy workloads, and operate in negative school climates. Private education is prominent, especially in technical and vocational fields, where private enrollment reaches up to 70% at intermediate and secondary levels. While government oversight includes textbook support, financial aid, and paid directors, the closure of 113 private schools in rented villas (2004–2018)—reversed due to public backlash—highlights tensions over safety, quality, and governance. International schools (203 as of 2015) serve expatriate communities, with community-based schools (non-gender-segregated, no Saudi sponsorship) and private international schools (gender-segregated, regulated) coexisting under varying oversight. Despite progress, the system faces criticism for overemphasizing religious instruction—particularly Quranic memorization and doctrinal learning—which promotes rote learning, intolerance, and hinders innovation and entrepreneurship. While literacy is relatively high, especially among youth, regional disparities and gender gaps persist. Reform initiatives such as the King Abdullah Project and Tatweer aim to modernize education by training over 400,000 teachers, promoting extracurricular activities, and shifting toward problem-solving and vocational training. Textbook revisions have reduced anti-Semitic and anti-women content, but concerns remain about alignment with global economic demands and potential promotion of extremist ideologies.

### **Comparative Observations**

- **Strengths**: The Netherlands, South Korea, and Canada excel in equity, student well-being, and innovation in vocational and inclusive education. Japan and the Czech Republic show strong academic foundations but face challenges in student mental health and access.  
- **Challenges**: Russia and Bangladesh face ideological constraints, linguistic control, underfunded systems, and structural inequities. Mexico and Japan struggle with inequality and student well-being. Saudi Arabia’s overemphasis on religious instruction and regional disparities remain significant concerns.  
- **Emerging Trends**: A global shift toward competency-based learning, vocational integration, and student-centered models is evident in South Korea, the Netherlands, Mexico, and Saudi Arabia.  
- **Policy Note**: Effective reforms—such as inclusive education, mental health support, curriculum modernization, and teacher training—are critical across all systems to ensure sustainability, equity, and social inclusion.
