## Comparative Overview of Education Systems: Global Perspectives

### National System Profiles & Structure
Global education systems exhibit vast diversity in structure, funding, and cultural priorities. Key examples include:
*   **South Korea:** Its system, influenced by Confucianism, spans K-12 through higher education. It is characterized by a strong private investment culture driven by English language proficiency goals and high academic competition.
*   **Japan:** Relies heavily on intensive private tutoring ($\textit{juku}$) for entrance exams, maintaining a state-influenced curriculum while incorporating inclusive education since 2006.
*   **Russia:** Operates with general and vocational tracks, mandating nine years of secondary schooling (transitioning to eleven), and uses the Unified State Examination (USE) since 2009, with new laws restricting non-Russian speakers.
*   **New Zealand:** Runs a free, nationally curriculum-driven system (compulsory 6-16) that supports Māori language but faces ongoing equity challenges for Māori and Pacific Island students, alongside concerns about math proficiency.
*   **Saudi Arabia:** A state-guided system integrating Islamic values, which introduced bilingual instruction in 2021 and has achieved 60% female university enrollment.
*   **Bangladesh:** Suffers from low public investment (0.94%–2.2% of GDP), poor teacher morale, and gender inequality, despite aiming for free tertiary education.
*   **Canada:** Features a decentralized structure funded by roughly 5.2% of GDP, granting provinces significant autonomy.
*   **Czech Republic:** Provides free public education for Czech speakers under 26, culminating in the *Maturita*.
*   **Poland:** Requires eight years of primary education before students choose secondary tracks (*Matura*) or vocational paths, adhering to the Bologna scheme.
*   **Mexico:** Requires three compulsory education levels (primary through *preparatoria*), offering general or vocational tracks and supporting inclusion since 1993.
*   **Pakistan:** Has an English-influenced system with low primary completion (67.5%) and variable tertiary durations.
*   **Netherlands:** Implements a multi-tiered system beginning compulsory education at age five, featuring selective secondary education (12–18), including **VMBO** for extra aid recipients.

### Higher Education Structures and Reforms
Higher education pathways vary globally. In the **Netherlands**, post-secondary options are segmented into **MBO** (applied vocational), **HBO** (higher professional), and **WO** (research universities), all compliant with the Bologna process. **Russia** aligns with Bologna (since 2007) with a standard four-year Bachelor's and two-year Master's, though criticized for depth. **South Korea** mirrors the US model, utilizing intensely competitive institutions; university entry is heavily weighted toward the **College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT)** (60%) and transcripts (40%), though vocational alternatives exist but carry social stigma.

### Historical Context, Challenges, and Equity
Systemic pressures are prominent across nations. Challenges include Mexico's low secondary completion (45%) and Pakistan's funding shortages. While Japan contends with extreme academic stress from private tutoring, the Netherlands faces scrutiny over early segregation. Furthermore, in **South Korea**, the hyper-competitive environment, fueled by private tutoring ($\textit{hagwons}$), leads to low student happiness, high stress, and poor job-major alignment, alongside the devaluing of vocational education. Overall, inclusion efforts remain inconsistent, seen in New Zealand's struggles with equity and Russia's inadequate mental health support.
