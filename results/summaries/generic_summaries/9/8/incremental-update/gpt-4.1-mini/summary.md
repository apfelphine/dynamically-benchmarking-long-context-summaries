# Summary: Education Systems in Bangladesh, Canada, Saudi Arabia, the Czech Republic, New Zealand, Poland, Mexico, Pakistan, Japan, Russia, the Netherlands, and South Korea

## Education in Bangladesh  
Bangladesh features a multi-stream system with general, technical, vocational, religious (madrasah), and international education overseen by various authorities. Primary and secondary education culminate in SSC and HSC exams, with strong female teacher presence at the primary level. Higher education, regulated by the University Grants Commission, includes public and an expanding private sector; technical certificates enable academic progression. The bilingual national curriculum covers core subjects and specialization streams at higher-secondary levels. Madrasahs encompass private Qawmi and government-supported Alia types. NGOs offer non-formal education for marginalized groups, including Rohingya refugees. Persistent challenges include teacher absenteeism (~15.5%), rural-urban disparities, dropout rates, rote learning, limited university capacity, and low public expenditure (~0.94%–2.2% GDP). Literacy improved from ~52% (2011) to ~75% (2022), achieving gender parity in basic education despite rural gaps.

## Education in Canada  
Canada's provincially governed system varies in grade structures and bilingual (English/French) instruction across secular, Catholic, independent, and charter schools (with Alberta restricting charters to non-religious). Education covers early childhood, elementary, intermediate, secondary, and tertiary levels; Quebec’s CEGEP bridges secondary and tertiary education. Kindergarten may last up to two years; secondary offers university-preparatory tracks like IB and AP. Post-secondary institutions confer certificates, diplomas, apprenticeships, and degrees. Spending hovers around 5.2% GDP (2020). Constitutional protections uphold religious and language rights, with curricula increasingly integrating bilingual, Indigenous, and anti-racism content. Alberta emphasizes rigorous assessments amid quality concerns.

## Education in Saudi Arabia  
Saudi Arabia’s education evolved from mosque-based Quranic teaching, expanded girls’ schooling post-1964, blending Islamic values with modernization. The Ministry of Education and Technical and Vocational Training Corporation oversee education, with spending over 5% GDP (2006). Priorities include teacher quality, special needs, girls' technical education, ICT, and adult learning. Kindergarten enrollment is ~11% (2022); compulsory education covers primary, intermediate, and secondary (general, specialized, technical) tracks. Female enrollment grows, women constitute 60% of university students in gender-segregated settings. STEM-focused gifted education progresses under Vision 2030. Special education uses mixed segregated-mainstream models with resource constraints. Private/international schools primarily serve expatriates. Literacy rates reached ~85% adult and 98% youth (2007). Critiques target rote teaching and sectarian bias.

## Education in the Czech Republic  
Elementary education spans nine years in two stages covering languages, sciences, arts, and physical education. Secondary education offers vocational, professional, lyceum, and grammar schools preparing students for the maturita exam, essential for university admission. Special education increasingly integrates support within mainstream schools. Higher professional schools confer "DiS." titles; universities offer bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. The academic year runs September to June in two semesters.

## Education in New Zealand  
Originating from Māori oral traditions and European models, New Zealand established a free, secular, compulsory system in 1878. Early childhood education blends centre-based and whānau-led programs guided by *Te Whāriki*. Schooling is free from age 5 and compulsory 6–16, allowing home-schooling. The 13-year sequence covers primary, intermediate, and secondary levels. Curricula comprise the New Zealand Curriculum (English-medium) and *Te Marautanga o Aotearoa* (Māori-medium) across eight learning areas. Qualifications begin at Year 11 with NCEA, IB, and Cambridge options. School types include state, state-integrated, and private; charter schools have been reintegrated. Māori-medium education revitalization improved outcomes, though international assessments reveal declines in math and science since 1995 and underperformance among Māori and Pacific students. Bullying is widespread, prompting government actions.

## Education in Poland  
Compulsory schooling starts at age 7 with eight years of primary education after a 2017 reform removing the gimnazjum. Secondary education divides into general (liceum) and vocational/technical paths preparing for the Matura exam or employment. Higher education follows the Bologna process with bachelor’s, master’s, and some long-cycle degrees. Universities use a 2.0–5.0 grading scale with exam retake options. Foreign language instruction is widespread, including two compulsory languages at lower secondary. Poland’s education traces to the 12th century with historic institutions like Jagiellonian University (1364). Recent reforms enhanced OECD rankings but introduced nationally focused, socially conservative content. The 2006 discipline policy enforces strict zero tolerance protecting teachers.

## Education in Mexico  
Mexico’s system spans preschool, bilingual primary, secondary (general and vocational including telesecundaria distance learning), and high school (Preparatoria/Bachillerato, grades 10–12) with general, vocational, and IB tracks. Inclusive policies integrate special needs students amid challenges in role clarity, accessibility, and teacher preparation. Attendance declines from ~90% primary to 62% secondary and 25% tertiary. Rural areas often lack infrastructure. Mexico scores below OECD averages in math, science, and reading. 2013 reforms curtailed teacher union power amid tensions. Higher education offers Licenciatura, Maestría, and Doctorado degrees. Indigenous access rises via Intercultural Universities. By 2015, 151 international schools provide English-medium curricula. The SEP textbook program emphasizes Mexican art and culture. The National Autonomous University ranks 103rd globally; private and business schools have reputations. Historically, education evolved from elite Aztec systems through secular and republican reforms expanding access and reshaping gender and ethnic roles.

## Education in Pakistan  
Pakistan’s education mirrors the English system, spanning pre-school to tertiary levels. Primary completion and attendance hover around 67%. Curricula cover arts, sciences, languages, and social studies; secondary ends with SSC and HSSC certificates; British and North American systems also operate. Technical education awards DAE diplomas. University graduation was 6% (2007), targeting 15% by 2020. Teacher education faces shortages and theoretical focus. Non-formal education includes vocational centers and madrasas (12,000–40,000), mostly Islamic with some secular content. Gender disparities arise from cultural and economic barriers; literacy and enrollment favor males, though enrolled females perform well. Quality issues include outdated curricula and infrastructure shortages. COVID-19 accelerated distance learning aiding remote and female access. Literacy is 60.65%, second lowest in South Asia, linked to poverty and feudalism. Spending is ~2% GDP. Madrasas resist evolution content; minority groups lag educationally.

## Education in Japan  
Japan, influenced by Chinese and Confucian traditions, mandates nine years of compulsory coeducational schooling followed by upper secondary and higher education. Elementary and lower secondary cover broad subjects, including English. Upper secondary offers academic, technical, and vocational tracks. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, colleges of technology, and training colleges, with competitive admissions feeding cram schools. Special needs education integrates disabled students. Programs emphasize equal rights and diversity. Bullying and psychological stress are concerns. Approximately 30–40 international schools operate. The 2007 Basic Act on Education and 2014 disability rights ratification guarantee equal opportunity.

## Education in Russia  
Russia provides preschool to postgraduate education with compulsory nine-year basic education, many completing 11 years. The Unified State Examination standardizes university admissions but faces criticism. Vocational diplomas are equivalent to secondary education. Tertiary qualifications exceed OECD averages; bachelor’s, master’s, and specialist diplomas coexist; postgraduate degrees include Soviet-era Candidate and Doctor of Sciences. Postgraduate education is mostly fee-based. Special support exists for physical disabilities; integration for mental disabilities remains limited. The 2021 Science Outreach Law regulates knowledge dissemination amid rights concerns. “Patriotic education” intensifies nationalistic curricula and monitoring. From 2024, foreign students must pass Russian language exams for school admission.

### Education in Languages of Russia  
About 24.8 million speak minority languages; native-language education is available in thousands of schools for groups like Tatars, Bashkirs, Chuvash, and Avars, supporting cultural preservation.

## Education in the Netherlands  
Compulsory education runs from 5 to 16 years across public, special (religious), and general-special schools with equal funding. Elementary education (ages 4–12) culminates in the Cito test guiding secondary placement. Concerns include early tracking and socioeconomic biases. Secondary tracks include VMBO (vocational), HAVO (applied sciences prep), VWO (research university prep), plus adult education (VAVO). Vocational MBO programs lead to trades or further studies. HBO offers professional degrees; WO offers research degrees (bachelor’s, master’s, PhDs) following the Bologna Process and ECTS credits. Schools provide health and social care support teams. The system evolved over two centuries, with reforms addressing equality and immigrant needs.

## Education in South Korea  
South Korea's education system reflects Confucian meritocratic traditions shaped by historical village/private schools and colonial repression, evolving into a modern 6-4-3-4 structure: infant/nursery, kindergarten, elementary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), high school (10–12), and tertiary education. Primary education is largely teacher-led, with specialists for physical education and languages; mobile phones were banned in 2023, and corporal punishment prohibited since 2011. Secondary education divides into general, specialty (science, language, art), vocational, and gifted tracks, achieving near-universal completion (~98%), though not compulsory. A forthcoming 2025 credit system aims at curricular flexibility. Vocational education, stigmatized and low-attended (~5%), includes vocational high schools, Meister Schools (industry-linked), industrial universities, and junior/community colleges, with government efforts to improve quality and labor market readiness.

Higher education is predominantly private (87.3%), with about 69% of high school graduates matriculating. University admission heavily depends on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) (60%) and high school records (40%), supplemented by extracurricular and recommendation evaluations. Bachelor’s degrees typically require 130–140 credits over four years; professional degrees (medicine, law, dentistry) extend up to six years. Master’s degrees require a bachelor’s with GPA ≥3.0 plus coursework, thesis, exams, and interviews, usually completed in two years. Doctoral degrees require a master’s, research, recommendations, 60 credits, comprehensive exams, language proficiency, and dissertation defense over up to four years. The system’s intense academic competition fosters extensive preparatory schooling and social pressures. Leading universities include Seoul National, Korea, and Yonsei ("SKY").

### English Education in South Korea  
With roots in the Joseon Dynasty, modern English education shifted in 1994 from grammar to communicative skills, emphasizing oral proficiency. Due to large public school classes, many families invest heavily in private English hagwons, English-only kindergartens, overseas education, and parental relocation ("goose dads") to English-speaking countries. South Korea significantly improved its English proficiency rankings by 2020.

### Controversies and Challenges  
South Korea faces social stigma against vocational education despite its economic contributions; government initiatives promote reform in collaboration with international partners. The education system is criticized for entrance exam-focused competition starting early, with over 95% of middle school students attending private hagwons and enduring long study hours, including forced night study, prioritizing admission to prestigious SKY universities and contributing to student fatigue and health issues. Hagwons are costly and linked to low birthrates; operating hours are regulated but enforcement varies. Special Purpose High Schools evoke criticism for elitism and high tuition.

A notable mismatch exists between jobs and university majors, with only 36.8% job-major alignment, ranking South Korea lowest among OECD countries in skill matching. Student well-being concerns include overemphasis on academics at the expense of health, creativity, and happiness. Issues include neglected physical education, heavy workloads, restricted student rights, high teacher dissatisfaction, bullying, and restrictive policies such as a ban on coffee in schools to improve health.
