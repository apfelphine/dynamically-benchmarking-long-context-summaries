# **Final Summary**

Bangladesh’s education system spans pre-primary to higher education, featuring standardized exams at Grades 5, 8, 10, and 12, and a rigid stream-based structure from Grade 9 onward. It includes national, international, and religious institutions (Qawmi, Alia), with governance split across ministries and regional boards. Despite progress in literacy (from 51.77% to 74.66%), challenges persist in funding (0.94–2.2% of GDP), teacher attendance (15.5% absence), infrastructure, and equity.

In contrast, Canada offers a decentralized, well-resourced model with strong vocational and private pathways, including IB and AP programs, and competitive university admissions. Poland restructured its system in 2017 by extending primary education to eight years and eliminating *gimnazjum*, now offering clear academic and vocational tracks leading to the *Matura*. Its tertiary system follows the Bologna model, with strong foreign language education (67.9% English) and a robust national minority education sector. A 1999 reform introduced a 6+3+3 structure, improving OECD rankings, while the 2017 PiS government reverted to an 8+4/5 structure, altering history curricula to emphasize Polish and noble figures, removing communism from 19th-century teaching, and promoting nationalism. A 2006 "zero tolerance" reform strengthened teacher protections and penalties for school violence.

Saudi Arabia has modernized significantly, with women now constituting 60% of university students and 85% of employed women in education. It offers extensive scholarships, supports gifted and special education, and has established women’s universities like PNU. Critics argue the system remains overly religious, emphasizes rote learning, and lacks marketable skills despite reforms like the $2 billion *Tatweer* initiative.

The Czech Republic emphasizes academic rigor and standardized assessments (*maturita*), with a balanced mix of academic and vocational tracks. New Zealand provides free, secular education from age 5 to 19, with strong cultural inclusivity through Māori language schools (*kōhanga* and *kura kaupapa*), though Māori language use remains low and math performance has declined—9-year-olds ranked 34th in TIMSS, with multiplication proficiency dropping from 47% to 37% between 2001 and 2009. Bullying remains widespread, with inconsistent policy implementation. Rising tertiary costs and student debt are also major concerns.

Mexico offers compulsory primary and junior high education (Grades 1–9), with increasing inclusion of high school (*preparatoria* or *bachillerato*). Bilingual education and vocational tracks (*técnica*, *tecnología*) are available, and the 1993 General Education Law mandates inclusive education via *USAER* units. However, implementation remains weak due to unclear roles, lack of training, poor collaboration, and limited accessibility—especially for sensory disabilities. Teacher confidence grows with experience and training. Despite high primary attendance (>90%), secondary and higher education completion rates are low (62% and 45%, respectively), with rural schools facing poor infrastructure and underfunding. Mexico ranks below average in math, science, and reading (OECD 2012). A 2013 reform shifted control from the teachers’ union (SNTE) to central and state governments, though tensions with CNTE persist. Higher education follows a US-style structure with a 4-year *Licenciatura*, followed by a 2-year *Maestría* and 3-year *Doctorado*, with a *Doctor en Ciencias* as a higher doctorate. Most public universities lack part-time programs. Intercultural Universities (established in 2004) aim to increase indigenous enrollment, which remains low (1–3% of higher education students despite 10% of the population being indigenous). As of 2015, Mexico had 151 international schools offering English-medium curricula. The system includes academic and technical tracks, culminating in *Licenciatura*, *Maestría*, and *Doctorado* degrees, and aligns with the Bologna Process.

Historically, Mexico’s education system has deep roots in pre-Columbian Mesoamerican societies, where elite Aztec schools trained warriors and priests. After the Spanish conquest, Catholic friars established education systems to evangelize indigenous populations, creating literacy in Nahuatl and other languages, though literacy remained limited to men. Elite Spanish-descended men received education through institutions like the Royal and Pontifical University of Mexico, while girls and mixed-race individuals had minimal access. Post-independence, liberal reforms promoted secular, free, and compulsory public education, culminating in the separation of church and state under Benito Juárez. The Porfiriato expanded public schooling as a tool for modernization, though illiteracy persisted and teacher training was inadequate. Despite these efforts, education remained highly stratified, with access shaped by class, gender, and race. Ongoing tensions between secular and religious influences continue to shape Mexico’s educational landscape.

Post-1940, the SEP promoted free textbooks, including Mexican art, reinforcing national identity. The National Autonomous University of Mexico ranks 103rd globally (QS), the highest in the country, while the Monterrey Institute of Technology is the top private university (ranked 158th globally). Private business schools (IPADE, EGADE) are among the top 10 globally in recruiter rankings (Wall Street Journal survey), reflecting strong private sector engagement in higher education.

Pakistan’s education system features a primary completion rate of ~67.5%, with a curriculum based on the English system, including arts, science, math, religious, and social studies. Pre-school stages are Play Group, Nursery, and Kindergarten. Secondary education begins at Year 9 (SSC-I and SSC-II), leading to a Secondary School Certificate (SSC), followed by intermediate colleges (HSSC-I and HSSC-II) for the Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC), with streams in pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities, and commerce. Alternative qualifications such as GCE (O-Level and A-Level), IGCSE, and AP exams are available, especially in private schools. Technical education is offered via TSC and DAE (equivalent to 10th and 12th grades), leading to associate engineering degrees. Tertiary education has a low graduate rate—6% of Pakistanis are university graduates (9% men, 3.5% women) as of 2007, with a target of 15% by 2020. Degree programs include engineering, medicine, law, pharmacy, and BA/BSc/BCom, accredited by national councils.

Education in Pakistan is structured into formal, non-formal, and informal stages. Formal education includes pass and honours bachelor’s degrees, with a gradual shift toward honours programs. Teacher education offers multiple pathways, from associate to PhD degrees, though training remains knowledge-focused and lacks practical application. Non-formal education includes vocational training and apprenticeships, while informal education is increasingly supported by NGOs. Madrassas, prevalent in poorer communities, offer Islamic education with some secular subjects and are numerous, often outnumbering public schools. Gender disparity remains a significant issue, with women facing lower enrollment, literacy rates (42% vs. men’s 67%), and limited access due to patriarchal norms, unsafe infrastructure, and cultural barriers. Despite improvements in enrollment ratios and policy efforts, women remain underrepresented in higher education and STEMM fields, especially in medicine, where quotas were later struck down as unconstitutional. While low-cost private schools (LCPSs) may help close gaps, they are predominantly attended by males, exacerbating gender imbalances. Women's academic performance is generally superior to men’s, except in mathematics, and systemic sexism and lack of work-life balance support remain challenges.

Education in Pakistan faces significant gender disparities, driven by parental disinterest, cultural norms, high costs, and poor quality. Female students receive less investment in education and health, and transgender individuals, though granted legal recognition under the 2018 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, remain marginalized and harassed. The overall quality of education is declining due to outdated curricula, teacher shortages, inadequate infrastructure, and a lack of relevance to modern needs. Teacher education is weak, with insufficient training, outdated curricula, and limited focus on human rights. Technical and vocational education (TVET) is underdeveloped, despite its potential to boost employment and economic growth. Distance learning, exemplified by Teleschool and AIOU, improves access—especially for women and rural populations—but remains limited by internet access and infrastructure. While notable achievements in science and education exist (e.g., Abdus Salam, Atta-ur-Rahman), systemic reforms are needed to ensure equitable, high-quality, and relevant education for all.

Pakistan has made notable contributions in science and education through individuals like Ayub Ommaya, Mahbub-ul-Haq, Ismat Beg, Arfa Abdul Karim Randhawa, Dr. Naweed Syed, Nergis Mavalvala, and Muhammad Irfan-Maqsood. Despite these achievements, education expenditure remains low—around 2% of GDP, with a proposed target of 7% lacking clear justification or feasibility. Chronic underfunding has led to deteriorating school infrastructure, especially in Punjab, and has reinforced religious influences in education, including in curricula that marginalize minorities, promote extremist ideologies, and misrepresent scientific concepts like evolution. Literacy has improved significantly since 1951, reaching 60.65% in 2023, but gender and regional disparities persist. The education system faces structural challenges requiring an unconventional, politically resilient approach to achieve sustainable growth.

Pakistan’s literacy rates vary significantly by province and district. As of 2023, Punjab leads with 66.25%, followed by Sindh (57.54%), Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (51.09%), and Balochistan (42.01%). Islamabad has the highest literacy rate at 83.97%, with several districts in Punjab and Sindh exceeding 75% and some surpassing 80%. Youth literacy (15–24 years) is strong in regions like Upper Chitral (95%) and Rawalpindi (90.08%). Mean years of schooling have improved over time, especially in Punjab and Islamabad, while regions like Balochistan and FATA lag behind. The 2023 census identifies 14 districts with literacy ≥75% and 13 with youth literacy ≥85%.

Pakistan's education system faces challenges including low literacy (60.65%, second lowest in South Asia), poverty, gender inequality, rural access barriers, teacher absenteeism, and inadequate infrastructure. Compared to Indonesia (90%) and Malaysia (89%), Pakistan lags in literacy and educational development. International education opportunities exist via Chinese institutions through online programs, with 439 international schools recognized by ISC.

Japan's education system is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Lower secondary (grades 7–9) covers students aged 12–15, with nearly all completing the level (98.3% by 2012). Instruction includes lectures, media, and labs, with a standardized curriculum. English became compulsory in elementary schools in 2011, increasing demand for English teachers through the JET program. Upper secondary (grades 10–12) is not compulsory but 98.8% of junior high graduates enroll; most schools offer academic or vocational tracks. Public high schools are tuition-free since 2010. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, and technical colleges, with universities being most prestigious (e.g., University of Tokyo, Kyoto University). The system emphasizes academic rigor, with students preparing for university entrance via *juku* (cram schools). Grading uses a 5-point scale (5 = excellent to 1 = failed), with traditional Japanese terms like *shū* and *fuka*. Government policies under the Basic Act on Education (2007) and CRPD (ratified 2014) ensure equal access and support for students with disabilities through inclusive or specialized education programs.

Special education reforms in Japan began in the 1990s, leading to the "Resource Room System" and later inclusive education (2006), aiming to integrate students with disabilities into mainstream classrooms. Despite this, extracurricular "shadow education" (e.g., *juku*, practice exams) is widespread, with over 86% of students with university plans participating, often in multiple forms. Criticisms include intense academic pressure, high suicide and bullying rates (especially in elementary schools), psychological harm, and limited free time. While *juku* is deeply embedded in the system and economically significant, it raises concerns about student well-being and equity. International schools exist but are not officially accredited and do not fulfill compulsory education.

Russia’s education system includes a mandatory **Unified State Exam (USE)** introduced in 2007, which assesses students after nine years of schooling and is now fully required for university admission. The USE uses a two-stage grading system—primary and final grades—with criticism over transparency. Specialized institutions provide education for children with physical and mental disabilities, though access remains limited, especially for those with severe developmental challenges. At the tertiary level, Russia has over 8 million students enrolled in state and private universities, with a traditional five-year specialist degree model being phased out in favor of a **Bologna Process-aligned** structure featuring a four-year bachelor’s and two-year master’s degree. Despite efforts to consolidate institutions and improve standards, challenges remain in regional access, integration of students with disabilities, and academic flexibility.

Russia’s tertiary education system has adopted the Bologna Process formally, but critics argue it lacks substantive curriculum reform, with BSc/BA degrees seen as irrelevant and the MSc/MA stage now costly and less valuable. Postgraduate education retains a unique Soviet structure with degrees (Candidate of Sciences and Doctor of Sciences) based on original research, often equated to a Ph.D. or habilitation, though with distinct requirements and limited mobility. Science outreach is now legally regulated under Federal Law №85-FZ, which critics claim enables prior censorship of knowledge dissemination, violating constitutional rights. Additionally, the government has intensified "patriotic education" in schools, including mandatory "Important Conversations" lessons, criticized for their militaristic tone and leading to student investigations or threats of expulsion. Russia’s education system includes a new law (passed in December 2024) requiring foreign citizens to pass a Russian language exam to enroll in schools.

The Netherlands features a decentralized, well-funded system coordinated by the Ministry of Education, Culture and Science with municipal governments. Compulsory education starts at age 5 (commonly at age 4), with partial compulsory education from age 16. Public, special (religious), and general-special schools receive equal government funding; private schools are rare. Schools are inspected for quality and policy adherence. Elementary education (ages 4–12) includes reading, writing, and arithmetic; English is taught in grades 7–8. The Cito test helps determine secondary education path. Secondary education begins at age 12 with a bridge year (*brugklas*). Students choose between VMBO (pre-vocational), HAVO (general academic), or VWO (advanced academic). Students can move up or down levels based on performance or interest. VMBO has four paths (TL, GL, KBL, BBL) and practical training; VMBO students can later access HAVO or VWO. The system emphasizes flexibility, personal development, and access to higher education regardless of initial placement.

The Netherlands offers three main post-secondary pathways:  
- **MBO** (vocational): 4 levels (1–4), offering school-based or apprenticeship training for skilled trades and support roles.  
- **HBO** (higher professional education): equivalent to college, taught at universities of applied sciences, requiring 240 credits (4 years), with degrees like BA, BSc, or B.Eng.  
- **WO** (scientific/academic): university-level education, requiring 180 credits (3 years), with degrees like BA, BSc, LL.B., and PhDs awarded after research-based theses.  
Higher education follows a **three-cycle system** (bachelor’s, master’s, PhD) aligned with the Bologna Process, using ECTS credits (1 credit = 28 hours). Admission to WO requires a VWO diploma or HBO propaedeuse; HBO requires HAVO or MBO-4. A **quota system** and entrance exams apply in some cases. Quality assurance is ensured by the **NVAO**, which accredits programmes for government funding and legal recognition, with accredited programmes listed in CROHO.

Historically, education was stratified by socioeconomic class: primary schools for working-class students, middle-level schools (mulo, hbs, mms) for middle-class students, and gymnasium/universities for upper-class students. Key 19th-century reforms expanded curricula and introduced secular and religious schooling. The 1874 *Kinderwetje* banned child labor under 12, and compulsory education began in 1901. The 1963 *Mammoetwet* streamlined secondary education into four streams (vmbo, havo, vwo, and mavo), later restructured into vmbo, havo, and vwo with a three-year *basisvorming* phase. In the 1990s, reforms emphasized autonomy, information skills, and subject integration. Islamic schools were introduced in 2003 to serve immigrant communities.

**South Korea’s education system** is rooted in Confucian meritocracy, with traditional *seodang* schools and a long-standing emphasis on literary and academic excellence. Post-1945 reforms introduced democratic, decentralized education under U.S. influence, but later centralization under the Ministry of Education led to rigid curricula and limited access to higher education. During the Japanese occupation (1905–1945), education was used for assimilation, restricting Korean students to basic levels and suppressing Korean culture.

The system is highly structured and competitive, with teacher-led primary education, banned mobile phone use, and illegal corporal punishment since 2011. Secondary education is nearly universal (98% completion), with academic and specialized tracks (science, foreign language, art), competitive entrance exams, and high tuition costs. Vocational education has been revitalized through *Meister Schools*—German-style institutions offering apprenticeships, 100% employment rates, and pathways to bypass university exams after three years of work. Meister graduates earn higher incomes and secure jobs at top conglomerates like Samsung. Higher education is dominated by private institutions, with Seoul National University, Korea University, and Yonsei University ("SKY" universities) being most prestigious. The system is evolving to balance academic and vocational paths in response to economic and labor market demands.

South Korea has a long history of English education, evolving from early foreign language training in the Joseon Dynasty to a modern, exam-focused system emphasizing oral proficiency. Despite high spending on English education, early assessments showed low proficiency, but by 2020, South Korea ranked 6th in Asia for English ability. The system is heavily centered on university entrance, driven by intense competition, *hagwons* (cram schools), and social stigma against vocational education. This results in excessive study hours, poor student well-being, low happiness, health issues, and a mismatch between majors and jobs. Critics argue the system prioritizes economic output over student welfare, leading to concerns about mental health, creativity, and student rights. Efforts to reform vocational education and reduce academic pressure have been ongoing, but systemic challenges remain.
