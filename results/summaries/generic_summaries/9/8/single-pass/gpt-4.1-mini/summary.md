# Summary of Global Education Systems

## Bangladesh
Bangladesh’s education system is structured in pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with instruction offered in Bengali or English. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB) oversees curriculum and standardized exams, with compulsory Bengali and English. Students select from Arts, Commerce, or Science streams in secondary education, with Science favored in urban areas. Cadet colleges and various international and religious schools also form part of the system. Primary education is managed by the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education, while secondary and higher education fall under the Ministry of Education. Notably, from 2023, Primary Education Completion (PEC) and Junior School Certificate (JSC) exams are discontinued. Tertiary education comprises public, private, and international universities. Technical and vocational education is coordinated by the Technical Education Board with diplomas leading to further university studies. Alternative systems include international schools following British and IB curricula, madrasah education with "Alia" and "Qawmi" madrasahs, and non-formal NGO-run schools. Challenges include quality issues, poverty, gender disparities favoring girls in some regions, and reliance on rote learning. Literacy improved from 51.77% in 2011 to 74.66% in 2022, with public education spending around 2% of GDP.

## Canada
Education responsibility lies mainly with provinces, with the federal government overseeing education for military colleges and Indigenous peoples. Compulsory education laws were implemented between 1871 and 1943 across provinces. Section 15(2) of the Charter allows targeted programs for disadvantaged groups, including racialized students, though data collection methods have raised concerns. The Canadian Education Statistics Council and OECD’s PISA coordinate education assessments. Canada spends about 5.2% of GDP on education, with high tertiary enrollment rates. Education includes co-educational public schools, private schools (6% attendance), and religiously funded systems, particularly Catholic schools in Ontario. School structure varies by province, with 13 grades generally spanning kindergarten to grade 12; Quebec ends secondary at grade 11. Post-secondary education includes universities offering bachelor's to doctorate degrees and vocational colleges. Private universities and religious schools exist with varying funding and regulations. Bilingual education in English and French is widespread.

## Saudi Arabia
Education traces back to Islamic schools (kuttabs) linked to mosques. Formal state education began in 1926, evolving to free education under the Ministry of Education and other agencies. Primary education is compulsory from ages 6-12, followed by three years each of intermediate and secondary education, with religious studies prominent throughout. Higher education features 24 government universities offering up to PhD levels, with women comprising 60% of university students. Gifted education began formally in 1969 and expanded significantly after 2002, with enrichment programs for identified talented students. Special education services exist, though general education teachers receive minimal training in inclusion. Private education supplements government schools, with many private schools transitioning from rented villas to proper educational facilities. International and community schools serve foreign nationals with various curricula, particularly Philippine schools. Literacy rates have improved but vary regionally and by gender. Major reform projects include the King Abdullah Project aiming to improve skills and teaching quality. Criticism focuses on rote learning, religious intolerance in textbooks, lack of marketable skills, and the need for reforms toward critical thinking and secular vocational education.

## Czech Republic
Elementary education lasts nine years, split into two stages with subjects including Czech, foreign languages, and sciences. Secondary education involves vocational schools, professional schools, grammar schools, and lyceums, with graduation dependent on passing the "maturita" exam. Special schools accommodate disabled or underperforming students. Higher professional schools and universities offer bachelor's to doctoral degrees with specialized titles. The school year runs September to June, divided into semesters, with multiple holidays.

## New Zealand
Early childhood education includes kindergartens and Māori-language immersion schools. Primary education spans Years 1–8, with secondary covering Years 9–13. Education is compulsory between ages 6 and 16, with free schooling for eligible students. State schools predominate (85% enrollment), with state-integrated (religious/special character) and private schools also present. The national curriculum covers key learning areas, with NCEA as the main qualification from Year 11. Home-schooling is allowed with exemptions. Funding is government-based per-student with parental donations common. Tertiary education includes universities and vocational institutions, with government loans and allowances available to students. Concerns exist about literacy and numeracy standards, especially among Māori and Pacific Island students, and widespread bullying.

## Poland
Compulsory education starts at age six or seven with eight years of primary school followed by upper secondary education. Recent reforms abolished the junior high system, reverting to extended primary and secondary schooling. Upper secondary education includes general, vocational, and technical schools, with final exams (Matura) granting access to higher education. Tertiary education follows the Bologna Process with bachelor's and master's degrees, plus doctoral studies. Universities use a numeric grading scale. Foreign language learning is widespread. Poland has a rich educational history dating back to the 12th century, featuring early universities and educational reforms. Noted historical reforms improved core subject focus and vocational training.

## Mexico
Mexico’s basic education is divided into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and high school (10–12). Primary education may include bilingual instruction. Junior high introduces more specialized subjects and vocational options. High school programs prepare students for university or vocational careers. Educational integration aims to include special needs students, though challenges persist in training and accessibility. Despite high primary attendance, secondary enrollment and completion are lower, with only about a quarter pursuing higher education. Education has a long history from pre-Hispanic times through colonial and modern eras, with ongoing reforms focused on decentralization and quality. Higher education offers bachelor's to doctoral degrees, aligned with the Bologna Process. Indigenous representation in higher education remains low, leading to specialized intercultural universities. Mexico hosts over 150 international schools.

## Pakistan
Pakistan’s education system is influenced by the British model, comprising pre-school, primary (years 1–5), middle (6–8), secondary (9–10), higher secondary (11–12), and tertiary levels. Instruction languages include Urdu, English, and regional languages. Secondary education culminates in board exams (SSC, HSSC), with alternative British systems (O/A Levels) and technical/vocational options available. Tertiary education offers professional degrees with specialized accreditation councils. Madrassas provide religious education, especially among lower-income families. Gender disparities favor males, though female enrollment and literacy have improved. Quality challenges include outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and infrastructure deficits. Distance learning gained importance during COVID-19. Key Pakistani scientific and educational figures have contributed significantly nationally and internationally. Education expenditure hovers around 2% of GDP, with calls to increase this substantially.

## Japan
Japan's education system began in the 6th century with Confucian and Buddhist learning, evolving through periods including the Meiji Restoration (modernization and compulsory education), post-WWII reforms (American model influence), and recent policies emphasizing inclusion and holistic education. Schooling is divided into elementary (6 years), lower secondary (3 years), and upper secondary (3 years), followed by tertiary education including universities and vocational colleges. English education is supported by teaching assistants via the JET program. Extracurricular "shadow education" (cram schools) is widespread due to competitive university entrance exams. Criticism focuses on academic pressure, bullying, and limited emphasis on creative thinking. The grading system varies but commonly uses a scale from 5 to 1 in secondary schools. Special needs education and inclusive practices have expanded since the 1990s.

## Russia
Russia's education includes general (preschool to secondary) and professional (vocational and higher) levels. Compulsory education lasts nine to eleven years, with primary, basic, and secondary stages. Vocational schools offer specialized training. Unified State Exam (USE) replaced university entrance tests to standardize admission. Special education services exist for physical and mental disabilities, though integration challenges remain. Tertiary education includes bachelor's, master's, specialist, candidate, and doctor degrees, with ongoing transition to the Bologna Process. The Ministry oversees quality assurance and reforms. Recent laws regulate educational outreach, with criticism of increased ideological control. Education in minority languages varies across regions.

## Netherlands
The Dutch system starts compulsory education at age five with elementary school (8 grades) and three secondary tracks: VMBO (vocational), HAVO (senior general), and VWO (pre-university). Secondary education includes various levels with multiple profiles tailored to student ability and career goals. Higher education splits into universities of applied sciences (HBO) and research universities (WO), following the Bologna Process. Funding and governance involve local and national authorities. Freedom of education allows religious and special schools with equal funding. International schools are present. Criticisms focus on early tracking at age 12 causing misplacement and social inequality.

## South Korea
Education in Korea has been strongly influenced by Confucian values, emphasizing meritocracy and examinations. The formal system includes six years of elementary, three years of middle, three years of high school, followed by higher education. Secondary education features both general and specialized schools, with significant emphasis on university entrance exams (CSAT). Vocational education has declined socially but is being revitalized through Meister schools to meet industry demands. South Korea leads globally in tertiary enrollment but faces criticisms of excessive academic pressure, high private tutoring costs (hagwons), and student well-being concerns. English education is prioritized with widespread private investment. Higher education includes universities, industrial universities, and junior colleges, with top universities highly competitive. The government is working to balance academic rigor with student health and vocational relevance.
