**Education in Bangladesh**  
Bangladesh's education system is structured into pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with students choosing instruction in English or Bengali. The government allocates 60% of primary teaching positions to women and has no middle school system. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB) manages textbooks and standardized exams in Bengali or English, with a fixed curriculum and mandatory subjects. Students follow one of three streams—Arts, Commerce, or Science—after class 9, with only one elective subject allowed for change. Examinations at class 8 (JSC), 10 (SSC), and 12 (HSC) are standardized, with no classroom performance included in grading.  

Key institutions include international schools (offering British curricula), religious madrasahs (Qawmi and Alia), and cadet colleges. The government plans to build 1,000 primary schools in char areas. Tertiary education includes 44 government, 101 private, and 3 international universities. Challenges include gender disparity, low school attendance in rural areas, and a grading system that favors rote learning. The literacy rate is 74.66% (2022), up from 51.77% in 2011.  

**Education in Canada**  
Canada has a provincial education system with no national curriculum. Education is compulsory until age 18, with a mix of public, private, and religious schools. Kindergarten is typically age 5, and schooling ends at grade 12. Schools offer diverse curricula, including French immersion and Indigenous studies. The government funds education through provincial ministries, with public schools being secular and private schools often religiously affiliated.  

Key features include a strong emphasis on Indigenous and multicultural education, with programs in Native studies, antiracism, and community engagement. The federal government supports Indigenous education and language programs. The education system is highly decentralized, with variations across provinces. Canada spends about 5.2% of GDP on education and has a high tertiary enrollment rate.  

**Education in Saudi Arabia**  
Saudi Arabia's system is rooted in Islamic education, with religious studies forming a core component. The national curriculum includes mandatory Islamic studies, with a focus on memorization and rote learning. The system is free for all, with a strong government role in management. Pre-primary, primary, and secondary education are compulsory, with a structured progression through grades.  

The education system includes private and government schools, with a significant presence of madrasahs (Qawmi and Alia). The government has introduced reforms to modernize education and reduce religious dominance, including a shift toward secular content and vocational training. Challenges include low teacher quality, poor infrastructure, and a lack of innovation. The literacy rate is 74.66% (2022), with gender disparities in access.  

**Education in the Czech Republic**  
The Czech system features a nine-year primary education (grades 1–9), followed by a four-year secondary education (grades 10–13). Students take the maturita exam, required for university entry. Vocational and general education are offered, with specialized schools for students with disabilities. The education system is highly centralized, with a strong emphasis on academic standards and teacher training.  

Key features include a national curriculum, standardized exams, and a focus on student development. The school year runs from September to June, with two semesters. The education system has faced criticism for excessive academic pressure and a lack of innovation. The government invests in teacher training and infrastructure.  

**Education in New Zealand**  
New Zealand has a free, compulsory, and secular national education system from age 5 to 19. The curriculum includes the New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa (TMoA) for Māori-medium schools. Schools offer diverse programs, including special education and international curricula.  

The education system emphasizes inclusivity, with a strong focus on Māori language and culture. The government funds schools through a per-student model, with additional support for special needs and rural education. Challenges include low performance in international assessments and a lack of teacher training. The literacy rate is 97.5% (2020), with a high rate of youth literacy.  

**Education in Poland**  
Poland's education system has undergone significant reform, transitioning from a 6+3+3 structure to an 8-year primary system (grades 1–8), followed by a 4-year upper secondary education. The system emphasizes national curriculum and standardized exams.  

Key features include a strong focus on vocational training, with technical schools and vocational colleges offering practical education. The government has introduced reforms to modernize the curriculum and reduce religious influence. The education system is highly centralized, with a strong emphasis on teacher training and student performance.  

**Education in Mexico**  
Mexico's education system is divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary education lasts six years, followed by three years of secondary education. Students take national exams (SSC and HSSC) to progress.  

The system includes a strong emphasis on vocational training, with technical and vocational education programs. The government has introduced reforms to improve access and quality, including the integration of students with special needs. Challenges include low enrollment in secondary education, poor infrastructure, and gender disparities. The literacy rate is 94.86% (2018), with a high rate of youth literacy.  

**Education in Japan**  
Japan's education system is highly structured, with a nine-year compulsory education (grades 1–9), followed by a three-year upper secondary education. The system emphasizes academic performance, with a strong focus on standardized exams.  

Key features include a national curriculum, standardized exams (such as the Unified State Examination), and a strong emphasis on extracurricular activities. The education system is highly centralized, with a strong focus on teacher training and student performance. The system has faced criticism for excessive academic pressure and a lack of innovation.  

**Education in Russia**  
Russia's education system includes general and professional education, with a nine-year compulsory education (grades 1–9), followed by a two-year upper secondary education. The system emphasizes vocational training and standardized exams.  

Key features include a strong focus on vocational training, with a wide range of technical and professional programs. The government has introduced reforms to modernize the curriculum and improve access. The system is highly centralized, with a strong focus on teacher training and student performance.  

**Education in the Netherlands**  
The Dutch education system is decentralized, with compulsory education from age 5 to 18. Students are assessed annually and may be required to retake a year. The system includes a range of school types, including public, special (religious), and general-special schools.  

Key features include a strong emphasis on student autonomy, with a focus on individualized learning. The education system is highly flexible, with a strong focus on vocational and higher education. The system has faced criticism for early tracking and high academic pressure.  

**Education in South Korea**  
South Korea's education system is highly competitive, with a strong emphasis on university entrance. Students attend primary, middle, and high school, with a focus on standardized exams.  

Key features include a strong emphasis on academic performance, with a high rate of university enrollment. The system has faced criticism for excessive academic pressure, leading to high stress and mental health issues. Vocational education is being reformed to address the skills gap. The literacy rate is 99.9% (2023), with a high rate of youth literacy.
