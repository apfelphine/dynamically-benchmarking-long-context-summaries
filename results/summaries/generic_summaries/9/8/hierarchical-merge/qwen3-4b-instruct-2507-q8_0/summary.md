# **Merged Summary: Comparative Overview of Education Systems in Bangladesh, Canada, Saudi Arabia, the Czech Republic, New Zealand, Poland, Mexico, Pakistan, Russia, the Netherlands, and South Korea (with Japan as Comparative Context)**

## **Core System Structures and Key Features**

The education systems across these countries reflect diverse cultural, historical, and political contexts. **Bangladesh** follows a hierarchical structure from pre-primary to tertiary levels, with a critical分流 at Grade 9 into Arts, Commerce, or Science streams. Government schools are managed by regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education (BISEs), while private and madrasah systems—both religious and secular—coexist. Despite progress in literacy (from 51.77% to 74.66%), challenges persist: outdated curricula, high dropout rates, and significant gender and regional disparities. Education expenditure remains low (0.94%–2.2% of GDP), and refugee education, especially for Rohingya children, remains a critical gap.

In **Canada**, education is decentralized, with provinces managing policy, curriculum, and funding. Compulsory education runs from age 5–18 (ending at Grade 12 in most provinces), with strong equity initiatives such as Indigenous studies and language immersion. Post-secondary options include universities and vocational institutions, with competitive admissions based on GPA, essays, and extracurriculars. Private schools serve a small share of students but are linked to higher achievement, raising debates over access and religious exemptions.

**Saudi Arabia** is rooted in Islamic tradition, beginning with Quranic literacy in *kuttabs*. Compulsory education spans ages 6–12, with a strong emphasis on religious studies. The government has significantly increased spending on education, tripling it since 1970, and prioritizes gender equality, STEM, and inclusive education. Women now dominate university enrollment and public sector participation. However, challenges remain in curriculum modernization, teacher training, and special education, where school climates are often negative despite high demand for trained staff.

The **Czech Republic** offers a balanced, future-oriented model with two elementary stages, followed by vocational (VOŠ) or grammar schools. Public universities provide free Czech-language education to students under 26, while private institutions charge €2,000–€3,000 annually. Degree programs follow a three- to five-year structure, with a rigorous *zakouška* (oral defense) required for the master’s and PhD levels. The system emphasizes academic rigor and practical outcomes.

**New Zealand** delivers free, compulsory education from age 5 to 19 under the national curriculum (*Te Marautanga o Aotearoa*). Schools are categorized as state, state-integrated, or private, with most students attending state schools. Māori-language immersion programs have revived cultural education, though overall enrolment and outcomes for Māori and Pacific students remain poor. Persistent underperformance in math and science (34th in TIMSS 2012), deep inequities, and a lack of enforceable anti-bullying frameworks highlight systemic gaps in student well-being and mental health support.

**Poland** introduced a new 8-year primary system in 2017, replacing the old 6+3 model. The upper secondary system includes general, vocational, and profiled tracks. A 2017 curriculum shift removed communist references and promoted nationalistic narratives. The "zero tolerance" policy strengthened discipline and school safety, but has raised concerns about student autonomy and mental health. English is the dominant foreign language, reflecting a shift toward Eurocentric and national identity.

**Mexico** has seen significant expansion in primary enrollment (from 3 million to 32 million), with literacy rising from 83% to 94.86%. However, secondary and tertiary completion rates remain low (62% and 45%), and rural schools face infrastructure and access challenges. The 1992 reforms decentralized control and introduced merit-based pay, but tensions with the National Teachers’ Union (SNTE) persist. Higher education follows a Bologna-aligned model, with strong institutions like UNAM and Monterrey Institute of Technology. Indigenous representation remains low (1–3%), prompting the creation of Intercultural Universities.

**Pakistan** operates a multi-sectoral system with deep gender and religious disparities. Madrassas—especially in rural areas—promote religious extremism and marginalize minorities. Women face lower enrollment, literacy (42% vs. 67% for men), and limited access due to patriarchal norms and unsafe infrastructure. Teacher training is criticized for being knowledge-focused and lacking practical skills. Despite low education spending (2% of GDP), the system has produced global scientific leaders like Abdus Salam. Regional disparities are stark, with Balochistan at 42.01% literacy. International schools offer English-medium curricula, but inclusion and equity remain major challenges.

**Russia** has formally adopted the Bologna Process but retains weak tertiary education standards. Bachelor’s and specialist degrees are seen as academically weak and financially burdensome, with specialist programs unchanged since the Soviet era. Postgraduate degrees (Candidate of Sciences, Doctor of Sciences) are rigorous and research-based but lengthy and selective. A 2021 law promoting science outreach has been criticized for enabling prior censorship. "Patriotic education" and mandatory "Important Conversations" with a militaristic tone have led to student investigations and expulsions. Over 60 languages are taught, with strong support for Tatar, Bashkir, and Chuvash, though minority languages are underrepresented. A 2024 law requires foreign citizens to pass a Russian language exam, reinforcing linguistic gatekeeping.

The **Netherlands** operates a flexible, equitable system with compulsory education from age 5, and partial obligation at age 16. Students take the Cito test to determine their secondary path: **VMBO** (vocational), **HAVO** (general), or **VWO** (scientific). All schools receive equal funding, and private schools are rare. The system uses ECTS credits and is overseen by the NVAO for quality assurance. While inclusive and practical, early tracking via standardized tests can misplace gifted students and lead to underperformance and dropout. Socioeconomic disparities and unequal parental involvement contribute to inequities, with declining performance in international assessments.

**South Korea** is rooted in Confucian meritocracy, emphasizing academic excellence and career preparation. Over 98% of students complete secondary education, with highly competitive private and specialized high schools. Admission to universities is dominated by the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), which accounts for 60% of evaluation. Vocational education is growing through *Meister Schools* and industrial universities, offering apprenticeship-based training with high employment rates. However, the system faces intense academic pressure, widespread use of *hagwons* (cram schools), poor student well-being, and skill-mismatch between majors and jobs. Cultural stigma around vocational paths remains a barrier.

**Japan** offers a high-performing, well-structured model with strong equity and inclusion frameworks. The system spans elementary (1–6), junior high (7–9), and senior high (10–12), with public high schools being tuition-free. Private schools account for about 55% of upper secondary institutions. English became compulsory in 2011, increasing demand for English teachers. Higher education includes prestigious institutions like Tokyo and Kyoto Universities. Despite strong performance in math and science, the system suffers from intense academic pressure: over 86% of college-bound students attend private cram schools (*juku*), leading to 612,496 bullying cases and 317 suicides in 2019. The government promotes inclusive education through the Basic Act on Education and CRPD, with the Resource Room System supporting students with disabilities.

## **Key Themes and Contrasts**

Across all systems, **equity, access, and student well-being** emerge as central challenges. **Japan** exemplifies structural excellence and inclusive policy frameworks but at a high cost to student mental health due to academic pressure and private tutoring. **New Zealand** struggles with persistent underperformance among Indigenous populations and lacks a robust anti-bullying or mental health framework. **Pakistan** faces deep-rooted issues: underfunding (2% of GDP), religious bias, gender disparities, regional inequities, and a lack of secular inclusivity. **Russia** reflects strong state control and national identity, with education serving as a tool for ideological reinforcement, including language and patriotic messaging.

In contrast, **the Netherlands** balances equity and flexibility through a well-funded, decentralized model with clear pathways to higher education. **South Korea** prioritizes merit and academic rigor, producing high achievement but at the expense of student well-being and vocational inclusion. **Poland** emphasizes national identity and discipline, while **Mexico** focuses on inclusive integration and social equity, though implementation remains uneven.

The **Czech Republic** and **Canada** offer models of balance between academic rigor, vocational access, and equity, with strong post-secondary pathways and inclusive policies. **Saudi Arabia** demonstrates progress in gender equality and public investment, though curriculum modernization and inclusive education remain challenges.

Overall, these systems reveal a critical tension: **academic excellence must be balanced with student well-being, equity, and social inclusion**. While high-performing systems like Japan and the Netherlands achieve strong outcomes, they also expose the dangers of over-reliance on standardized testing, rigid tracking, and excessive pressure. For countries like Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Mexico, sustainable reform requires sustained investment, curriculum modernization, inclusive policies, and a shift from rote learning to outcome-based, practical education. The global lesson is clear: education systems must evolve not only in structure, but in values—prioritizing human development, diversity, and long-term societal well-being over rigid performance metrics.
