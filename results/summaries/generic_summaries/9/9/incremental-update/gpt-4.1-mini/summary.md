# Final Summary of Education Systems in Canada, the Czech Republic, Bangladesh, Poland, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, New Zealand, Russia, Mexico, South Korea, Japan, and the Netherlands

## Education in Canada  
Education is provincially administered and compulsory from roughly ages 4–7 through 16–18 or diploma completion, with protections for disadvantaged and minority language groups. Governance involves provincial ministries and diverse school boards. Kindergarten starts around age 5; graduation emphasizes upper secondary education (grades 10–12), except Quebec’s distinct CEGEP system. Alternatives and private schools complement the public system, with private institutions accounting for about 6% of enrollment mainly in Quebec. Post-secondary institutions offer diverse credentials, apprenticeships, and trades. Canada boasts high tertiary attainment, invests about 5.2% of GDP, and supports bilingual and Indigenous curricula. Assessment practices vary, with Alberta noted for rigorous testing.

## Education in the Czech Republic  
Elementary education spans nine grades with core subjects like Czech, foreign languages, math, and sciences. Secondary education requires entrance exams and divides into vocational, professional (maturita diploma), lyceums, and grammar schools; the maturita exam is key for university admission. Inclusive education is expanding, replacing special schools. Higher education comprises public and private universities, free for local-language studies, following Bachelor’s, Master’s, and doctoral degrees with an academic year from September to June.

## Education in Bangladesh  
Spanning pre-primary to tertiary levels, instruction is primarily Bengali or English under a national curriculum managed by multiple boards, including madrasahs. Primary education is government-managed; standardized exams like PEC and JSC were discontinued in 2023. Secondary education streams into Arts, Commerce, or Science, with exam-based assessments. The University Grants Commission oversees diverse public and private universities. Technical and vocational training, alternative international curricula, madrasahs, and non-formal education complement the system. Challenges include poverty, teacher absenteeism, rural gender gaps, dropout rates, and a colonial legacy of rote learning. Literacy rose to 74.66% by 2022; public spending is about 2% of GDP.

## Education in Poland  
Rooted in 16th-century religious and grammar schooling, compulsory education was established regionally in the 19th century and nationwide after 1919 independence. Recent reforms adjusted schooling structures, embedding nationalistic and Eurocentric history curricula. Secondary education divides into general and vocational tracks; the Matura exam is required for university entrance. University degrees follow the Bologna model. English and German are main foreign languages; Poland hosts historic institutions like Jagiellonian University (1364) and created the world’s first state education ministry (1773).

## Education in Saudi Arabia  
Rooted in Islamic tradition, formal education began in 1926; girls’ schools opened in 1964. Free education is overseen by the Ministry and Supreme Committee, with compulsory primary and optional pre-primary education. The curriculum includes Arabic, Islamic studies, math, science, and English starting grade 1 (from 2021). Intermediate and secondary levels last three years each, with general and specialized tracks, and reforms in Islamic subjects. Enrollment is high; 24 government universities coexist with scholarship programs for overseas study. Women, now 60% of university students, face labor market underrepresentation but have expanding opportunities. Gifted and special education programs exist but are limited. Private and over 200 international schools serve expatriates, using gender-segregated instruction. Literacy approaches 98% among youth; reforms aim to reduce rote learning, intolerance, and improve vocational education.

## Education in Pakistan  
Inspired by British models, schooling spans pre-primary to tertiary with major streams in arts, sciences, and religious studies. Attendance and completion rates hover around 66–67%, with prevalent single-sex schools. Alternative qualifications like O/A Levels are common. Madrassas combine secular and religious instruction but face critiques for rote learning and conservatism. Gender disparity is significant: female literacy around 42%, with underrepresentation in STEM and cultural barriers to female employment. Quality issues stem from teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and low funding (~2.2% of GNP). TVET addresses skills gaps but faces challenges. Distance learning expanded during COVID-19, improving access amid infrastructural and regional disparities. Literacy and youth education years vary regionally, with ongoing challenges.

## Education in New Zealand  
Māori oral traditions preceded European schooling, with a national free, secular, compulsory system since 1878. Early childhood education includes culturally specific programs (Kōhanga Reo) under the Te Whāriki curriculum. Compulsory schooling spans ages 6 to 16, with primary, intermediate, and secondary stages. The curriculum offers English- and Māori-medium versions with eight key learning areas; qualification options include NCEA, Cambridge, and IB. Most schools are state-run; enrollment zoning affects access and housing markets. Māori language education has legal recognition and demonstrated success. Historical manual and technical training evolved post-WWII. Tertiary education is unified under the New Zealand Qualifications Framework. Funding supports students and special needs, though rising tertiary fees raise concerns. Educational attainment is generally high but disparities, especially for Māori and Pacific students, remain. Bullying persists despite government interventions.

## Education in Russia  
General education includes preschool, primary, basic, and secondary stages, with professional and higher education including Bachelor (4 years), Specialist (5–6 years), Master’s (2 years), and postgraduate degrees. Access to preschool grew post-2005 but remains uneven and partly paid by families. Secondary education follows a fixed curriculum and 5-point grading; vocational tracks build on nine years of compulsory schooling. The Unified State Examination (USE) standardizes university admissions since 2009. Special education offers tailored curricula, though inclusion is limited. With over 1,100 institutions, Russia has high tertiary attainment (>50%), with ongoing reforms and mergers into federal universities. The Bologna Process is partially implemented amid criticism. Postgraduate degrees include Candidate (PhD equivalent) and Doctor of Sciences. Since 2020, patriotic education promotes national identity with sanctions for refusal. Minority language education is extensive. From December 2024, foreign students must pass a Russian language exam for school admission.

## Education in Mexico  
Education comprises 2–3 years preschool, 6 years primary, 3 years junior high (secundaria), and 3 years high school (preparatoria), now compulsory. Since 1993, special needs students are integrated despite training and accessibility challenges. Completion rates decline after primary: about 45% complete secondary and 23% hold college degrees. Higher education follows a hybrid U.S./Bologna model with Licenciatura, Maestría, Doctorado, and Doctor en Ciencias degrees. Intercultural Universities serve indigenous populations with low higher education enrollment. Over 150 international schools offer English and foreign curricula as of 2015. The SEP commemorated free textbook provision in 2019, highlighting Mexican art inclusion. The National Autonomous University ranks #103 worldwide (2019); Monterrey Institute of Technology and private business schools IPADE and EGADE have international recognition.

Historically, pre-colonial Aztec education centered on warrior and priest training; colonial education focused on Catholic evangelization through institutions like Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco and the 1551 Royal University, emphasizing Spanish literacy and religious indoctrination with gender and class restrictions. Post-independence reforms secularized, centralized, and expanded education introducing positivist ideas. The Porfiriato era centralized education amid persistent illiteracy. Women became key educators and activists. Post-revolution education centralized under the SEP with often assimilationist policies toward indigenous cultures. Socialist education promoted anti-religious policies and later secular curricula. Mid-20th-century expansion increased literacy and enrollment amid influential teachers’ unions. Student protests (1968–71) spurred reforms emphasizing adult education. Higher education grew with CONACYT and Conalep. Economic crises and neoliberal reforms in the 1980s–90s decentralized education, instituted merit pay, and revised curricula amid union protests. Enrollment grew from 3 million (1950) to 32 million (2011); literacy reached about 95% (2018) with >97% attendance ages 6–14 by 2020 despite regional disparities.

## Education in South Korea  
Rooted in Confucian meritocracy, modernized post-WWII, with near-universal secondary completion (~98%) and peak investment of 4.5% GNP. Structure includes primary (grades 1–6), secondary (general and vocational), and tertiary levels. Primary education emphasizes core subjects; corporal punishment and mobile phones are banned. Secondary schools are competitive and costly, with vocational tracks like Meister Schools offering apprenticeships and near-full employment, though vocational enrollment remains low. Higher education is mostly private with bachelor’s to doctoral degrees; admission relies on the highly competitive College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT). Top universities include Seoul National, Korea, Yonsei (SKY), KAIST, and POSTECH.

English education evolved from early translator training to proficiency-focused programs; proficiency was low in the early 2000s but improved by 2020. Challenges include intense academic pressure, reliance on cram schools (hagwons), health impact, low skill-job match rates (~37% OECD), and social stigma around vocational education despite workforce importance.

## Education in Japan  
Rooted in 6th-century Buddhist and Confucian traditions, evolving through samurai and merchant schools and Western studies at the Edo period’s end, Japan’s education modernized during Meiji Restoration and post-WWII Allied reforms. Compulsory schooling covers elementary (grades 1–6, ages 6–12) and lower secondary (grades 7–9, ages 12–15) with near-universal attendance; upper secondary (grades 10–12, ages 15–18) is non-compulsory but ~99% enrolled, mixing academic and vocational tracks, and provisions for disabled students. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, technical colleges, and special training schools serving over 2.89 million students (2017). Public universities are national or local; most are private; top include University of Tokyo and Kyoto University.

Curricula feature core subjects with compulsory English since 2011, standardized textbooks, and typical university grading from Excellent (90–100%) to Fail (<60%). Entrance exams govern progression, supplemented by private cram schools (juku). The Basic Act on Education (2007) and CRPD ratification (2014) promote inclusivity in special needs education with integrated or specialized programs.

Since the 1990s, Japan has shifted toward inclusive education. Shadow education (cram schools, practice exams) is widespread (86% participation for college-bound students), reflecting academic pressure that contributes to psychological stress, bullying (verbal and online), and concerns over limited independent thinking and free time, though youth suicide rates align globally. About 30–40 international schools mainly teach in English but are outside compulsory education.

## Education in the Netherlands  
Education is coordinated by the Ministry and local governments, with compulsory schooling from age five until 16 or diploma completion. Schools include public, special (often religious), and neutral types, equally funded. Elementary education covers ages 4–12 (groups 1–8), formally compulsory from five, integrating early education since 1985. English is introduced in later primary grades, with standardized tests guiding secondary placement.

Secondary education starts around age 12, stratified into three main tracks with a bridge year for flexible progression:  
- **VMBO (4 years):** Vocational education across four levels including practical training via Praktijkonderwijs, ending typically at 16.  
- **HAVO (5 years):** General education preparing for higher professional education (HBO).  
- **VWO (6 years):** Pre-university education preparing for research universities, subdivided into atheneum and gymnasium (with Latin and Greek).  

A shared core curriculum underpins early secondary years, followed by specialization into four profiles: arts/languages, social sciences/economics, natural sciences/medical, and physics/technical. Adult education offers diploma completion or exam retakes.

Vocational education (MBO) includes school-based and apprenticeship routes lasting up to four years for ages 16–35, with apprentices typically older. Graduates progress to HBO or work. Regional Education Centers provide MBO.

Higher education comprises:  
- **Universities of Applied Sciences (HBO):** Professionally oriented bachelor’s (3–6 years), requiring HAVO, VWO, or MBO-4, offered by over 40 institutions.  
- **Research Universities (WO):** Academic bachelor’s (3 years), master’s, and PhD degrees, requiring VWO or HBO propaedeuse, with quality assured by the NVAO.  

### Historical Development and Reforms  
The 1857 Lower Education Act recognized religious private schools without funding, sparking the "school struggle," achieving equal funding in 1917. Early stratification aligned with socioeconomic class. Compulsory education began in 1901 for ages 6–12, expanding secondary attendance. The 1963 Mammoetwet law restructured secondary education into four streams (lts/vbo, mavo, havo, vwo), extended compulsory schooling to 10 years, and modernized curricula through 1990s reforms. Islamic schools emerged amid demographic changes.

### School Calendar and Support  
The school year includes six-week staggered regional summer holidays, autumn, Christmas, spring, and May breaks; universities have longer summer and flexible terms. Since 2004, schools must maintain care teams to address student health and social issues in coordination with regional health services.

### Criticisms and Challenges  
The Dutch system faces criticism for early student tracking at age 12 based on IQ and standardized tests like the Cito, despite evidence that IQ can improve with proper educational support. This early streaming risks misplacing gifted students in lower tracks, contributing to underachievement and higher dropout rates. Parental involvement in school choice varies, with research linking lower income to recommendations for lower educational levels, exacerbating inequality. Although movement between tracks is possible, it requires significant effort. International rankings since 2006 have declined, with Dutch students now performing only slightly above average in reading, math, and science.
