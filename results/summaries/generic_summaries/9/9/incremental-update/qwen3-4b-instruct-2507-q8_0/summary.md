# **Final Summary**

Education systems globally reflect diverse cultural, political, and historical influences, with core challenges including equity, inclusion, teacher quality, curriculum relevance, infrastructure, and student well-being. In **Canada**, education is provincially governed with universal kindergarten and compulsory schooling from age 5–7 to 16 (or 18 in some provinces), ending at Grade 12 (except in Quebec). The system emphasizes equity, inclusive access, and supports Indigenous content and targeted equity programs. Religious and language-based schools are constitutionally protected, and post-secondary education is funded provincially with no national accreditation.

In **New Zealand**, a secular, national, free, and compulsory system established in 1878 includes state, state-integrated, and private schools (state schools serve 85% of students). The structure spans primary (Years 1–8), intermediate (Years 7–8), and secondary (Years 9–13), with composite schools serving students from Year 1 to 13. Māori language and cultural education has seen a revival through kōhanga reo and kura kaupapa Māori schools, with Māori students achieving better outcomes in mainstream schools. However, the system faces challenges in numeracy, bullying (second highest internationally), and equity—particularly in access via enrolment schemes. Tertiary education is structured under the NZ Qualifications Framework, with limited welfare support and rising student debt.

**Russia** operates a dual education system with nine-year compulsory schooling (expanded to eleven years in some regions), followed by a choice between general or vocational tracks. The Unified State Examination (USE) standardizes university admissions, though its transparency is criticized. Vocational training (three years) provides high school equivalency, and over half of adults hold university degrees. Tertiary education has adopted a Bologna-style model, but implementation remains superficial. Postgraduate degrees follow a unique Soviet structure (Candidate of Sciences, Doctor of Sciences). The government promotes "patriotic education" and "science outreach," the latter criticized for censorship and violations of free expression. A 2024 law requires foreign citizens to pass a Russian language exam to enroll, raising access and integration concerns.

**Mexico** has a long history of educational evolution—from pre-Columbian elite schools to colonial religious instruction and post-independence secularization. The current system includes primary (1–6), junior high (7–9), and high school (10–12), with vocational and bilingual pathways. Despite significant gains in attendance and literacy (97.5% in 2020), rural illiteracy and low outcomes persist. The system remains a site of tension between secularism and Catholic influence, with teacher unions wielding political power. The National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM) and Monterrey Institute rank highly globally.

**South Korea** features a Confucian-rooted system emphasizing meritocracy and academic excellence. A uniform 6-4-3-4 curriculum and high government investment have driven near-universal enrollment and economic growth. However, intense academic pressure, high suicide rates, and over-reliance on private cram schools (hagwons) contribute to poor student well-being. The government has introduced Meister Schools—vocational training with strong industry ties and 100% employment rates—to diversify pathways. A shift toward competency-based learning and reduced exam focus is underway, though the system remains heavily focused on university entrance via the CSAT.

**Japan** has a long history of formal schooling, evolving from pre-modern traditions to a modern, highly competitive system. Compulsory education spans grades 1–9, with 98.3% retention to upper secondary (10–12). Public high schools are tuition-free, and private schools account for 55% of upper secondary institutions. The system emphasizes academic rigor, with students preparing for university through private cram schools (*juku*). Over 86% of college-bound students participate in shadow education, and while academic performance in math and science improved post-2011, the system faces severe criticism for academic pressure, mental health issues, bullying (over 600,000 cases in 2019), and limited free time. Inclusive education reforms (1990s–2006) support students with disabilities, but the system remains rigid and under pressure.

**The Netherlands** offers a decentralized, equitable system with compulsory education from age five to 18 or completion of a diploma (VWO, HAVO, or MBO-2). Public, religious, and neutral schools receive equal funding and are overseen by the *Inspectie van het Onderwijs*. The system emphasizes inclusivity, flexibility, and student well-being, with lower bullying rates than international averages. Gifted students may skip grades, and annual assessments allow retaking. Private schools are rare, and the system prioritizes equity and holistic development. Students transition from elementary school to secondary education at age 12 via a *brugklas* (bridge class), which prepares them for the shift. Secondary education includes VMBO (vocational, 4 years), HAVO (general, 5 years), and VWO (academic, 6 years), with pathways tailored to student readiness. Students can move up or down levels based on performance, and a safety net allows advancement if they later demonstrate readiness—ensuring flexibility and academic support. Secondary education is further structured by profiles in culture, economy, nature and health, or nature and technology, with VWO offering atheneum (Latin/Greek) and gymnasium tracks, as well as optional bilingual or advanced programs. VAVO supports adult learners seeking diplomas.  

Post-secondary education in the Netherlands includes MBO (vocational, levels 1–4), HBO (applied science, leading to a Baccalaureus or Ingenieur), and WO (university-level, with BA, BSc, LLB, MA, MSc, and PhD degrees). MBO offers two pathways—school-based or apprenticeship—with training ranging from 20% to over 60% of study time, leading to the same certification. After MBO, students can pursue HBO or enter the job market. HBO is professional-oriented and accessible to HAVO, VWO, or MBO-4 graduates, requiring 240 credits (4 years) for a bachelor’s. WO is research-focused, open only to VWO or HBO graduates, requiring 180 credits (3 years) for a bachelor’s. Both systems follow a three-cycle structure (bachelor’s, master’s, PhD) under the Bologna Process, using ECTS credits (60 credits = one year). Master’s programmes vary in credit requirements (60–180), and PhDs are research-based, awarded only by WO institutions. Admission to WO bachelor’s requires a VWO or HBO propaedeuse; HBO requires HAVO or MBO-4. A quota system and entrance exams apply in some cases. Accreditation by the NVAO ensures quality, with only accredited programmes receiving government funding and issuing legally recognized degrees. The system evolved from early 1800s reforms to modern structures, with clear distinctions between vocational and academic education.

The Dutch education system has a long history rooted in socioeconomic stratification, with distinct school types established by key legislative acts: the 1857 and 1878 acts introduced secular public schools and religious "special schools," leading to the "school struggle" and eventual equal funding in 1917. By the 1960s, a diverse range of school types existed, later streamlined by the 1963 *Mammoetwet*, which introduced four secondary education streams (vwo, havo, mavo, vmbo) and expanded compulsory education to 10 years. The system includes Islamic schools, and by 2004, schools were required to have care teams for student well-being. Holidays follow regional patterns, including summer, autumn, Christmas, spring, and May breaks. A key point of criticism is the use of standardized tests (e.g., Cito) at age 12 to assign students to secondary levels, often misplacing gifted children into lower tracks (30% of gifted children go to VMBO), leading to academic underachievement and higher dropout rates.

**Additional critique of the Dutch system** includes limitations in IQ testing for student placement, early academic separation that may hinder cognitive development, unequal parental involvement, socioeconomic disparities in access to educational advice, and a decline in international performance in reading, maths, and science since 2006—highlighting systemic weaknesses in equity and academic outcomes despite its structural strengths in flexibility and inclusivity.
