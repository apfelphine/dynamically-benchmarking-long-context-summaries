# Education in South Korea and Japan

South Korea’s education system, deeply influenced by Confucian values, has rapidly advanced since 1945 to achieve near-universal literacy and enrollment from early childhood to higher education. It features a structured progression emphasizing foundational skills, science, and technology, underpinning its global developmental success. Intense academic pressure arises around the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), with widespread use of private tutoring (hagwons) that exacerbate social inequalities. Vocational education offers apprenticeships and industry collaboration but faces social stigma despite strong employment outcomes. Higher education largely follows a private, U.S.-style model, confronting student dissatisfaction and labor market mismatches. Efforts to improve student well-being include banning mobile phones and prohibiting corporal punishment, though concerns over creativity and stress persist.

Japan’s education system combines Chinese tradition with Western reforms, providing six years of elementary, three years of lower secondary (compulsory education), and three years of upper secondary education with academic, technical, and vocational tracks. English instruction has been compulsory since 2011, and special needs education promotes inclusiveness. Higher education serves over 2.8 million students through universities and specialized institutions, with prestigious universities recognized globally. Challenges include intense academic pressure leading to psychological stress and bullying, limited free time, and creativity constraints. Government reforms focus on enhancing inclusiveness and student support.

# Education in the Netherlands

The Dutch education system starts with primary schooling emphasizing basic skills and early English, culminating in the Cito Eindtoets test that channels students into three secondary tracks: vocational (VMBO), intermediate (HAVO), or academic (VWO). Students in HAVO and VWO select academic profiles aligned with future university or applied science education. Post-secondary vocational education (MBO) provides practical training, while research universities offer bachelor’s through PhD programs, coordinated under the Bologna Process. Rooted in compulsory education since 1800, it values school choice, equal funding, and quality assurance.

Despite its inclusiveness and flexibility, early tracking risks misclassifying students, increasing dropout rates and underachievement, particularly among lower-income families often steered toward lower-level tracks. Since 2006, Dutch primary students’ international rankings in reading, math, and science have declined, raising concerns about education quality and prompting attention to socio-economic disparities.

# Education in Canada

Canada’s provincially governed education system features regional variation in grade structures, terminology, and curricula. Compulsory education generally starts between ages 5 and 7 and ends at secondary completion around ages 16 to 18, typically Grade 12 except in Quebec, which concludes at Grade 11 followed by mandatory CEGEP. Public education includes constitutionally protected minority-language and religious separate schools, as well as International Baccalaureate (IB), Advanced Placement (AP), and private options. Curricula increasingly incorporate Indigenous and anti-racism studies.

Canada enjoys one of the world’s highest tertiary education completion rates, with a diverse array of universities and vocational colleges. Admission criteria include academic performance and extracurriculars, supported by provincial and federal funding, attracting many international students aided by favorable immigration policies. Current debates focus on issues like grade inflation, pedagogical innovation, student data privacy, and expanding early childhood education.

# Education in Pakistan

Pakistan’s British-modeled education system consists of primary (Years 1–8), secondary (Years 9–12), and tertiary levels but faces critical challenges. Despite literacy improvements to about 60.65%, public education suffers from low spending (~2% of GDP), inadequate infrastructure, poor teacher quality, and stark gender and regional disparities; female literacy averages 42% compared to 67% for males. Education streams include formal, non-formal (vocational and madrasa), and growing distance learning, which revealed digital divides during the COVID-19 pandemic. Low-cost private schools offer some access but often favor boys.

Higher education is expanding, with some universities ranking regionally, and international student mobility increasing. However, socio-political issues like poverty, feudalism, and ideological influences in madrasa education hinder quality and access. Pakistan has produced notable contributors to science and technology despite these challenges.

# Education in the Czech Republic

The Czech Republic mandates nine years of elementary education in two stages, followed by diverse secondary pathways: vocational schools, professional lyceums, and grammar schools, each granting specific qualifications. University admission requires passing the national maturita exam. Inclusion efforts grow through special and practical schools for students with disabilities. Higher education includes public and private universities and state academies; public universities offer tuition-free programs in Czech for first-time students, while English-taught and private programs charge fees (€2,000–€10,000). The academic year runs September to June.

# Education in Bangladesh

Bangladesh’s education spans pre-primary to tertiary levels, including technical, vocational, alternative, non-formal, and religious education sectors. Primary schooling is government-managed; secondary education operates under regional boards up to Grade 12. Recent reforms have discontinued major exams like PEC and JSC. The grading scale uses a 5.0 GPA with a 33% pass mark. The tertiary sector includes over 140 universities, with religious education through government-subsidized Alia madrasahs and private Qawmi madrasahs. NGOs provide non-formal education for vulnerable groups. Government initiatives promote compulsory primary education, gender equity, stipends, and free textbooks.

Literacy has improved substantially (from 51.77% in 2011 to 74.66% in 2022), with education spending rising (~2.2% of GDP in 2007). Challenges remain in teacher absenteeism, poverty-related access, rural-urban divides, rote learning prevalence, weak industry linkage, and limited critical thinking development.

# Education in Poland

Poland’s education, with historical roots back to the 12th century, includes eight years of compulsory primary education followed by four or five years of secondary schooling divided among general, technical, vocational, and special tracks. The curriculum highlights Polish history and early foreign language instruction (English and German). Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Policies like the 2006 "zero tolerance" rule address school violence and support educators.

# Education in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia’s education system, rooted in Islamic tradition, comprises expanding pre-primary (non-mandatory), compulsory six-year primary, and intermediate and secondary phases (3 years each) with general and vocational tracks. English instruction starts in first grade (since 2021), alongside unified Islamic studies curricula. Female education has grown significantly, with women representing about 60% of university students, though female workforce participation remains low (~14.4%). Higher education includes 24 government universities offering diplomas to PhDs, distance education, and focuses on humanities and social sciences.

Major reforms like the King Abdullah Project and Tatweer emphasize teacher training, critical thinking, and vocational skill enhancement. The private/international school sector is growing, serving expatriates, with curricular updates addressing previous gender and intolerance issues.

# Education in New Zealand

New Zealand’s secular, free education system incorporates Māori traditional knowledge, with compulsory schooling from ages 6 to 16. It features primary through secondary levels across state, state-integrated, and private schools, with geographic enrollment zoning. Māori-medium education revitalization since the 1970s has driven improved outcomes. The national curriculum is delivered in English and Māori across eight learning areas, with senior qualifications via the NCEA system and alternatives like Cambridge and IB.

Challenges include declining early math and science skills, achievement gaps for Māori and Pacific Island students, and bullying. Government initiatives promote positive behavior and anti-bullying policies.

# Education in Russia

Russia’s education encompasses preschool, general, vocational, and tertiary levels with near-universal enrollment, though urban preschool availability is limited. General education lasts 11 years with nine years compulsory, including specialized schools. The Unified State Examination standardizes university admission. Special education exists but lacks full integration for severe disabilities. Higher education includes many public and private institutions, consolidated into federal universities since 2006 to improve quality but potentially centralize access. Degree structures are shifting toward Bologna bachelor’s and master’s degrees, but bachelor’s alone often lack labor market value, sustaining traditional postgraduate degrees.

Since 2020, patriotic education policies emphasize national identity, intensified by the Ukraine conflict and censorship concerns. Multilingual education supports minority languages. From 2024, foreign students will need to pass Russian language exams for enrollment.

# Education in Mexico

Mexico’s education system, rooted in pre-Hispanic and colonial Church traditions, is now secular, free, and compulsory from nursery through preparatoria. Intercultural Universities promote indigenous inclusion since 2004. Literacy exceeds 94%, with near-universal child attendance, but rural infrastructure and secondary completion rates (~45%) lag. Junior high and high school offer academic, vocational, and International Baccalaureate tracks. Inclusive education for special needs students is mandated but under-resourced. Higher education follows the Bologna Process, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees; about 23% of young adults hold college degrees.

Centralized governance under the Ministry of Education since 2013 reduced union influence amid reforms. Despite progress, educational outcomes remain below OECD averages due to persistent regional and social inequalities.
