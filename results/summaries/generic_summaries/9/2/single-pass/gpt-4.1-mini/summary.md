# Education in South Korea
South Korea's education system has deep Confucian roots emphasizing meritocracy and social mobility through education, historically shaped by Joseon dynasty village schools and private academies. The Japanese occupation (1905–1945) imposed a centralized, assimilationist education system limiting Korean access beyond elementary levels. Post-liberation, education was reformed under U.S. military governance with democratic ideals emphasizing literacy, decentralization, and universal access. Over time, education centralized under the Ministry of Education, and a 6-4-3-4 structure was established, blending academic and vocational paths. The 1960s-70s emphasized vocational training to support industrialization despite social resistance rooted in Confucian values. South Korea's "education fever" drove rapid literacy growth and higher education access, with about 34% attending tertiary institutions by the late 1980s. Reforms in the 1980s expanded higher education and shifted towards greater fairness. Since 2025, a high school credit system allows more course choice.

Primary education spans grades 1–6, focusing on Korean language, mathematics, moral education, and basic skills, with mobile phone use banned during classes since 2023. Secondary education includes general and special-purpose high schools; over 97% complete high school. Vocational high schools serve about 20%, providing balanced academic and technical training; "Meister Schools" are modern vocational institutes modeled on German systems focusing on skilled trades, helping to reduce stigma and improve employment outcomes despite modest enrollment rates.

Higher education follows a U.S. model with universities and colleges awarding degrees from associate to doctoral levels. University entrance highly depends on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT). Despite social stigmas favoring university education, vocational and industrial universities offer valuable alternatives. English education is widespread, driven by global competitiveness, though public English proficiency was low historically and has improved substantially.

South Korea faces criticism for intense academic pressure, high reliance on private cram schools ("hagwons"), social stigma against vocational tracks, excessive competition, student health issues, and mismatch between education and job markets. Efforts to diversify education and reduce pressure are ongoing.

# Education in the Netherlands
The Dutch education system starts compulsory education at age five (practice starts at four). Schools (public, religious special, and general-special) receive equal government funding, with strict inspection to ensure quality. Students are annually assessed, with grade repetition common and gifted children occasionally allowed to skip years.

Elementary education (ages 4-12) comprises eight grades ("groep 1" to "groep 8") involving foundational skills, including early English education. Transition to secondary education is influenced by the Cito test taken in group 8, guiding placement into vocational (VMBO), general (HAVO), or pre-university (VWO) tracks. 

Secondary education includes:

- **VMBO** (4 years) focuses on vocational and theoretical combinations with several learning paths.
- **HAVO** (5 years) prepares for higher professional education (HBO).
- **VWO** (6 years) prepares for research universities (WO), including classical languages in gymnasium variants.

Students can move up/down between tracks through orientation years. MBO (vocational colleges), HBO (universities of applied sciences), and WO (research universities) form post-secondary education, aligned with the Bologna Process and ECTS credit system. MBO has four levels, including apprenticeships and school-based paths. HBO emphasizes professional training; WO focuses on academic education with bachelor’s, master’s, and PhD degrees. Quality assurance is maintained via accreditation (NVAO).

The Dutch system has been criticized for early tracking decisions (around age 12), which may misplace gifted students and cause social inequalities. Recent international assessments indicate declining educational performance.

# Education in Russia
Russia’s education system comprises general education (preschool to secondary) and professional education (vocational, higher, postgraduate). Preschool is widely available, though shortages and regional disparities exist. General education is compulsory for nine years (elementary, middle, senior segments), with many schools offering full eleven-year programs. Unified State Examinations (USE) standardize university admissions since 2007.

Vocational training is a common alternative post-basic education, lasting up to three years and providing certificates equivalent to secondary education, though conscription timing affects male students.

Higher education historically followed a single-stage specialist diploma (5-6 years), transitioning to Bologna Process standards with bachelor’s and master’s degrees since 2007. Russian postgraduate degrees include Candidate of Sciences (similar to PhD) and Doctor of Sciences (habilitation equivalent). 

Special education is provided in segregated and inclusive forms for physical and mental disabilities but faces resource and integration challenges.

Recent laws (e.g., on outreach activity) and government efforts have promoted patriotic education and introduced controversial sciences and ideology curricula. Non-Russian language schools have declined, and restrictions on Russian language proficiency for foreign students have been enacted.

# Education in Japan
Japanese education began in the 6th century influenced by Chinese and Buddhist learning, evolving through samurai and merchant classes. The Meiji Restoration modernized education modeled after Western systems, establishing compulsory education and universities like Tokyo University.

Post-WWII reforms introduced democracy and American-style schooling: six years elementary, three years lower secondary, three years upper secondary, and four years university. Re-centralization occurred in the 1950s with curriculum standardization and moral education.

Schooling starts in April; primary and secondary levels follow a structured curriculum with compulsory English starting elementary level since 2011. Upper secondary is non-compulsory but highly attended, with varied academic and vocational tracks. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, technical and specialist colleges, with elite universities like Tokyo and Kyoto prominent.

Extracurricular “shadow education” (juku, mogi shiken) is widespread to prepare for competitive university entrance exams, resulting in heavy pressure on students, linked to stress and mental health concerns. Bullying remains a societal challenge.

Special needs education has advanced since the 1990s, promoting inclusion and rights for disabled children. International schools predominantly teach in English but are outside compulsory education regulations.

# Education in Pakistan
Pakistan’s education system mirrors British influence and comprises pre-school, primary (grades 1-5), middle (6-8), secondary (9-12), and tertiary levels. Primary education features diverse subjects but suffers from low attendance (about 66%) and quality issues. Secondary education culminates in matriculation exams; post-secondary includes intermediate and university programs with professional tracks.

Tertiary education offers bachelor’s (two or four years), master’s, and doctoral degrees. Teacher education faces challenges in quality and resources. There is significant gender disparity, with females underrepresented in enrollment and literacy, although progress has been made. Societal norms, safety, and infrastructure are barriers to female education.

Vocational and technical education (TVET) is expanding, aiming to meet labor market needs. Distance learning grew during COVID-19, though internet access limits its reach.

Religious education (madrassas) plays a crucial role, with “Qawmi” (privately funded) and “Alia” (government-subsidized) systems. Madrasas provide free education and shelter but are criticized for promoting rote learning and inverse academic quality.

The literacy rate is around 60.65% overall, with disparities across provinces and gender.

Pakistan has produced notable scientists and scholars despite educational challenges. Public expenditure on education is low (~2% of GDP), and infrastructural deficiencies persist.

# Education in Mexico
Mexico’s basic education is divided into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and high school (10–12). Primary education emphasizes bilingual programs; junior high offers more specialized subjects and vocational tracks.

High school (preparatoria) prepares students for university or vocational careers and includes specialization streams. Public and private institutions co-exist, with vocational programs supporting workforce readiness.

Educational integration mandates inclusive education for students with special needs, though challenges in collaboration, accessibility, and training remain.

Education quality concerns relate to infrastructure deficits and teacher union conflicts, particularly over reforms against entrenched unions like SNTE.

Higher education offers bachelor’s (Licenciatura), master’s (Maestría), and doctoral (Doctorado) degrees, aligned with the Bologna Process.

Intercultural Universities were created to improve access for indigenous populations.

Mexico hosts over 150 international schools providing English-medium curricula.

# Education in Poland
Poland’s compulsory education begins at age 6 or 7 with an eight-year primary school followed by three or four years of secondary education. A 2017 reform extended primary schooling to eight years and abolished the three-year middle school.

Secondary education includes general secondary schools (liceum), technical schools (technikum), and vocational schools, preparing students for “Matura” exams granting university access.

Higher education follows the Bologna Process with three-year bachelor’s and two-year master’s programs; some long-cycle degrees exist. A numeric grading system from 2 (fail) to 5 (excellent) is used.

Foreign languages are emphasized, with English and German most common.

Historically, Poland has a rich educational tradition, including Europe’s oldest universities and early compulsory education laws.

# Education in New Zealand
New Zealand’s education is free for citizens and residents from age 5 to 19. Early childhood education includes kindergartens and Māori language immersion programs. Primary education is Years 1-8, with secondary education spanning Years 9-13. Schools may be state, state-integrated (with special religious/cultural character), or private.

The national curriculum covers eight learning areas, and most secondary students pursue the National Certificate of Educational Achievement (NCEA).

Funding is allocated per student with additional support for low-decile (poor) schools. Student loans and allowances assist with tertiary education costs.

New Zealand faces challenges in mathematics and science achievement and has significant issues with bullying. Māori language revitalization and education have become a priority, with dedicated immersion schools.

# Education in the Czech Republic
Compulsory education lasts nine years across two stages of elementary school. The first stage covers grades 1–5 with one teacher per class; the second stage (grades 6–9) introduces specialized subject teachers. Students may continue education at eight- or six-year grammar schools.

Secondary education includes vocational, professional, lyceum, and grammar schools, typically ending with the “Maturita” exam required for university admission.

Tertiary education includes public and private universities and higher professional schools. University degrees include bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral with various specialized academic titles. The school year runs from September to June, with traditional holidays.

# Education in Saudi Arabia
Saudi education originates from Islamic religious schools (kuttabs). Modern formal education began in the 20th century, expanding with public schools, including girls' education starting in 1964. Education emphasizes religious studies alongside sciences and languages.

Education is free, managed primarily by the Ministry of Education with extensive plans for expansion and quality improvement. Primary (6 years), intermediate (3 years), and secondary (3 years) schooling include general and specialized science tracks.

Higher education has grown rapidly since 1957’s first university, with female enrollment surpassing males since 2015. Technical and vocational education is provided, as well as special education with increasing inclusion efforts.

Gifted education programs have developed since 1969, focusing on enrichment and talent development.

Private and international schools coexist alongside public schools, regulated by the Ministry.

The education system faces criticism for rote learning, religious intolerance in curricula, lack of innovation, and gender disparities, although reforms and scholarship programs aim for modernization and inclusion.

# Education in Canada
Education in Canada is provincially managed with varying structures but typically begins with kindergarten (ages 4–5) and continues through Grade 12 (age 17–18). Post-secondary options include universities and vocational colleges.

Approximately 51% of Canadians have tertiary education, with significant investment in education (~5.2% GDP). Universities offer bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, while vocational institutions provide certificates and diplomas with pathways to university transfer.

Canada’s system recognizes publicly funded religious and language-based schools under constitutional provisions. French and English-language education is widely available.

Private schools enroll about 6% of students, mostly in Quebec, usually outperforming public schools academically, often due to higher expectations.

Challenges include transparency in equity data collection, provincial variation, and maintaining academic standards.

# Education in Bangladesh
Bangladesh’s education system includes pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (9–12), and tertiary levels, with instruction in Bengali or English. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board manages standardized tests and curricula. Secondary education splits into Arts, Commerce, and Science streams from class 9 onward.

Madrasas provide religious education alongside general education with two main types: government-subsidized Alia and private Qawmi madrasas. Refugee education and NGO-run non-formal schools address marginalized groups.

Tertiary education comprises public, private, and international universities. Technical and vocational education is overseen by the Technical Education Board.

Despite increasing enrollment and literacy (approx. 74.66% in 2022), challenges include gender disparity especially in rural areas, low quality of education, teacher shortages, infrastructure deficits, and outdated curricula. Education expenditure hovers around 2% of GDP.

Programs focus on inclusion, gender parity improvements, and combating poverty-linked educational barriers. Grading uses GPA with a maximum of 5.0. Religious education integrates multiple faiths but often suffers from insufficient non-Muslim teacher presence.

# Education in Mexico
Mexico’s education system features three basic levels: primary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and high school (10–12), with a mix of academic and vocational tracks. Special education promotes inclusive classrooms but faces challenges in collaboration and accessibility.

Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, offering Licenciatura (bachelor’s), Maestría (master’s), and Doctorado (doctorate) degrees. Intercultural Universities attempt to increase indigenous enrollment.

Public education expanded significantly post-revolution, yet quality and infrastructure concerns persist, exacerbated by teacher union conflicts and political issues.

Mexico hosts numerous international schools with English-medium instruction.

# Education in Poland
Poland provides compulsory education starting at age 6 or 7 with eight years of primary school followed by secondary education through general (liceum), technical (technikum), or vocational schools. The 2017 reform shifted from a three-tier to an eight-year primary plus four-year secondary structure.

Higher education follows Bologna Process cycles of bachelor’s and master’s, with doctoral studies thereafter. University grading is numerical from 2 (fail) to 5 (excellent). Foreign language study, mainly English and German, is emphasized.

Poland has a rich educational history dating back to the 12th century and one of the oldest European universities.

# Education in New Zealand
New Zealand offers free education from age 5 to 19 across early childhood, primary (Years 1–8), and secondary (Years 9–13). Its national curriculum covers eight learning areas with qualifications mainly through NCEA.

Schools are state-funded, state-integrated (with special religious or cultural character), or private. Funding varies by socio-economic decile. Māori language education has been revitalized through immersion schools and programs.

New Zealand faces challenges in math and science achievement and bullying. The school year runs January–December with four terms. Home-schooling is permitted under strict conditions.

# Education in the Czech Republic
The Czech education system comprises a nine-year elementary school divided into two stages, followed by various types of secondary education:

- Vocational and professional schools offering certificates and maturita exams for university entrance.
- Grammar schools (general and specialized).
- Maturita is the standard secondary leaving qualification.

Tertiary education includes public and private universities with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. The academic year runs September to June with semester exams and several holidays.

# Education in Saudi Arabia
Saudi Arabia’s education originated from Islamic religious schools and modernized through government reforms starting in the 20th century. Education is free and managed by the Ministry of Education, encompassing pre-primary, primary (6 years), intermediate (3 years), and secondary (3 years) education, with expanding female education access.

Higher education includes over 24 government universities offering bachelor’s through doctoral degrees. Female university enrollment surpasses males, though women’s labor participation remains limited.

Gifted and special education programs have been developed progressively. Private and international schools operate under Ministry supervision.

The system emphasizes Islamic studies heavily, with recent reforms aiming to move towards critical thinking and secular subjects. Criticisms include rote learning, religious intolerance in textbooks, lack of marketable skills, and gender disparities, though extensive scholarship and reform initiatives are underway.

# Education in Canada
Canada’s education is provincially governed, generally beginning with kindergarten and extending through Grade 12, followed by post-secondary education at universities and vocational institutions. Approximately half of Canadians hold tertiary qualifications.

University admissions rely on high school performance and extra-curricular achievements, with institutions offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral programs. Vocational colleges provide diplomas and certificates in skilled trades and technical fields.

Canada funds religious and language-based schools constitutionally. Private schools enroll around 6% of students, usually outperforming public counterparts.

Challenges include data transparency in equity policies, provincial curricular variations, and balancing academic rigor with inclusivity.

# Education in Bangladesh
Bangladesh’s education spans pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (9–12), and tertiary levels, with instruction in Bengali or English. The government manages curricula and standardized assessments but faces quality issues, teacher shortages, and infrastructure challenges.

The system includes general education, madrasas (Islamic religious schools), and non-formal education addressing vulnerable populations, including refugees.

Literacy has increased to approximately 74.66% (2022), with ongoing efforts to improve gender parity and access. Grading uses a 5.0 GPA scale.

Challenges remain in equitable teacher training, poverty-linked access barriers, curriculum modernisation, and educational funding (~2% of GDP). International and private schools offer alternative curricula.
