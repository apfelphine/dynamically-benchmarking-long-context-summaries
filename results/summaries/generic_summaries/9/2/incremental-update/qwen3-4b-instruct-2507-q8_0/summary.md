# **Final Working Summary**

The comparative analysis of education systems in South Korea, the Netherlands, Russia, Japan, Pakistan, Mexico, Poland, New Zealand, the Czech Republic, Saudi Arabia, Canada, and **Bangladesh** reveals distinct models shaped by historical, cultural, and socioeconomic forces.

**South Korea** maintains a meritocratic, high-pressure system excelling in STEM and economic output but struggling with student well-being and vocational stigma.  
**The Netherlands** emphasizes flexibility, inclusivity, and student well-being through early tracking and clear post-secondary pathways, though early placement disparities and declining international performance remain concerns.  
**Russia** operates a centralized, state-driven system with high enrollment and standardized outcomes, yet faces access, equity, and linguistic diversity challenges—particularly for indigenous and marginalized groups.  
**Japan** promotes high academic achievement through rigorous curricula and widespread private tutoring (*juku*), but at the cost of student mental health and social equity.  
**Pakistan** suffers from low enrollment, gender disparities, and fragmented curricula, with religious institutions (madrassas) playing a strong role. Women face significant barriers, including lower literacy (42% vs. 67% for men), underrepresentation in STEM, and systemic discrimination. Transgender individuals—especially hijras—face severe marginalization, though the 2018 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act offers legal protection. Teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and misalignment with workforce needs undermine quality. Distance learning platforms (e.g., Teleschool, AIOU) improve access, especially for rural and female students, but are constrained by internet access. Education spending remains critically low at ~2% of GDP, with a proposed 7% target lacking sustainable funding. Regional disparities are stark: national literacy is 60.65% (second lowest in South Asia), with Gilgit-Baltistan at just 3% and Balochistan reporting low mean years of schooling.  

**Mexico** features a structured three-tier basic education system that increasingly integrates students with special needs. Only 45% complete secondary school, and just 25% progress to higher education, highlighting persistent barriers. Postgraduate studies follow the Bologna Process, with intercultural universities aiming to increase indigenous enrollment (despite 10% of the population), though enrollment remains low. Indigenous education remains constrained by structural and cultural barriers. Pre-Hispanic and colonial roots include elite schools (calmecac, cuicacalli) and Catholic friar-led instruction. Post-independence reforms under Benito Juárez established secular, free, and compulsory public education, while Porfirian modernization failed to eradicate illiteracy or produce upward mobility. Since the 1920s, education has evolved through political shifts: Vasconcelos promoted indigenous integration, Cárdenas introduced socialist education, and later reforms emphasized secularism and teacher training. Literacy rose from 83% (1980) to 94.86% (2018), though disparities persist. UNAM ranks 103rd globally; Monterrey Institute ranks 158th.  

**Poland** offers a compulsory 6-year primary education (now 8 years post-2017), followed by upper secondary education including general (liceum), vocational (technikum), and profiled schools. Tertiary education follows the Bologna model (3-year bachelor’s, 2-year master’s), with a 2.0–5.0 grading scale and frequent retakes. Foreign language instruction is widespread (English 67.9%, German 33.3%). Historically rooted in the Jagiellonian University (1364), compulsory education reduced illiteracy to 1.2% by 1978. The 1999 reform improved OECD rankings; the 2017 reform introduced a nationalist, elite-focused history curriculum that downplays lower-class roles and removes "communism" from 19th-century teaching. A 2006 "zero tolerance" policy elevated teacher status and imposed penalties for failing to report violence.  

**New Zealand** has a free, compulsory system from age 5 to 19 (Years 1–13), with 85% of students in state schools. The national curriculum (NZC/TMoA) includes eight learning areas, with health education offering optional sexuality content. School types include state, state-integrated (10%), and private (4%) schools. Students pursue NCEA, CIE, or IB. Enrolment schemes face criticism for inequity. Māori and Pacific students lag behind Pākehā in achievement. Bullying—especially cyberbullying—is prevalent, and despite a $60 million investment in anti-bullying programs, results remain unsatisfactory. Educational standards have declined in TIMSS, particularly in math and numeracy. Integrated technical training and teacher salaries are centrally managed.  

**The Czech Republic** features a two-stage elementary system (Grades 1–5 and 6–9), with entrance tests (CERMAT) required for secondary school (12.3% failed in 2025). Secondary education includes vocational, professional, lyceum, and grammar school tracks. The *maturita* exam is mandatory for university entry. Public universities (e.g., Charles, Palacký) offer free Czech-language study for under-26s; private institutions charge €2,000–10,000 annually. Degrees range from 3-year bachelor’s to 6-year doctorates. Public universities are generally higher quality. Doctorate titles (PhDr., JUDr., etc.) require rigorous thesis exams and master’s degrees.  

**Saudi Arabia** is rooted in Islamic teachings, with religious studies receiving 9 weekly periods in primary school. The Ministry of Education oversees the system, with key goals including gender equality, teacher development, and modernization. Primary education is compulsory (6 years), with English introduced from Grade 1. Female enrollment in higher education reached 36.1% (vs. 24.7% for males), surpassing men in undergraduate enrollment. Post-secondary education includes 24 government and private institutions, with programs lasting 4–6 years. A government scholarship program supports study abroad. Girls' education began in 1960, with women now comprising 60% of university students. Gifted education has been formalized since 1969, with programs like the Summer Enrichment Program (SEP) serving over 6,000 students annually. Special education is growing through mainstreaming, though teacher shortages and negative school cultures remain barriers. Private education plays a significant role, especially in technical fields. International schools (203 as of 2015) offer foreign curricula under government oversight. Criticisms include rote learning, lack of innovation, and limited global competitiveness.  

**Canada** features provincial responsibility, with compulsory schooling from age 5–7 to 16 (or 18 in some provinces). Kindergarten is universal, with full-day programs common. Secondary education ends at Grade 12 (except Quebec). Post-secondary education includes universities (offering degrees) and vocational institutions (diplomas, certificates, apprenticeships). Canadian universities are globally recognized, with strong research and liberal arts programs. Vocational training is accessible via community colleges and technical schools. Private schools (especially in Quebec) and religious institutions (e.g., Ontario’s Catholic system) exist, with debates over equity and access.  

**Bangladesh** has a formally structured system across primary, secondary, tertiary, technical/vocational, and alternative (including international and madrasah) levels. Primary education is managed by the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education (MOPME) and the National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB), overseeing textbook distribution (over 34 million in 2022). Secondary education is administered by ten regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education (BISEs), conducting SSC (10th) and HSC (12th) exams. Government schools are managed by headmasters; non-government schools by SMCs or GBs. Tertiary education is regulated by the University Grants Commission (UGC), including 43 public, 103 private, and 2 international institutions. Public universities are government-subsidized and admit via competitive exams; private universities offer more foreign student and teacher opportunities but are restricted in postgraduate degrees (except master’s). All private medical colleges are affiliated with public universities. Technical and vocational education is administered by DTE and BTEB, offering diplomas (1 month to 4 years) leading to bachelor’s degrees. Alternative education includes international schools (Cambridge, IB) and madrasahs—Qawmi (private, small enrollment) and Alia (government-subsidized, larger enrollment), which offer religious education alongside general studies. Refugee education, especially for Rohingya children, remains a major challenge. Non-formal education (e.g., BRAC) serves dropouts but lacks continuity into secondary education.  

**Key challenges in Bangladesh**:  
- Public expenditure on education ranges from **0.94% to 2.2% of GDP**, significantly below global benchmarks.  
- **High teacher absenteeism (15.5%)** and inadequate deployment undermine instructional quality.  
- **Rising education costs** and **rural school dropouts** contribute to poor attendance and equity gaps.  
- While **gender parity is achieved in primary and secondary education**, disparities persist in rural areas.  
- Literacy has improved dramatically—from **51.77% (2011) to 74.66% (2022)**—reflecting progress in access and basic learning.  
- Systemic issues remain, including **outdated curricula**, **overreliance on rote learning**, **lack of industry-aligned education**, and **insufficient access for marginalized groups**.  
- A shift toward **outcome-based education** is still in development, with limited implementation of reforms.  

This comprehensive overview underscores the diverse pathways, strengths, and persistent challenges across the studied education systems, highlighting the interplay of culture, policy, equity, and economic context in shaping national outcomes.
