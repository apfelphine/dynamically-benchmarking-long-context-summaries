### Comparative Overview: Global Educational Systems

**Western Europe, Oceania, and Asia**
*   **Western/North Atlantic:** The **Netherlands** operates under a centralized framework criticized for income-linked disparities and early tracking. **Poland** achieved literacy gains but faces nationalism-related debates; the **Czech Republic** utilizes a nine-grade system with unique degree titles. **New Zealand** relies on the NCEA framework, balancing high state funding against significant equity gaps between Pākehā and Māori/Pacific students, alongside bullying issues. **Russia** maintains centralized control via Unified State Examinations, navigating linguistic diversity and patriotic education reforms. **Japan** features compulsory education ending at grade nine, pervasive shadow education (*juku*), and high suicide rates, though shifting toward inclusivity. **Saudi Arabia** prioritizes reducing illiteracy and instilling national values, with female enrollment now exceeding males; recent reforms focus on STEM gifted education, English integration, and infrastructure under the 2030 Vision.
*   **Eastern/Middle East & Asia:** **Japan** and **Saudi Arabia** exemplify targeted investments in cultural and technological areas.

**South Asia, North America, and the Caribbean**
*   **Canada:** Features a decentralized system where provinces manage compulsory education (1871–1943) while the federal government funds specific sectors like Indigenous education. With education spending at 5.2% of GDP, the system guarantees minority language rights under the *Canadian Charter*. Admission for competitive programs prioritizes Grades 11–12 GPAs, extracurriculars, and personal statements. The structure varies by province (e.g., Quebec treats Grade 12 as tertiary), and funding models for private/religious schools differ significantly (e.g., Ontario fully funds Catholic schools; Alberta funds non-religious charters). Canada is a global leader in higher education, hosting top-tier institutions offering degrees, vocational certificates, and apprenticeships.
*   **South Asia & Mexico:** **Pakistan** struggles with low literacy (60.65%), underfunding, and unsafe infrastructure despite notable alumni and recent transgender rights reforms. **Mexico** has seen enrollment surges and high literacy rates through intercultural universities for indigenous populations, though it faces union dominance and accessibility hurdles.
*   **Bangladesh:** The system is divided into pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels without a middle school tier. It is administered by three primary boards: the **Standard Board** (covering Pre-Primary to postgraduate degrees including MBBS and LLB), the **Qawmi Education Board** (religious education from Tahfeez to Fazilat), and the **Alia Education Board** (Islamic studies and vocational training). Students study in English or Bengali mediums with mandatory subjects in both. The curriculum follows a rigid national model (NCTB) with specialized subject selection from Class 9 (Arts, Commerce, Science). Beyond standard academics, vocational and artisan courses exist at the secondary level, while higher education has expanded to include engineering, commerce, and medical fields. Efforts continue to expand primary education in rural char areas.

**Structural and Institutional Details**
*   **Compulsory Education & Levels:** Mandatory schooling typically begins at ages 5–7 and extends until ages 16–18 or diploma completion. Secondary education concludes at Grade 12 in most provinces, whereas Quebec treats Grade 12 as tertiary, allowing students to continue up to ages 19–21 via alternative learning.
*   **Global Trends:** The reviewed systems highlight a tension between centralized control and regional autonomy, alongside persistent challenges in equity, funding, and infrastructure. While nations like Japan and Saudi Arabia invest in specific cultural or technological areas, others like Pakistan, Mexico, and Bangladesh grapple with systemic deficits in literacy, rigid curricula, or access. Reforms across Poland, New Zealand, and the Czech Republic aim to balance standardization with inclusivity, while Canada navigates constitutional protections for language and religion amidst debates over academic rigor and private education accessibility.

**Bangladesh: Specific Administrative, Curriculum, and Alternative Structures**
*   **Administrative Framework:**
    *   **Primary:** Managed by the **Ministry of Primary and Mass Education (MOPME)** and implemented by the **Directorate of Primary Education (DPE)**, handling teacher recruitment, training, textbook distribution, and infrastructure. The **National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB)** develops the curriculum. Historically, PEC (5th grade) and JSC (8th grade) were conducted by the DPE, but these examinations were discontinued starting in 2023.
    *   **Secondary:** Governed by the **Directorate of Secondary and Higher Education (DSHE)** and administered through **10 regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education (BISE)**. The BISE boards conduct SSC (10th grade) and HSC (12th grade). Management is decentralized for non-government schools (via School Management Committees and PTAs) while government schools remain centrally managed.
    *   **Tertiary:** Regulated by the **University Grants Commission (UGC)**, overseeing public, private, and international universities. The system includes 43 subsidized public universities, 103 self-funded private universities, and 2 international universities. Universities operate autonomously, though private medical colleges must affiliate with public institutions and currently cannot offer postgraduate degrees.
    *   **Technical/Vocational:** Controlled by the **Technical Education Board** and implemented by the **Directorate of Technical Education (DTE)**. Pathways include diplomas (1 month to 4 years) leading to Bachelor's degrees in Engineering & Technology, as well as pathways to Chartered Accountancy (CA) via the ICAB.
*   **Alternative Education Systems:**
    *   **Madrasahs:** Bangladesh operates two types of madrasahs. **Qawmi** institutions are privately funded, accounting for 1.9% of primary and 2.2% of secondary enrollment. **Alia** institutions are government-subsidized (covering 80% of salaries), representing 8.4% of primary and 19% of secondary enrollment, offering Arabic alongside general education. Students can progress from "Alim" to "Fazil" (religious) and then to "Kamil" (general education/university level).
    *   **Refugee Education:** Approximately 145,000 Rohingya children are educated via UNICEF-run centers (1,600 locations). A transition is underway from temporary centers to formal schools using the Myanmar curriculum.
    *   **Non-Formal Education:** Primarily NGO-run (e.g., BRAC), focusing on dropouts and vulnerable groups. These schools often operate informally with varying management styles and teacher recruitment processes.
*   **Grading and Assessment:**
    *   **Passing Criteria:** Grades of 33% or higher are considered passing.
    *   **Calculation:** The system uses a Grade Point Average (GPA) out of 5.0, calculated as $(TGP + OGP) / N$ (Total Grade Points + Optional Grade Points) / Total Subjects.
    *   **Optional Subjects:** In secondary/higher secondary levels, a fourth optional subject can boost the GPA (GP - 2), allowing students to achieve a 5.0 GPA even if individual subject marks are below 80%.
    *   **Letter Grades:** Ranging from A+ (100-80%) to F (0-32%).
*   **Religious Education:**
    *   The national curriculum includes Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Christianity.
    *   **Challenges:** Non-Muslim schools often lack teachers for minority religions, forcing students to learn privately.
    *   **Integration:** Islamic history and Hindu stories are taught to all students regardless of belief.

**Current Issues and Improvements**
*   **Government Initiatives:** Efforts include compulsory primary education, free schooling for girls up to class 10, stipends, and free distribution of curriculum books.
*   **Challenges:**
    *   **Historical:** Previously an English-medium, upper-class system modeled after Britain.
    *   **Pedagogy:** Reliance on rote-learning and passivity rather than critical thinking.
    *   **Curriculum Gap:** Lack of industry-oriented, Outcome-Based Education (OBE) blended systems, particularly in engineering.
*   **Recent Progress and Persistent Deficits:** Despite a significant literacy rate increase from 51.77% (2011) to 74.66% (2022), the sector faces severe structural hurdles. Key issues include low public expenditure (hovering near 2% of GDP), insufficient college and university seats, and rising costs that exclude many students. The system suffers from poor human resource management, evidenced by a 15.5% teacher absence rate, alongside high drop-out and repetition rates, particularly in rural areas. While gender parity has been largely achieved in primary and secondary levels, rural households still face gender discrimination, and overall performance remains low due to factors like poverty and inadequate school contact time.
