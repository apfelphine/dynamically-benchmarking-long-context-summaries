# Education in East Asia

South Korea’s highly centralized education system, rooted in Confucian meritocracy, offers near-universal secondary education with diverse academic and vocational tracks, including integrated models like Meister Schools. Higher education is mostly private and competitive, relying heavily on the CSAT exam for elite university admissions. English education is deeply embedded, supported by private tutoring. However, intense academic pressure leads to societal stress and a low alignment between university majors and employment.

Japan mandates six years of elementary and three years of junior high education with multiple non-compulsory upper secondary tracks. Despite reforms like "Yutori education" aimed at reducing exam stress, academic pressure remains high alongside issues like bullying. English has been compulsory since 2011, and the system emphasizes inclusive education for students with special needs. Russia features a centralized system aligned with the Bologna Process, yet faces challenges like preschool disparities, restricted academic freedom, and patriotic education debates, while preserving minority languages remains a priority.

# Education in Europe

The Netherlands enforces compulsory schooling from age five with early academic and vocational tracking into streams supporting labor market entry or higher education. Its higher education system comprises professional (HBO) and research (WO) institutions with strong quality assurance. Challenges include socioeconomic disparities linked to early tracking and declines in literacy and STEM. Poland’s education mandates schooling from age six to eighteen with primary and various secondary tracks culminating in the Matura exam for university access. Reforms emphasize Polish and Eurocentric history and anti-bullying efforts. The Czech Republic’s nine-grade elementary education precedes entrance exams for diversified secondary schools, ending with the maturita exam; tertiary education is affordable for public university students and includes bachelor’s through doctoral levels.

# Education in Oceania

New Zealand integrates Māori traditions with European models through compulsory, secular education from ages 5 to 19. Māori-language immersion schools outperform others, but the system faces disparities affecting Māori and Pacific students, rising tertiary costs, STEM shortages, and high youth bullying. Despite implementing the Positive Behaviour for Learning initiative and allocating significant funding, bullying remains a persistent challenge, prompting calls for a unified national policy.

# Education in the Americas

Mexico provides free, secular, centralized education through primary, junior high, and high school levels with general and vocational tracks. Improvements in attendance and literacy are notable, supported by nationwide textbooks and intercultural universities catering to indigenous populations. However, secondary completion lags and student performance trails OECD averages. Reforms aim to reduce union influence and enhance quality. Canada’s provincially governed system spans early childhood to post-secondary, featuring diverse curricula including International Baccalaureate, advanced placement, and vocational certifications. Discussions focus on balancing academic standards, equity, and critical thinking.

# Education in South Asia and the Middle East

Pakistan’s education system is fragmented across formal, non-formal, informal, and madrasa sectors, hindered by low funding (~2% GDP), outdated curricula, and insufficient teacher training. Marked gender and regional disparities persist with female and rural literacy rates notably low despite expanding distance learning. Sustained, politically insulated reforms are necessary for improving equity and quality. Bangladesh covers pre-primary to tertiary education with general, technical, religious, and international streams. Despite compulsory primary education and improved literacy with gender parity, challenges include rote learning, limited critical thinking, teacher shortages, and rural attendance issues.

Saudi Arabia’s system comprises public, private, and international schools under gender segregation, with free compulsory education in general, technical, and vocational streams. Recent reforms aligned with Vision 2030 focus on modernizing curricula, removing extremist content, improving teacher training, and fostering creativity. Higher education hosts 24 government universities with women constituting about 60% of students. Gifted and special education services aim to foster STEM skills and inclusion despite resource constraints.

# Cross-Cutting Themes

Across regions, common patterns include centralized governance with diverse levels of decentralization, early academic and vocational tracking, emphasis on inclusion and minority language preservation, and efforts to align education with labor market needs. Persistent challenges involve socioeconomic disparities, academic pressure, mental health concerns, bullying, and mismatches between education outputs and employment, which countries address through targeted reforms and policy innovation.
