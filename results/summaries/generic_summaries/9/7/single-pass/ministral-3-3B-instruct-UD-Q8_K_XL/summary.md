### **Education in Saudi Arabia**

#### **Historical Background**
Education in Saudi Arabia traces back to Islamic teachings, emphasizing knowledge and literacy. The first schools, *Kuttabs*, were established by wealthy Muslims and later by the Ottomans, often linked to mosques. Islamic education dominated until the 17th century, when education became more formalized. In 1926, King Abdulaziz bin Abdul Rahman Al Saud established the first Department of Education, expanding schools and student enrollment. By 1957, the King Saud University was founded, marking the first university in Saudi Arabia.

#### **Education System Structure**
Education in Saudi Arabia is free at all levels and governed by the Ministry of Education and the Technical and Vocational Training Corporation (TVTC). The system aims to reduce illiteracy and integrate Islamic values into education. Religious studies are a core component, with an average of nine periods per week at the primary level, compared to 23 periods for core subjects like mathematics and science. The education system has faced criticism for rote learning, limited scientific instruction, and cultural theology influencing women’s education.

#### **Education Management and Funding**
Public spending on education has increased significantly, reaching 5.1% of GDP in 2006. The Ministry of Education’s 10-year plan (2025–2035) focuses on improving teacher quality, expanding infrastructure, and integrating technology. The system includes pre-primary education, primary, intermediate, secondary, and post-secondary education, with a strong emphasis on vocational and technical training.

#### **Special Education and Inclusivity**
Students with disabilities are educated in special institutions or mainstreamed with support. Special education teachers receive additional training and are paid 30% more than mainstream teachers. The system has improved but still faces challenges in teacher training and resource allocation.

#### **International and Private Education**
Saudi Arabia hosts numerous international schools catering to expatriate communities, offering curricula from various countries. Private education is supported by the government, with schools receiving subsidies and qualified directors. The system has seen reforms to address infrastructure issues, though some private schools remain in rented spaces.

#### **Literacy and Challenges**
The illiteracy rate among the Saudi population was 13.7% in 2007, with significant regional disparities. Despite progress, the education system has been criticized for its focus on rote learning, limited critical thinking, and cultural influences that may stifle innovation and creativity.

### **Education in Bangladesh**

#### **Historical and Structural Overview**
Bangladesh’s education system is divided into three main levels: pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB) develops textbooks and administers standardized tests in English or Bengali. The system is compulsory up to the 12th grade, with a focus on arts, sciences, and vocational training. Madrasah education, which emphasizes Islamic studies, is also prominent, particularly in rural areas.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
The curriculum includes mandatory subjects like Bengali, English, mathematics, and science, with additional electives in arts, commerce, and sciences. Students must complete 12 years of education, with standardized exams at the 8th, 10th, and 12th grades. The system has faced criticism for its reliance on rote learning and limited critical thinking, though recent reforms aim to improve quality and accessibility.

#### **Vocational and Alternative Education**
Vocational education is provided through technical and vocational training institutions, offering courses in engineering, agriculture, and healthcare. Madrasah education, which focuses on Islamic studies, is widely available and often free, attracting many students. Alternative education includes international schools and non-formal education programs run by NGOs, catering to vulnerable groups.

#### **Literacy and Gender Disparities**
Bangladesh’s literacy rate improved significantly from 51.77% in 2011 to 74.66% in 2022. However, gender disparities persist, with women’s literacy rates lagging behind men’s. The education system has made progress in reducing gender gaps, but challenges remain in ensuring equal access and quality education for all.

#### **Criticisms and Reforms**
Critics argue that the education system is overly rigid, with a focus on rote learning and limited emphasis on critical thinking and innovation. Recent reforms aim to address these issues, including the introduction of Outcome-Based Education (OBE) and improvements in teacher training and curriculum development.

### **Education in New Zealand**

#### **Historical Development**
New Zealand’s education system traces its roots to Māori wānanga, which taught traditional knowledge, and later to European-style schooling introduced by missionaries. The system was formalized in the 1850s, with compulsory education introduced in 1878. The system is secular, free, and compulsory from the age of 5 to 16, with coeducation becoming more common post-WWII.

#### **School Structure and Curriculum**
The system includes early childhood education, primary (years 1–8), intermediate (years 7–8), and secondary (years 9–13) schools. The curriculum is based on the New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa for Māori-medium schools. Students are assessed annually, and the system emphasizes literacy, numeracy, and critical thinking. Special schools and alternative education options cater to students with disabilities or unique learning needs.

#### **Higher Education and Funding**
Higher education in New Zealand includes universities, polytechnics, and colleges, with funding provided by the government. The system is regulated by the Ministry of Education, which allocates funding based on socio-economic decile ratings. Tertiary education is free for New Zealand citizens and permanent residents, with students relying on government loans and allowances.

#### **Criticisms and Reforms**
Criticisms include concerns about academic standards, particularly in mathematics and science, and the impact of standardized testing on student performance. Recent reforms aim to improve teacher training, curriculum development, and student well-being, though challenges remain in addressing inequality and ensuring equitable access to education.

### **Education in Canada**

#### **Historical and Structural Overview**
Canada’s education system is managed by provincial governments, with each province having its own curriculum and structure. Education is compulsory from the age of 5 to 16, with most students attending kindergarten through grade 12. The system includes public, private, and separate (religious) schools, with funding varying by province.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
The curriculum varies by province but typically includes core subjects like English, mathematics, science, and social studies. Assessment methods differ, with some provinces using standardized tests and others relying on continuous evaluation. The system emphasizes bilingual education in English and French, with separate schools available for minority language groups.

#### **Higher Education and Funding**
Higher education in Canada includes universities and colleges, with funding provided by provincial governments. The system follows the Bologna Process, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Universities are autonomous, with admission based on academic performance and standardized tests. Private universities are also available but face regulatory scrutiny.

#### **Criticisms and Reforms**
Criticisms include concerns about grade inflation, lack of critical thinking, and the impact of standardized testing on student performance. Recent reforms aim to improve teacher training, curriculum development, and student well-being, though challenges remain in addressing inequality and ensuring equitable access to education.
