**Education in Saudi Arabia**  
Saudi Arabia’s education system is rooted in Islamic teachings, with religious studies forming a core component. It began with early Islamic schools (kuttabs) and evolved through key reforms in the 20th century, including the establishment of the first Department of Education in 1926 and King Saud University in 1957. The system emphasizes religious content, with students receiving nine weekly periods of Islamic studies at the primary level—far more than in science or math. Despite this, the system faces criticism for rote learning, lack of scientific instruction, and gender-based limitations, though reforms like the Tatweer program aim to modernize curricula and promote secular, vocational education. Women’s education has advanced significantly, with women now comprising 52% of university students and forming a large portion of the education workforce. Private education is supported by the government, and international schools cater to expatriate communities. The system is free and managed by the Ministry of Education, with strong religious influence and growing efforts to integrate technology and global skills.

**Education in Bangladesh**  
Bangladesh’s education system is structured into pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a national curriculum in both English and Bengali. The system is managed by the Ministry of Education and includes a national curriculum with mandatory subjects in both languages. Students choose between three streams—Arts, Commerce, and Science—after class 9. The curriculum emphasizes standardized exams (JSC, SSC, HSC) and includes vocational training through technical and vocational schools. Education is accessible to most, but challenges remain, including gender disparities, poor infrastructure, and a lack of industry-oriented skills. The system has improved access to education, with a growing emphasis on practical skills and inclusive education. Religious education is taught in schools, and there is a strong presence of madrasahs, which provide religious instruction. The government has implemented policies to improve access and quality, especially in rural areas.

**Education in New Zealand**  
New Zealand offers a comprehensive, publicly funded education system with free schooling from age 5 to 18. The curriculum follows the National Curriculum and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa for Māori-medium schools, emphasizing literacy, numeracy, and cultural diversity. Schools are categorized into state, state-integrated, and private institutions, with state schools being the most common. Māori language education is a key component, with immersion programs and full integration into the curriculum. The system promotes inclusivity, with special education services for students with disabilities and strong emphasis on community involvement. The government has invested in teacher training and curriculum development, though challenges remain in student performance and teacher retention. The education system is known for its strong emphasis on equity, cultural preservation, and student well-being.

**Education in Canada**  
Canada’s education system is decentralized, with provinces managing their own policies. Education is compulsory from age 5 to 16, with a focus on both academic and practical skills. The system includes primary, secondary, and tertiary education, with a strong emphasis on diversity, inclusion, and student choice. Canada has a robust system of public and private schools, with a growing number of Indigenous and bilingual programs. The curriculum includes a wide range of subjects, and students are encouraged to explore interests through extracurricular programs. The system is supported by government funding, with a focus on teacher training, equity, and access. Challenges include socioeconomic disparities, teacher shortages, and the need for improved student outcomes in STEM and vocational fields. Canada’s education system is recognized globally for its diversity and innovation.

**Education in Mexico**  
Mexico’s education system is structured into primary (1–6), secondary (7–9), and preparatory (10–12) levels, with a strong emphasis on vocational training. The system is managed by the Ministry of Public Education and includes both public and private institutions. The government has implemented reforms to improve access and quality, including increased investment in teacher training and infrastructure. The system has faced criticism for low secondary school completion rates and limited access to higher education. However, efforts to improve access to vocational and technical education have been made, with a growing number of students pursuing technical and vocational programs. The curriculum includes a strong emphasis on science, technology, and critical thinking, with a focus on preparing students for the workforce. The system is also working to address gender disparities and improve access for marginalized communities.

**Education in the Czech Republic**  
The Czech education system is structured into primary (grades 1–9), secondary (grades 10–13), and tertiary levels. Primary education is divided into two stages, with a focus on foundational skills. Secondary education includes general, vocational, and professional tracks, with a strong emphasis on standardized testing and academic achievement. The system is known for its high standards and strong emphasis on teacher training and curriculum development. The Czech Republic has a well-developed system of vocational and technical education, with a strong focus on practical skills and job readiness. The system is supported by government funding, with a focus on equity and access. Challenges remain in addressing disparities in access and quality, particularly in rural areas.

**Education in Japan**  
Japan’s education system is highly structured, with a strong emphasis on academic achievement and standardized testing. The system is divided into primary (ages 6–12), secondary (ages 12–18), and tertiary education. The curriculum emphasizes core subjects such as mathematics, science, and language, with a strong focus on discipline and academic excellence. The system is known for its rigorous entrance exams, which determine access to higher education. The government has implemented reforms to reduce student stress and promote holistic development. Despite these efforts, the system continues to face criticism for high levels of student stress, bullying, and a lack of creativity. The education system is also working to improve access to vocational and technical education, with a growing number of students pursuing vocational programs.
