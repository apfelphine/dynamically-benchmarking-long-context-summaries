# Summary of Education Systems Worldwide

---

## Saudi Arabia
Education began with Islamic teachings, evolving from mosque-linked Kuttabs to modern institutions. Since 1926, the Ministry of Education expanded schooling, with King Saud University (1957) as the first university and girls' education starting in 1964. Education emphasizes Islamic studies alongside sciences, with women comprising nearly two-thirds of university graduates. Despite criticism in the 1980s-90s for rote learning and poor technical skills, recent reforms include integrating diverse fields and improving female education.

The education system is free, governed by the Ministry of Education and other agencies, with strategic goals focusing on early childhood, inclusivity, quality teaching, ICT infrastructure, and social participation. Primary education is compulsory for ages 6–12, now including English from first grade. Secondary education has general, specialized, and technical streams. Higher education sees 24 government universities offering diverse degrees, supported by scholarship programs abroad.

Girls' education historically faced resistance but now accounts for 60% of university students, with dedicated female institutions like Princess Nora University. Gifted and special education programs have been increasingly developed since 2002, with specialized enrichment and inclusive services expanding. Private education complements public schooling, with regulations improving infrastructure. International schools serve expatriate communities, particularly serving large South Asian populations.

Criticism includes heavy religious content promoting intolerance and rote learning limiting innovation. Reforms seek to modernize curricula and reduce extremism. Literacy rates have improved but disparities exist regionally and by gender. Large-scale projects like King Abdullah’s development plan aim to enhance educational quality.

## Bangladesh
The education system comprises pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (classes 9–12), and tertiary levels, with instruction possible in Bengali or English. Primary education is managed by the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education, while secondary and higher education fall under the Ministry of Education. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board governs curriculum and assessments, which focus heavily on standardized exams.

Various schooling options include government, private, cadet, religious madrasahs (Alia and Qawmi), non-formal education, and international schools, offering diverse curriculums such as Cambridge and IB. Vocational and technical training is coordinated by the Directorate of Technical Education. Madrasah education provides both religious instruction and general education, with government subsidizing Alia madrasahs.

Primary and secondary education face challenges like teacher absenteeism, poverty-driven dropouts, and regional disparities, though recent initiatives aim for universal primary education, gender parity, and increased enrollment. Literacy rates have significantly increased to approx. 75% (2022 census). Higher education is expanding across over 140 universities, though industry-oriented critical thinking remains limited. NGO-run non-formal education supports dropouts and marginalized groups.

## New Zealand
Education is compulsory from ages 6 to 16, with free state and state-integrated schooling available up to age 19. Early childhood education includes kindergartens and parent-led services, following the curriculum framework Te Whāriki. Primary education covers Years 1–8, with secondary education spanning Years 9–13, offering nationally prescribed curricula in English and Māori (Te Marautanga o Aotearoa).

Schools are state, state-integrated (religious or special character), or private, with governance by Boards of Trustees. Enrolment schemes define zones to manage capacity and demographics. Māori-medium education has been revitalized since the 1980s with immersion schools (kura kaupapa Māori), though participation remains lower than desired.

Higher education includes universities and vocational training, funded by government subsidies, student fees, and loans/allowances. Educational standards face challenges, especially in mathematics, with initiatives addressing equities for Māori and Pacific Island students. Bullying remains a concern, prompting calls for comprehensive school policies.

## Canada
Education is primarily a provincial responsibility, with variation across provinces including compulsory schooling ages, curriculum, and length of study. Kindergarten to Grade 12 is typical, with provinces offering two-year kindergarten programs and different secondary school structures. Post-secondary education includes universities and vocational colleges, offering a range of degrees and certifications.

Canada has constitutional provisions for publicly funded religious and minority language schools. University admission relies on secondary school grades and varies by institution. Private schools constitute about 6% of enrollment, with students often achieving higher outcomes attributed to socio-economic factors.

Assessment agencies such as the Canadian Education Statistics Council and OECD PISA evaluate educational outcomes. Section 15(2) of the Charter permits programs enhancing disadvantaged groups, sparking debate over data collection on racial groups in schools. The country invests heavily in tertiary education with high enrollment and international rankings.

## Mexico
Basic education includes primary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and high school/preparatoria (10–12). Junior high includes general and vocational tracks; high schools offer specialized and university-preparatory paths. Since 1993, educational integration policies promote inclusion of special needs students, though challenges persist in training, accessibility, and collaboration.

Criticism centers on high dropout rates beyond primary school and inadequate infrastructure, particularly in rural areas. Reforms in 2013 aim to reduce union control and improve quality. Higher education follows a Bologna-like structure with bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. Intercultural universities address indigenous underrepresentation.

As of 2015, Mexico hosts numerous international schools offering diverse curricula.

## Czech Republic
Elementary education covers nine years split into two stages, with a single teacher in early years and subject specialists later. Secondary education includes vocational schools, professional lyceums, grammar schools, and academies, culminating in the maturita exam necessary for university entry.

Tertiary education comprises public and private universities and professional schools; higher education is free for Czech speakers under 26. Degrees range from bachelor's to doctoral, with specialized academic titles. The school year runs from September to June, divided into two semesters, with various holidays.

## Japan
Education began in the 6th century with Confucian and Buddhist teachings. The modern system, influenced by Meiji Restoration reforms, features six years of elementary, three years of lower and upper secondary, and four-year university programs. Compulsory education ends after junior high (grade 9).

School years start in April, with multiple types of primary and secondary schools. English is compulsory from elementary level, supplemented by native language teachers under the JET program. Upper secondary education includes general and vocational tracks, with high university entrance exam pressure leading to widespread private "juku" tutoring.

Higher education includes universities and junior colleges; prestigious universities such as Tokyo and Kyoto rank internationally. Criticisms focus on intense academic pressure, bullying, and limited creativity cultivation. Inclusive education for students with disabilities has expanded. International schools operate mainly in English but are not part of compulsory education.

## Poland
Compulsory education begins at age 6 or 7, with eight years of primary education followed by various paths in secondary education: general secondary schools (liceum), technical schools (technikum), vocational schools, and special schools. A recent reform abolished the junior high (gimnazjum) system, returning to 8 years of primary and extended secondary education.

Higher education follows the Bologna model with bachelor's and master's degrees, while doctoral programs continue. Universities use a grading scale from 2.0 (fail) to 5.0 (excellent). Foreign languages are widely taught, with English and German most common. Historically, Poland had early compulsory education and one of Europe's oldest universities (Jagiellonian University). Reforms improved educational outcomes but political shifts impacted curricula.

## Netherlands
Education is managed by the Ministry of Education, Culture and Science and municipal governments. Compulsory schooling starts at age 5 (practically 4) and ends at 18 or upon diploma acquisition. Schools include public, special (religious), and general-special (neutral), all government-funded equally under conditions.

Elementary school (basisschool) lasts 8 years, followed by secondary education with tracks: VMBO (vocational), HAVO (general), VWO (university preparatory). Assessment includes the Cito test at elementary level. Movement between levels is possible but challenging.

Higher education splits into MBO (vocational), HBO (applied sciences), and WO (research universities). Accreditation is ensured nationally. International schools serve expatriates. The Dutch system has been critiqued for early tracking affecting student potential and declining reading and math standards.

## Pakistan
Education is patterned on the British system with stages: pre-school, primary, middle, secondary (SSC, HSSC), tertiary, and vocational education. Public expenditure on education is around 2% of GDP, below desired levels. Gender disparities exist with lower female enrollment and literacy, though improvements have been made.

Formal education includes general and vocational paths, while religious madrasas provide alternate Islamic-focused education. Issues include outdated curricula, teacher shortages, inadequate infrastructure, and urban-rural divides. Distance and digital learning have expanded during COVID-19. Pakistan has notable alumni in science and mathematics.

Special education, teacher training quality, and socio-cultural factors affect access and equity. Textbooks often propagate biased perspectives. Literacy rate stands at ~60%. International schools exist mainly in urban centers.

## South Korea
Grounded in Confucian traditions, South Korea emphasizes meritocratic education. The system includes 6 years elementary, 3 years middle, 3 years high school (academic and vocational streams), and university education. Education pressure is intense, culminating in highly competitive university entrance exams (CSAT). Vocational education is being revitalized with initiatives like Meister Schools.

English education is widespread with strong parental investment in private tutoring (hagwons). Higher education includes diverse institutions with universities like Seoul National University considered prestigious. Despite high attainment, the system faces criticism for stress, social inequality, and undervaluing vocational routes.

## Russia
The education system comprises general and professional tracks with preschool, primary (4 years), basic general (5 years), and secondary (2 years) education. Secondary education is compulsory for nine years. Vocational options exist alongside standard schooling.

Higher education traditionally combined undergraduate and graduate into specialist diplomas but is transitioning to a Bologna-aligned bachelor and master system. Postgraduate degrees include Candidate and Doctor of Sciences. State examinations (Unified State Exam) standardize university admissions.

Education for disabled students includes specialized schools and inclusive programs. Russian schools offer instruction in various minority languages. Recent laws introduce outreach activities and patriotic education, sparking concerns over censorship and militaristic curriculum.
