# **Merged Summary: Global Education Systems – Saudi Arabia, Bangladesh, New Zealand, Mexico, Czech Republic, Japan, Poland, Netherlands, Pakistan, South Korea, and Russia**

## **Saudi Arabia**  
Saudi Arabia’s education system is deeply rooted in Islamic tradition, beginning with Quranic instruction in *kuttabs* affiliated with mosques. Since 1926, institutional development has been led by the Department of Education, resulting in the establishment of 24 government universities and expanded access to higher education—particularly for women, who surpassed men in enrollment by 2015 (60% of students). Free and compulsory education is provided from ages 6 to 12, with English introduced from the first grade. Secondary education offers general and technical tracks, and higher education reforms, such as the $2 billion *Tatweer* program, aim to reduce rote learning, promote critical thinking, and modernize curricula. Textbooks have been revised to remove or soften extremist and anti-Semitic content, earning international recognition. However, religious instruction remains dominant, and critics note persistent anti-Christian, anti-Jewish, and anti-Shia content. The Mawhiba program supports over 6,000 gifted students in STEM through 91 centers, while special education is expanding with higher teacher salaries—though challenges remain in teacher training, inclusion, physical accessibility, and transition planning. Parental involvement and career services are limited. Private schools offer international curricula under government oversight, with cultural integration remaining a challenge. Literacy varies by region (lowest in Riyadh at 9.9%, highest in Jizan at 23.5%), and gender disparities persist in adult literacy (89.1% male vs. 79.4% female). The King Abdullah Project (SR9 billion) aims to train 400,000 teachers and enhance extracurricular development to support workforce readiness.

## **Bangladesh**  
Bangladesh’s education system spans five levels: primary, secondary, tertiary, technical/vocational, and alternative (including madrasahs and international schools). Primary education is managed by the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education, with curriculum oversight by the National Curriculum and Textbook Board. As of 2023, the Primary Education Council and Junior School Certificate exams have been discontinued. Secondary education is administered by 10 regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education, conducting SSC and HSC exams. Government schools are led by headmasters; non-government schools operate under School Management Committees. Tertiary education includes 43 public, 103 private, and 2 international universities offering programs in engineering, medicine, agriculture, and chartered accountancy. Public universities are subsidized and competitive, while private institutions offer greater flexibility and access to foreign students. Technical and vocational education provides diploma programs (one month to four years) with pathways to bachelor’s and postgraduate degrees. Alternative education includes international schools (e.g., Cambridge, IB) and madrasahs—divided into Qawmi and Alia types—where Alia madrasas receive government subsidies and deliver both religious and general education. Religious education is integrated into the national curriculum for Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Christianity. The grading system uses a GPA of up to 5.0, with only scores above 33% being passing. Despite progress in accessibility and gender parity (girls often outperform boys), systemic issues persist: outdated curricula, heavy rote learning, poor teacher performance (15.5% absenteeism), and high dropout and repetition rates—especially in rural areas. Public education spending is around 2% of GDP. Literacy has improved significantly—from 51.77% in 2011 to 74.66% in 2022. Refugee education, particularly for Rohingya children, remains severely limited, and non-formal education (e.g., by BRAC) supports dropouts but lacks continuity into secondary education.

## **New Zealand**  
New Zealand operates a free, compulsory, secular national education system established in 1878, spanning 13 years (Years 1–13): 8 years of primary (Years 1–8) and 5 years of secondary (Years 9–13), with compulsory schooling from age 6 to 16 and most students continuing until age 17. Early childhood education includes licensed centers (e.g., kindy, day care) and parent-led programs such as *Kohanga Reo* and *Playcentre*, guided by the *Te Whāriki* early learning framework. The national curriculum (NZC or TMoA) covers eight learning areas, and private schools are required to offer equivalent programs. The system emphasizes equity, cultural inclusivity, and student well-being, with strong support for Māori language and identity. Teacher training and curriculum development are centralized, and accountability mechanisms ensure quality. While challenges such as regional disparities and access in rural areas exist, the system is widely regarded for its inclusivity, strong early learning foundations, and integration of cultural values. State schools (85% of students) are free and secular, while state-integrated schools (10%) maintain religious or cultural character and charge attendance dues. Private schools (4%) operate on tuition fees. Students may choose between the Cambridge International Examination (CIE) or the International Baccalaureate (IB). Enrolment schemes at 700 schools restrict access based on geographic zones and priority criteria, raising equity and property value concerns. Initiatives like *kōhanga reo* and *kura kaupapa* promote Māori language and culture, with students in *kura kaupapa* outperforming peers academically, though Māori and Pacific Island students as a whole underperform in national assessments. Educational standards have declined in international evaluations like TIMSS, with criticism of teaching methods favoring alternative strategies over foundational skills. Bullying—especially cyberbullying—is widespread, prompting reforms after a 2009 incident at Hutt Valley High School. A $60 million *Positive Behaviour for Learning* initiative was launched, but results were unsatisfactory. In 2013, school leaders called for a national anti-bullying policy. Manual training schools were phased out post-World War II.

## **Mexico**  
Mexico’s education system evolved from pre-Hispanic, religiously grounded institutions like the Aztec *calmecac* and *cuicacalli*, through colonial Catholic training, to a modern, secular, and compulsory system. Post-independence reforms under Benito Juárez established free and mandatory primary education, while the Reform era (1850–1876) introduced uniform secondary education. The Porfiriato expanded access with a focus on literacy, though teacher training remained weak. After the Mexican Revolution, centralization under the Secretariat of Public Education (SEP) promoted a nationalist curriculum; Lázaro Cárdenas’ socialist policies targeted religious schools and emphasized collective responsibility, sparking unrest. Neoliberal reforms in the 1990s decentralized education and introduced merit-based pay, leading to union resistance. From the 1940s to 1960s, access improved through teacher training, free textbooks, and mass media. By 2020, literacy and attendance reached 97.5%, though rural areas and teacher autonomy remain challenges. UNAM ranks 103rd globally (QS), and Monterrey Institute of Technology ranks 158th; business schools IPADE and EGADE are among the world’s top 10 in recruitment.

## **Czech Republic**  
Elementary education spans nine grades, divided into two stages: grades 1–5 focus on core subjects, and grades 6–9 offer expanded curricula. Students may continue in elementary school or transfer to academies (e.g., six- or eight-year programs). Secondary education requires passing the *maturita* exam, with a 12.3% failure rate in 2025. Vocational, professional, and grammar schools provide diverse pathways; grammar schools prepare students for university without formal qualifications. Special and practical schools support students with developmental disabilities, with integrated education expanding. At the tertiary level, public universities offer free education to first-time students under 26 in Czech. Private universities charge €2,000–10,000. Degrees include Bachelor (3 years, Bc), Master (MgA, Ing., Mgr.), and Ph.D., all requiring final exams and theses. Doctoral degrees require a *rigorózní zkouška* after a master’s. Academic titles such as PhDr., JUDr., RNDr., and PharmDr. are used, with older titles being phased out. The academic year runs from late September to late June, with breaks including winter, spring, and summer holidays. Children’s Day (1 June) is not a public holiday but involves school trips.

## **Japan**  
Japan’s education system consists of three levels: primary (grades 1–6), lower secondary (grades 7–9), and upper secondary (grades 10–12). Students begin in April, with two to three terms and a six-week summer break. Lower secondary is nearly universally completed (98.3% retention), with core subjects including Japanese, math, science, and social studies. English became compulsory in elementary schools in 2011. Upper secondary is not compulsory but highly attended (98.8%), offering academic and vocational tracks. Public high schools are tuition-free since 2010; private institutions vary in cost. Prestigious universities include the University of Tokyo and Kyoto University. The traditional *shū to fuka* grading system is used, and many students attend private cram schools (*juku*) to prepare for university entrance exams. The government promotes equal access via the Basic Act on Education and the CRPD, with inclusive reforms since the 1990s (e.g., *Resource Room System*) improving access for students with disabilities. Despite progress, Japan faces serious student well-being challenges. *Shadow education*—including *juku*, *mogi shiken*, and *ronin*—is widespread, with over 86% of students planning to attend university participating in such programs. This intense pressure contributes to significant psychological harm: 612,496 bullying cases in 2019, 317 suicides (10 linked to bullying), and widespread anxiety and depression. Critics argue the system lacks creativity, limits free time, and over-reliance on cram schools consumes time and resources. International schools (*kakushu gakko*) are not recognized as fulfilling compulsory education requirements.

## **Poland**  
Poland extended compulsory education to 8 years (grades 1–8), phasing out middle schools by 2019. Upper secondary education offers general (e.g., *liceum*, leading to *Matura*) or vocational paths (e.g., *technikum*), with vocational training leading to certificates or *Matura* after additional study. The system follows the Bologna model with three-year bachelor’s and two-year master’s degrees (some programs, like medicine, longer), and doctoral programs lasting about three years. Teacher training requires three years; vocational education lasts 2.5 years. Grading uses a 2.0–5.0 scale (0.5 increments), with 2.0 as failing and 3.0 as minimum passing; retakes are common. Foreign language education includes English (67.9%), German (33.3%), and French (13.3%), with a 2005 reform mandating two foreign languages in lower secondary. The 2017 reform removed communist content from history curricula, which was criticized for promoting nationalism.

## **Netherlands**  
The Dutch education system is rooted in 19th-century reforms, including the 1806 School Act (mandatory primary education, secular curriculum), and later expansions via the 1857 and 1863 acts introducing secondary and vocational education. By the 1960s, diverse secondary pathways emerged: VMBO (pre-vocational), HAVO (general), and VWO (advanced). The 1963 *Mammoetwet* standardized secondary education into four streams, expanded compulsory education to 10 years, and introduced structured progression. Later reforms emphasized autonomy, integration, and student responsibility, merging LTS/VBO into VMBO and introducing profile-based upper secondary education. Islamic schools were introduced in 2003, and all schools now have care teams for students with health or social needs. The system remains largely secular, with religious schools receiving equal funding since 1917.  

**Primary education** (ages 4–12) spans eight grades, with foundational subjects introduced from grade 3. English is taught from grades 7–8, and students take the Cito test to inform placement in secondary education. Montessori and Pestalozzi philosophies are applied in public schools. Secondary education begins at age 12 with a bridge class (brugklas) supporting transition. Students choose among three main pathways:  
- **VMBO** (pre-vocational, 4 years), with four learning paths (TL, GL, KBL, BBL) and practical training;  
- **HAVO** (general, 5 years), leading to HBO (applied universities);  
- **VWO** (advanced, 6 years), leading to WO (research universities), with options like *atheneum* (no classical languages) or *gymnasium* (Latin/Greek required), and optional programs like TVWO or VWO-plus.  
Students can progress from VMBO to HAVO and HAVO to VWO. Adult learners may access **VAVO** for subject certificates.  

After secondary education, students enter one of three higher education pathways:  
- **MBO** (middle-level applied education): 1–4 year vocational training in skilled trades or support roles; graduates may enter the workforce or pursue HBO.  
- **HBO** (higher professional education): Applied education at universities of applied sciences (hogescholen), 3–6 years, leading to a Bachelor’s (e.g., BSc, BA); graduates may pursue a professional master’s.  
- **WO** (scientific education): Academic university-level education at research universities, offering Bachelor’s, Master’s, and PhD degrees. Open to VWO or HBO graduates with a propaedeuse.  

Admission varies: WO requires VWO or HBO propaedeuse; HBO requires HAVO or MBO-4. Master’s programs require a bachelor’s degree. Quality is ensured by the **NVAO accreditation system**, which evaluates programs against national standards. Only accredited programs receive government funding, issue legally recognized degrees, and are listed in the **CROHO register**. The system follows the **Bologna Process** with the **ECTS credit system** (1 credit = 28 hours; 60 credits = one academic year). HBO and WO remain distinct—professional vs. academic.  

**Challenges** include early tracking at age 12 based on standardized tests like Cito, which may misplace gifted students into lower tracks (e.g., VMBO), leading to underperformance and higher dropout rates. Socioeconomic factors significantly influence outcomes. PISA data shows a declining trend in reading, math, and science performance, raising concerns about equity and quality. Regionally structured holidays (north, middle, south) avoid mass vacation timing, with a six-week summer break followed by autumn, Christmas, and spring holidays.

## **Pakistan**  
Pakistan’s education system is diverse, including formal, non-formal, informal, and madrassa-based pathways. Primary education (ages 3–14) follows an English-influenced structure, with pre-school, junior, and middle school stages; only about 67.5% of children complete primary school. Secondary education begins at year 9 with SSC exams (SSC-I and SSC-II), leading to a matriculation certificate, followed by intermediate colleges (years 11–12) for HSSC exams and entry into fields like pre-medical, engineering, or commerce. Public spending on education is critically low at ~2% of GDP—far below the recommended 7%—leading to outdated curricula, poor infrastructure, and a shortage of qualified teachers. Teacher training is often knowledge-based and lacks practical components, and many educators lack human rights awareness or are influenced by conservative ideologies. Gender disparities persist: women have lower enrollment, literacy (42% vs. men’s 67%), and access—especially in rural areas—due to cultural norms, safety, and inadequate infrastructure. Despite comprising up to 80% of medical college students, many women do not enter practice due to systemic sexism and lack of women-friendly policies. A gender quota policy was declared unconstitutional, and low-cost private schools (LCPSs) are more accessible to males, deepening gender imbalances. Transgender individuals, including hijras, face severe marginalization despite legal recognition under the 2018 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act. TVET suffers from poor curricula, teacher quality, and equity issues. Distance education via AIOU offers access to rural women but is limited by insufficient research support and low teacher compensation. The pandemic exposed significant digital divides. While university rankings have improved (e.g., NUST entering global lists), experts stress the need for an "unconventional" approach to protect education budgets from political and fiscal instability.  

**Additional Insights from Pakistan (from Summary 2):**  
The system is shaped by strong religious values, with many science teachers rejecting human evolution on religious grounds—despite acknowledging no inherent conflict between science and Islam. Religious minorities, particularly non-Muslim communities, face significant literacy disparities, though Ahmadis are an exception. School curricula often promote negative portrayals of Jews and Israel, omit key historical events like the Holocaust, and foster a religiously charged opposition to the West and India. Despite these challenges, national literacy has improved since 1951, reaching 60.65% in 2023. Urban and developed regions such as Punjab and Islamabad lead in literacy, with 14 districts achieving 75% or higher—six exceeding 80%—and 13 reporting youth literacy rates of 85% or above. Rural and remote areas like Balochistan and Gilgit-Baltistan lag due to poverty, gender inequality, and poor infrastructure. Pakistan’s literacy rate remains the second lowest in South Asia, below Indonesia and Malaysia. International education is supported through initiatives like SICAS and over 439 international schools offering English-medium curricula.

## **South Korea**  
South Korea’s education system is rooted in Confucian ideals, emphasizing meritocracy, academic excellence, and social mobility. Historically shaped by village schools, Japanese occupation, and U.S. military governance, it evolved into a centralized 6-4-3-4 structure that enabled near-universal secondary education completion (98%) and high literacy (93% by the 1980s), supported by over 27% of the national budget in education by 1986. The system remains highly competitive, centered on rigorous entrance exams—especially the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), which determines admission to top "SKY" universities (Seoul National, Korea, and Yonsei)—and intense private tutoring (*hagwons*), leading to high student stress, financial burdens, and elevated suicide rates. While vocational education was historically stigmatized, recent reforms have revitalized it through initiatives like *Meister Schools*, modeled on Germany’s apprenticeship system, offering hands-on training in high-demand trades (e.g., plumbing, welding, machining) with 100% job placement and competitive wages. Graduates may gain exemption from university admission after three years of work. Currently, only 5% of high school students enroll in vocational programs, but growing awareness and improved social perception are helping reduce stigma. Community and junior colleges offer two- to three-year technical and liberal arts programs with admission standards comparable to four-year institutions, including 50% of spots reserved for vocational graduates. Ongoing reforms, including a credit-based system introduced in 2025, aim to balance academic rigor with student well-being and practical skill development.

## **Russia**  
Russia’s education system features a national secondary framework with specialized schools in math, languages, and arts, as well as vocational training programs offering a three-year pathway to a high school equivalency. All secondary certificates are standardized through the Unified State Examination (USE), introduced in 2007, which streamlines university admissions and reduces regional disparities—though its scoring system lacks transparency. Vocational students, especially males, face challenges due to conscription timelines. Specialized institutions exist for children with physical disabilities (e.g., blind, deaf, mobility-impaired), with limited access to higher education and ongoing integration issues. Students with mental disabilities are often placed in compensatory classes or specialized boarding schools, and severe cases are largely excluded from mainstream education. At the tertiary level, over half of Russia’s adults hold tertiary qualifications, with 8.1 million students enrolled across 658 state and 450 private institutions. Historically, the system followed a five- to six-year specialist model concentrated in major cities like Moscow and Saint Petersburg, with limited academic mobility and rigid curricula. Reforms aligned with the Bologna Process introduced a four-year bachelor and two-year master’s structure, but critics argue this shift is superficial and has not improved academic quality or job market relevance. Postgraduate education includes rigorous scientific degrees—Candidate of Sciences and Doctor of Sciences—based on original research, with the Doctor of Sciences equivalent to a habilitation. However, the 2021 "outreach activity" law has raised concerns about censorship of knowledge sharing, and since the Ukraine invasion, government-led "patriotic education" initiatives have intensified, mandating lessons on national identity and militarism, sparking significant backlash from parents and students. The system reflects significant regional and ethnic language diversity, with approximately 6,800 schools offering instruction in languages such as Russian, Tatar, Bashkir, Chechen, and others. Tatar and Bashkir have strong educational presence, while languages like Yiddish, Hebrew, and Eskimo have minimal or no instruction. In December 2024, the State Duma passed a law requiring foreign citizens to pass a Russian language exam to be admitted to schools, underscoring a broader policy emphasis on Russian language proficiency.

### **Key Observations**  
- **Japan** excels in academic outcomes but struggles with student well-being due to intense academic pressure and *shadow education*.  
- **Netherlands** prioritizes equity, local autonomy, and student well-being, with strong support systems and flexible pathways.  
- **Poland** has restructured education for clarity and access, though historical content changes have raised concerns about nationalism.  
- **Mexico** has made significant progress in access and literacy, with strong universities, though rural and teacher autonomy challenges remain.  
- **Czech Republic** offers a balanced, inclusive system with strong tertiary education and accessible pathways.  
- **Pakistan** faces systemic underfunding, gender disparities, poor teacher training, and religiously charged curricula, highlighting urgent need for reform and investment.  
- **South Korea** demonstrates high academic achievement through meritocracy and rigorous exams, but suffers from student stress, financial burden, and stigma around vocational education—now being addressed through modern apprenticeship models.  
- **Russia** maintains a centralized, standardized system with strong tertiary enrollment, but faces challenges in equity, inclusion, academic quality, and political interference in education, including censorship and mandatory patriotic content.
