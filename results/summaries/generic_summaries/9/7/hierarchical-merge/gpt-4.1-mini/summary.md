# Education in Saudi Arabia and Bangladesh

Saudi Arabia’s publicly funded education system, overseen by the Ministry of Education, integrates Islamic traditions and ranges from pre-primary to higher education. Reforms since 1926, notably the King Abdullah Project, have expanded female participation—now the university majority—and improved literacy despite regional disparities and rote learning challenges. The country hosts 24 government universities mainly focused on humanities and social sciences, alongside special education and private international schools with scholarship opportunities.

Bangladesh’s diverse education landscape includes general, technical, alternative, and religious streams, with primary education nationally managed and secondary education regionally overseen. Literacy reached 74.66% by 2022 with improved gender parity aided by government incentives. However, high rural dropout rates, teacher absenteeism, and rote learning persist. The 44 public universities are supervised by the University Grants Commission, while madrasahs and NGO non-formal programs provide education for marginalized populations.

# Education in New Zealand and Canada

New Zealand offers a secular education system from early childhood to secondary levels, with compulsory schooling from ages 6 to 16 and a national curriculum covering eight learning areas. It includes state, state-integrated (often religious), and private schools, with free public education. Māori language immersion schools significantly improve outcomes for Māori students. The system addresses challenges like bullying, math decline, and disparities among Māori and Pacific communities through initiatives like Positive Behaviour for Learning.

Canada’s provincially governed system generally mandates schooling from ages 5–7 up to 16–18, ending with Grade 12 (or Grade 11 plus CEGEP in Quebec). Public schools follow provincial curricula with growing International Baccalaureate and Advanced Placement offerings; private schools serve about 6% of students. The post-secondary sector includes universities, vocational colleges, and technical institutes. Challenges include grade inflation, achievement gaps, and the need to develop soft skills, with active participation in OECD assessments.

# Education in Mexico and the Netherlands

Mexico’s system spans preschool to high school, with compulsory education through preparatoria offering general and vocational tracks. While primary enrollment is about 90%, secondary (62%) and higher education (~25%) access remains limited, especially in rural areas. Inclusion for students with special needs is mandated but under-supported. Political shifts centralized control from teacher unions, and higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, including English-medium and Intercultural Universities for indigenous communities. Despite a 95% literacy rate, disparities and political challenges endure.

The Netherlands requires schooling from ages 5 to 18, with stratified secondary tracks (VMBO, HAVO, VWO) decided by primary exit tests leading to vocational colleges or research universities. The inclusive, government-funded system promotes student autonomy and digital skills but faces socio-economic disparities, limited gifted student support, and a recent decline in international rankings.

# Education in Japan and Poland

Japan mandates compulsory education from ages 6 to 15 with high secondary attendance supported by competitive entrance exams and prevalent supplementary cram schools. The curriculum integrates English education and inclusivity for disabilities. Reforms seek to reduce academic pressure and promote critical thinking beyond rote methods, though concerns persist about student well-being and holistic skills development.

Poland requires schooling starting at ages 6 or 7, featuring eight years of primary education followed by diverse secondary tracks culminating in the Matura exam aligned with the Bologna Process. Recent reforms emphasize foreign language learning, anti-violence policies, and nationalistic curriculum elements while balancing academic and vocational training.

# Education in Pakistan and South Korea

Pakistan’s education system covers primary to tertiary levels, vocational training, and Islamic madrasahs. Despite a rise in literacy to 60.65% by 2023, it remains among the lowest in South Asia with marked gender and urban-rural disparities. Challenges include poverty, feudalism, gender inequality, weak oversight, ideologically influenced content, and limited female access, especially in rural areas. Higher education quality varies with increasing international enrollment and emerging institutional improvements.

South Korea’s system combines academic and vocational tracks within a 6-4-3-4 structure, with vocational schools comprising 20% of secondary enrollment. Meister Schools promote trade skills but face stigma. Higher education centers on the CSAT exam, driving extensive private tutoring and overseas English study. Recent reforms target flexibility and student well-being, including bans on corporal punishment and mobile phones. Despite high literacy and education quality, issues around vocational stigma, elitism, and mental health remain.

# Education in Russia

Russia’s education includes preschool to postgraduate levels with general and professional tracks, compulsory secondary education between 9 and 11 years, and a favorable teacher-student ratio. Since 2007, the Unified State Examination standardizes university admissions, complementing a tertiary sector of over 1,000 universities transitioning towards the Bologna bachelor-master framework. Government initiatives since 2020 increased state control emphasizing patriotic education. Extensive language policies support indigenous and minority languages across thousands of schools, maintaining linguistic diversity. A 2024 law requires foreign students to demonstrate Russian proficiency before admission, underscoring language acquisition priorities.
