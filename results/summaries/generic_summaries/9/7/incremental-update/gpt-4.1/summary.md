## Comparative Overview of Education Systems

### Saudi Arabia
Saudi Arabia blends Islamic and secular curricula, providing free and compulsory primary education, teacher development, vocational and gifted programs, and special needs inclusion. However, gender segregation, infrastructure deficits, literacy gaps, and low female workforce participation remain challenges.

### Bangladesh
Bangladesh’s diverse network includes public, private, NGO, religious, and international schools. Free female education has improved literacy, yet dropout rates, urban/rural disparities, underfunding, minority access issues, and reliance on rote learning persist.

### New Zealand
New Zealand’s secular system integrates Māori culture and language, uses flexible qualifications, and provides need-based funding. Māori immersion and special needs support are available, but achievement gaps, bullying, restrictive zoning, and heavy student loan dependence are ongoing concerns.

### Canada
Canada’s decentralized structure supports diverse curricula and school types, including language and religious options. Equity and anti-racism initiatives address teacher compensation, tuition disparities, and regional achievement gaps, with Alberta leading in performance.

### Mexico
Mexico’s centrally managed, secular system is shaped by indigenous and colonial influences, focusing on literacy, teacher training, inclusion, and free resources. Mandatory schooling for ages 6–14 faces challenges of underfunding, weak infrastructure, and rural inequity.

### Czech Republic
Compulsory elementary education routes Czech students into selective secondary tracks or integrated programs for disabilities, followed by tertiary options including bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees.

### Japan
Japan offers six years of primary education and competitive secondary schools, with widespread “juku” tutoring, moral and English emphasis, special needs inclusion, and April university entry.

### Poland
Poland starts formal schooling at six or seven, with compulsory foreign language study, academic/vocational tracks, disability integration, robust teacher protections, and improved international performance.

### Netherlands
Dutch education is compulsory from age 5, with early vocational, professional, or academic tracking and standardized funding. Social mobility is declining, and gaps are widening for disadvantaged and gifted students.

### Pakistan
Pakistan’s system comprises formal, non-formal, and informal streams, plus technical and international schools. Chronic underfunding, teacher shortages, high dropout rates—especially among girls and minorities—literacy gaps, and curriculum bias persist, while special and higher education continue developing.

### South Korea
South Korea’s Confucian model achieves near-universal coverage and high international scores, but exam pressure, related health issues, limited student rights, teacher dissatisfaction, and vocational stigma drive ongoing reforms aimed at balance and wellbeing.

### Russia
Russia’s system features general (preschool, primary, basic, secondary) and professional (vocational, higher, postgraduate) tracks, rising preschool enrollment despite urban shortages, and nine years of compulsory schooling before further education. The Unified State Examination standardizes university admissions, while disability inclusion is mainly through specialized schools.

Higher education reforms are consolidating universities and removing low-quality institutions, shifting toward the Bologna Process structure. Recent laws emphasize “patriotic education” and restrict science outreach, attracting criticism. Minority and native language education serve millions, though coverage varies widely by language.

In December 2024, Russia added a requirement for foreign citizens to pass a Russian language exam before school admission.

---

This comparative analysis highlights global education systems’ administration, curricula, reforms, language and inclusion policies, vocational and higher education pathways, and persistent challenges.
