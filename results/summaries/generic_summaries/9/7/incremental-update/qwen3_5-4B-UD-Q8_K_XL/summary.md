**Russia: Educational Structure, Reforms, Linguistic Diversity, and Recent Legislation**

The Russian education system, comprising general and professional sectors, has seen preschool and primary enrollment rise from 67% (1999) to 84% (2005) before a dip in 2020. Regulated by local authorities, services are generally free or low-cost, though major cities like Moscow face capacity shortages. Secondary education is compulsory for nine years (eleven in Moscow), divided into elementary, middle, and senior levels. The system features a single teacher per grade (1–4) with a low pupil-to-teacher ratio, a fixed state curriculum, and a 5-step assessment scale. Admissions were standardized in 2007 via the Unified State Examination (USE) to eliminate regional disparities and curb bribery. Students can choose between standard 11-year schooling or a 3-year vocational track; while all secondary certificates are legally equivalent nationwide, vocational graduates face specific challenges regarding military conscription timing. Regarding inclusivity, special schools cater to physical disabilities, though integration into regular universities remains limited. For mental disabilities, mild cases receive compensatory classes, while those with stronger intellectual disabilities are often excluded or placed in correctional boarding schools focused on social adaptation. Tertiary education is robust, with an adult enrollment rate exceeding 50% (2005 data) and 8.1 million students enrolled in 2007–08 across state and private institutions. Recent trends indicate the consolidation of regional universities into federal conglomerates, such as the Southern, Siberian, and Far Eastern Federal Universities.

The tertiary sector is currently undergoing significant reforms, transitioning from a traditional five-year specialist model to the Bologna Process's two-tiered bachelor's and master's degrees. While this shift aims to modernize the curriculum, critics argue it creates financial burdens for students, results in a "formal" change where degrees are merely appended to existing programs, and fails to improve student mobility. Simultaneously, the country maintains a unique postgraduate structure featuring scientific degrees (Candidate and Doctor of Sciences) that emphasize research output over academic titles. Recent legislative moves, including laws on science outreach and "patriotic lessons," have sparked controversy regarding freedom of expression and the influence of government propaganda in education.

A distinct aspect of the Russian educational landscape is its support for minority and regional languages. Approximately 6,400–6,800 schools offer instruction in a language other than Russian as the primary medium, while roughly 10,500 schools teach minority languages as a subject. **Tatar** and **Udmurt** lead in schools offering medium-of-instruction, with Tatar significantly outnumbering others. **Bashkir** and **Mari (Meadow)** dominate both medium-of-instruction and subject-teaching categories. These educational figures correlate with demographic populations, where Tatars (5.5M) and Ukrainians (3M) are the largest groups, though Tatars specifically dominate the metrics for regional language education.

In December 2024, the State Duma approved new legislation prohibiting schools from enrolling foreign citizens who have not passed the Russian language exam, further reinforcing the central role of the Russian language in the national education system.
