# Education in Mexico
Mexico's basic education comprises three compulsory levels: primary (grades 1–6), junior high (secundaria, grades 7–9), and high school (preparatoria or bachillerato, grades 10–12). Primary education typically starts at age six, may include bilingual programs, and is followed by secundaria which introduces specialized subjects and vocational training options such as técnica and telesecundaria. High school allows students to specialize academically or vocationally, with institutions like Conalep offering technical skills.

Inclusive education was mandated in 1993 to integrate students with special needs into mainstream classrooms, supported by specialized professionals (USAER). Challenges include role confusion, lack of collaboration, inadequate accessibility, and insufficient teacher training. Despite high primary enrollment (>90%), secondary and higher education completion rates are lower, attributed partly to infrastructure deficits and funding constraints. Reforms since 2013 have shifted education control from powerful teachers' unions to government, facing ongoing resistance. Higher education follows a bachelor-master-doctorate structure parallel to the Bologna Process, with Indigenous-focused Intercultural Universities established to improve representation. Mexico hosts 151 international schools.

# Education in New Zealand
Pre-European Māori education was oral and tribal, with early mission schools teaching in Māori before English-language education was promoted. New Zealand's formal education system was nationally established in 1878, offering free, compulsory, secular schooling. Early childhood education is diverse, including kindergartens, playcentres, and Māori immersion programs (Kohanga Reo), all regulated under the Ministry of Education.

Primary (Years 1–8) and secondary education (Years 9–13) cover ages 5–18, with teaching in English and Māori. The national curriculum includes compulsory subjects, with qualifications from National Certificate of Educational Achievement (NCEA) and options for international qualifications like Cambridge and IB. Schools are state-funded, state-integrated (mostly former private religious schools charging attendance dues), or private. School zones regulate enrolment in urban areas. Māori language education faced historic suppression but has seen revival through immersion schools and legal recognition. Issues of bullying have prompted government programs to promote safe learning environments. Tertiary education includes diverse institutions funded through government subsidies and student loans.

# Education in Japan
Japan's education dates back to the 6th century with Buddhist and Confucian influences and scholar-monks as education centers. The Meiji Restoration introduced Western-style education with compulsory schooling modeled on Prussia. Post-WWII reforms extended compulsory education to nine years and established a model with elementary (6 years), lower secondary (3 years), upper secondary (3 years), and university education. The school year starts in April.

Elementary covers grades 1–6, junior high 7–9, and senior high 10–12. English became compulsory from elementary in 2011, supported by native-speaking assistant teachers via the JET program. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, technical colleges, with around 2.9 million enrolled students. Entrance exams are highly competitive, with strong emphasis on cram schools (juku) and supplementary "shadow education." Academic pressure has led to mental health concerns and bullying issues. Inclusive education initiatives support disabled students. International schools exist but do not fulfill compulsory education requirements.

# Education in Saudi Arabia
Education in Saudi Arabia has Islamic roots with early religious schools ('Kuttabs') linked to mosques. The Ministry of Education oversees a free education system, emphasizing Quranic studies alongside subjects like math, science, and languages. Primary education lasts six years starting at age six, followed by three years each of intermediate and secondary education. Recent reforms include introducing English from first grade and streamlining Islamic subjects.

Higher education has expanded rapidly since King Saud University was established in 1957, with 24 government universities and private institutions offering various degrees. Female education has grown substantially, with women constituting 60% of university students; separate women's universities like Princess Nora University support this. Gifted education programs began formally in 2002 with enrichment initiatives focusing on STEM. Special education provides inclusive and specialized services, though general educators often lack training in disabilities.

Private schools receive government support and oversight, with efforts to remove schools operating in unsuitable buildings. Saudi Arabia hosts numerous international schools, including community and private schools serving expatriate populations, with regulations on gender segregation and sponsorship. Literacy rates have improved but vary regionally.

Criticism of the system highlights overemphasis on religious rote learning, lack of innovation, and intolerance in curricula. Reforms such as the Tatweer program aim to modernize education and reduce extremism. Textbook revisions have sought to remove hate speech and promote tolerance.

# Education in the Netherlands
The Dutch education system mandates schooling from age five (practiced from four) to eighteen with various public, special (religious), and general-special schools all government-funded. Elementary education (basisschool) spans eight years (groups 1–8), with early groups functioning as kindergarten. Secondary education offers stratified tracks: VMBO (vocational, 4 years), HAVO (general, 5 years), and VWO (pre-university, 6 years). Students choose paths based on assessment tests and school advice; movement between tracks is possible but challenging.

Higher education splits into HBO (universities of applied sciences) and WO (research universities), conforming to the Bologna Process with bachelor's, master's, and PhD degrees. Vocational education is well developed within MBO levels (1–4). International schools are present (152 listed in 2015).

Historically, education evolved from early 19th-century reforms introducing compulsory schooling and stratified pathways by socio-economic class. The school year includes multiple breaks, with regional variations in holidays.

Criticism concerns early tracking at age 12, potential misplacement of gifted children, and a decline in international educational rankings and literacy standards.

# Education in Bangladesh
Bangladesh's education system has four levels: pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (classes 9–12), and tertiary. Education is delivered in Bengali or English medium, with 60% of primary teaching positions designated for women. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB) governs curriculum and standardized testing. Students choose between Arts, Commerce, and Science streams at secondary level, with national public exams at various stages, including SSC and HSC.

Primary and secondary education are managed by respective government directorates, with multiple regional boards overseeing secondary examinations. Tertiary education includes public, private, and international universities regulated by the University Grants Commission, with specialized institutions for engineering, medicine, commerce, and Islamic studies.

Alternative education includes international schools following foreign curricula, and madrasahs offering religious education with government-regulated ("Alia") and private ("Qawmi") systems. Non-formal and refugee education is provided by NGOs and UNICEF centers.

Challenges include outdated curricula focused on rote learning, infrastructure deficits, gender disparities favoring males, and limited access in rural areas. Literacy in 2022 was approximately 74.7%, up from 51.8% in 2011, with ongoing efforts to enhance quality and accessibility.

# Education in South Korea
Rooted in Confucian traditions emphasizing meritocracy, South Korea's education evolved from traditional schools and private academies to a centralized system influenced by Japanese occupation and U.S. models post-WWII. The system features 6 years of primary, 4 years of middle school, 3 years of high school, and tertiary education modeled on the U.S., with high competition for university entry.

Primary education covers grades 1–6 with a core curriculum including Korean and English. Secondary education includes general and vocational tracks, with specialized "Meister Schools" revitalizing vocational training aligned with industry needs. High school enrollment is near universal despite not being compulsory.

Higher education comprises universities, junior colleges, and industrial universities, with prestigious institutions like Seoul National University and competitive admissions relying heavily on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT). Vocational education faces social stigma but efforts are underway to improve its status and integration.

English education is widespread but historically challenged by inadequate proficiency. The education system faces criticism for excessive academic pressure, reliance on entrance exams, mental health issues among students, social stigma against vocational paths, and mismatches between education and employment. Government attempts to reform include curbing cram schools (hagwons) and promoting diversified education.

# Education in Canada
Education is provincially managed with federal responsibility limited to military and Indigenous education. Compulsory education generally spans kindergarten to grade 12, with variations in length and structure by province. Canada offers publicly funded English and French language schools and constitutional protections for religious-based schooling in some provinces.

Secondary education includes diverse pathways, with opportunities to pursue International Baccalaureate and Advanced Placement programs. Post-secondary education is provided by universities and vocational institutions, offering a wide range of degrees and certifications. Admission to universities emphasizes academic performance, with competitive cutoffs at prestigious institutions.

Canadian universities enjoy international recognition, including numerous institutions ranked globally. Vocational education offers practical training with pathways to further study or employment. Private schools account for about 6% of enrollment and tend to correlate with higher student achievement, often attributed to family engagement.

Religious schools vary with provincial policies, with Catholic schools publicly funded in some provinces like Ontario. Educational governance involves elected school boards with some provinces allowing charter schools.

# Education in Russia
Russia's education system comprises general education (preschool, primary, basic, secondary) and professional education (vocational, higher education). Preschool is widely available, though shortages exist in large cities. Compulsory education spans nine to eleven years, with an eleven-year program divided into elementary, middle, and senior classes.

Secondary education includes general and vocational tracks. Vocational education is prevalent but separate from ISCED classification. The Unified State Examination (USE) system was introduced in 2003 to standardize university admissions and reduce corruption.

Higher education is transitioning from the traditional specialist diploma model to Bologna Process cycles of bachelor's and master's degrees, though challenges persist. Postgraduate education follows a unique Candidate of Sciences and Doctor of Sciences hierarchy.

Special education caters to physical and mental disabilities, with some integration challenges. The government promotes outreach activities but faces criticism over censorship concerns. Recent efforts to incorporate patriotic education have raised controversy.

Education is offered in numerous regional languages alongside Russian. A 2024 law restricts school admission for foreign citizens without Russian proficiency.

# Education in the Czech Republic
Elementary education comprises nine grades split into two stages, taught primarily by a single teacher initially, expanding subjects and teachers in higher grades. Secondary education includes vocational schools, professional schools, lyceums, and grammar schools, with varying curricula and maturita exams as graduation requirements.

Vocational schools offer certificates after two or three years, with some providing academic maturita enabling university admission. Higher professional schools confer diplomas connected to professional high schools.

Universities are public, private, or state-run with free Czech-language tuition for first-time students; tuition is required for instruction in English or for private institutions. Degree programs follow bachelor (3 years), master, and doctoral cycles; various academic titles are awarded based on discipline. School years run from September to June with holidays in autumn, winter, spring, Easter, and summer.

# Education in Pakistan
Pakistan’s formal education system, inspired by the British model, includes pre-school, primary (years 1–5), middle (6–8), secondary (9–10), intermediate (11–12), and tertiary levels. Core subjects cover sciences, languages, social studies, religious education, and optional vocational training. Secondary and higher secondary public examinations regulate progression.

The system faces challenges of low school completion rates, gender disparities—especially in rural areas—and unequal access. Female enrollment and literacy have improved but remain lower than males. Madrassas provide religious education, sometimes substituting for formal schooling in impoverished regions.

Teacher education is under-resourced, with shortages and outdated training methods. Technical and vocational education seeks to bridge skills gaps but faces quality and management issues. Distance learning expanded during COVID-19 via television and online platforms, though rural access remains limited.

Notable Pakistani scholars include Nobel laureate Abdus Salam and scientists contributing to various fields. Education expenditure hovers around 2% of GDP with ambitions to increase, but implementation remains challenging. Curriculum and textbooks have faced criticism for promoting intolerance and ideological bias.

# Education in Poland
Children start primary education at age six or seven, currently comprising eight grades after reforms abolished the three-year gimnazjum middle school. Secondary education includes general secondary schools (liceum) preparing for university entry via the Matura exam, technical schools (technikum), and basic vocational schools. Vocational training may culminate in certificates or Matura qualifications.

Higher education follows the Bologna process with bachelor's, master's, and doctoral programs. University grading scales range numerically with 2.0 as failing.

Foreign language education is widespread, primarily English and German. Poland’s education has roots dating back to medieval times with the Jagiellonian University (1364). Compulsory schooling began post-independence with ongoing reforms to adapt structures and curricula. Recent reforms changed system structure and focus of history education, raising debates on nationalism and content.

A 2006 "zero tolerance" policy against school violence increased penalties and disciplinary measures for aggressive behavior.
