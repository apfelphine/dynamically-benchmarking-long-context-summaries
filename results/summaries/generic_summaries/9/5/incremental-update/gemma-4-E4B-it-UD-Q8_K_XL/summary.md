**Comparative Education Systems Overview**

**National Models in Detail:**

*   **Pakistan:** Faces substantial challenges, including severe underfunding (2% of GDP vs. 7% target), poor infrastructure, and low quality, compounded by systemic sexism limiting women's STEMM access. Despite a rising literacy rate of 60.65%, provincial gaps and curriculum bias persist, further hindered by teacher absenteeism and poverty, though recognized universities and alternative pathways (TVET, madrasas) exist.
*   **Czech Republic:** Utilizes structured secondary education (Vocational, Lyceums/Professional, Grammar) leading to the *maturita* for university entry, with public tertiary education offering free Czech-language study to new entrants.
*   **Russia:** Requires nine years of schooling with a state curriculum and a 5-step grading system. While tertiary education adheres to the Bologna Process, concerns remain regarding censorship within "patriotic education," and foreign citizens must meet a specific language exam deadline in December 2024.
*   **Canada:** Education is managed at the provincial level (K-12), while the federal government supports Indigenous education and college funding, mandating schooling from ages five to sixteen.
*   **South Korea:** Is defined by high academic pressure rooted in Confucian and meritocratic values, where the CSAT is critical for elite university access, raising issues of associated costs.
*   **Saudi Arabia:** The state-run system is undergoing modernization under Tatweer to shift focus toward vocational and secular learning away from rote memorization.
*   **Mexico:** Features a centralized and secular public education system that contends with infrastructure deficits, low progression rates, and decentralization difficulties.
*   **New Zealand:** Offers free K-13 education in English and Māori under the NCEA framework while actively striving to reduce the achievement gap among Māori students.
*   **Japan:** Mandates completion of primary and secondary school before students proceed to specialized upper secondary tracks, characterized by intense university competition and reliance on private tutoring (*Juku*).
*   **Netherlands:** Governance involves both the Ministry and municipalities. Compulsory schooling starts at age five, and the system incorporates early tracking (e.g., Cito at age 12) while following the Bologna process.
*   **Bangladesh System Deep Dive:** The governance is multi-tiered; MOPME sets the curriculum, ten boards manage secondary exams (SSC/HSC), and UGC oversees higher education. The system is restricted by low public funding and poverty, despite available pathways like DTE.
*   **Poland:** The educational structure involves primary education (now eight years, following a 2017 reform from 6+3+3/4), leading to upper secondary tracks. Students can pursue general university tracks (*liceum*) via the "Matura," or vocational tracks (*technikum*). Poland adheres to the Bologna scheme (3-year Bachelor's, 2-year Master's) and uses a numeric grading system (2.0 to 5.0). Beyond its historical roots (Jagiellonian University, 1364), recent reforms mandate two foreign languages in lower secondary school. Furthermore, the 2017 reform, pushed by the PiS government, faced criticism for emphasizing Polish and Eurocentric narratives and increasing nationalism. In terms of safety, a 2006 incident prompted a "zero tolerance" reform, intended to enhance teacher status as civil servants and impose stricter penalties for violence, while also mandating the reporting of school violence.
