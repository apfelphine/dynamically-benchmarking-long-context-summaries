**Updated Summary of Education in Mexico, New Zealand, Japan, Saudi Arabia, Bangladesh, South Korea, Canada, Russia, the Czech Republic, Pakistan, and Poland:**

Mexico, New Zealand, Japan, Saudi Arabia, Bangladesh, South Korea, Canada, Russia, the Czech Republic, and Poland each exhibit distinct educational models shaped by historical, cultural, and political contexts. Mexico’s system reflects colonial and indigenous influences, emphasizing inclusion and bilingualism, though rural access and teacher quality remain challenges. The 2013 reform shifted control from teachers’ unions to the government, with high school offering general or vocational tracks. New Zealand integrates Māori knowledge and language into its national curriculum, promoting cultural inclusivity, but struggles with poor international assessment performance and high rates of school bullying. Japan emphasizes academic rigor and discipline, relying heavily on private academies ("juku") and a centralized curriculum, resulting in high student stress and mental health concerns despite strong performance in science and math. Saudi Arabia has achieved significant gender equity—women now comprise over 60% of university students and are increasingly active in public sectors—though ideological rigidity and a lack of marketable skills persist. Bangladesh features a rigid, exam-based structure with limited subject flexibility and high dropout rates, especially in rural areas; public spending ranges from 0.94% to 2.2% of GDP, and issues such as teacher absenteeism (15.5%) and poor infrastructure remain systemic. Despite literacy growth (from 51.77% to 74.66% between 2011 and 2022), rote learning and lack of outcome-based education persist. South Korea’s education is rooted in Confucian meritocracy, with a centralized, competitive system that supports economic development but contributes to intense academic pressure, social inequality, and high suicide rates. The 6-4-3-4 structure and emphasis on vocational training during industrialization reflect alignment with national economic goals. Since the post-war era, literacy has risen dramatically—from 22% in 1945 to nearly 93% by the 1980s—driven by strong government investment. Key reforms in the 1980s and 1990s promoted democratization, including the abolition of military training, legalization of teacher unions, and curriculum flexibility. In 2023, South Korea banned mobile phones in classrooms and corporal punishment, signaling a shift toward student well-being. The system is transitioning from academic to competency-based models to reduce pressure and improve holistic development, though elite access remains dominated by the CSAT and private institutions.  

Canada’s system is provincially managed, with variation across provinces in curriculum, structure, and duration. Compulsory education begins at age 5–7 and ends at 16–18, with kindergarten available nationwide and two-year programs in some regions. Secondary education concludes at Grade 12 in most provinces, except Quebec, where it ends at Grade 11. The system offers diverse pathways, including vocational training through community colleges and apprenticeships, leading to certificates, diplomas, or associate degrees. Canadian universities are globally recognized, with institutions like the University of Toronto, McGill, and UBC ranked internationally. Admissions emphasize academic performance, GPA, and extracurriculars, with competitive cutoffs (e.g., 90%). The system includes constitutional protections for religious and language-based schools, and equity initiatives such as identity-based data collection and Indigenous studies are widely implemented. Education spending accounts for approximately 5.2% of GDP, with performance assessed via PISA and national evaluations. Provincial differences in grade structures (e.g., British Columbia’s Middle Schools, Quebec’s blended secondary/post-secondary) reflect decentralized governance.  

Russia features a two-tiered structure: general education (preschool to secondary) and professional education (vocational, bachelor’s, specialist, master’s, postgraduate). Preschool enrollment has grown, though kindergartens remain scarce in major cities. Secondary education is compulsory for nine years (Grades 1–9), with optional eleven-year programs. Students aged 15–18 may leave school with parental approval, and expulsion is possible from age 15. The Unified State Examination (USE) replaced regional exams to ensure fairness, though grading transparency remains a concern. All secondary certificates are nationally standardized. At the postgraduate level, Russia maintains a unique system with the Candidate of Sciences (Ph.D.-equivalent) and Doctor of Sciences (habilitation), requiring years of original research, especially in science and engineering. In social sciences and military fields, postgraduate training leads to command-level appointments without formal degrees. A 2021 law formalized science outreach initiatives, but critics argue it enables censorship and violates constitutional rights. “Patriotic education” is mandated in schools, including mandatory “Important Conversations” lessons, which some describe as militaristic and have led to student investigations. The system supports linguistic diversity with over 80 ethnic languages, though disparities exist—languages like Abaza and Dargin have limited or no representation in schools.  

The Czech Republic emphasizes language proficiency, academic rigor, and clear pathways to university or vocational careers. Elementary education spans nine grades in two stages: Grades 1–5 focus on core subjects, Grades 6–9 expand with optional foreign language tracks. Secondary education requires passage of CERMAT exams (math, Czech, or English), with 12.3% failing in 2025. Students pursue vocational schools (2–3 years), professional schools (4 years), lyceums (general), grammar schools (university prep), or special schools. The *maturita* exam is mandatory for university entry and includes Czech, literature, math/foreign language, and school-specific subjects. Tertiary education includes Charles University (founded 1348) and Palacký University (1576), with public universities offering free Czech-language education for first-time students under 26. Degrees follow a structured hierarchy: Bachelor (3 years), Master (2–5 years), and PhD (PhDr/JUDr/RNDr), with public institutions generally regarded as higher quality. The academic year runs from September to June, with major holidays including Christmas, Easter, and summer holidays (62+ days). Academic titles are increasingly phased out in favor of the Ph.D.  

Pakistan’s system includes formal, non-formal, and informal stages. Secondary education features British O-level and A-level systems, with A-levels popular in private institutions. Tertiary education offers professional degrees (MBBS, BE, LLB) and undergraduate degrees (BA, BSc, BBA) in Pass or Honours tracks, with Honours increasingly replacing Pass. Teacher education remains knowledge-based and underqualified. Madrassas, widespread and serving poor families, focus on Islamic studies with some secular content. Gender disparity remains a critical issue: women face lower enrollment, literacy (42% vs. men’s 67%), and academic outcomes. Male dominance in teaching (2:1 ratio) and leadership persists, hindered by cultural norms, unsafe infrastructure, and limited access. Despite progress since the 1990s, the Millennium Development Goal to eliminate gender disparity by 2015 was not fully achieved. Structural barriers in STEM and medical fields, including a declared unconstitutional gender quota, persist. Low-cost private schools (LCPSs) may improve access but often favor male students. Transgender individuals face marginalization despite the 2018 Protection of Rights Act. Education quality declines due to outdated curricula, teacher shortages, poor infrastructure, and lack of relevance. Technical and vocational education (TVET) is vital but faces challenges in curriculum, teacher quality, and equity. Special education teachers report higher emotional intelligence and life satisfaction. During the pandemic, Teleschool and AIOU provided emergency access, though rural and poor students remain underserved. As of 2023, 14 districts have literacy rates of 75% or above (e.g., Islamabad, Karachi Central), with 13 districts having youth literacy rates of 85% or above. Pakistan’s overall literacy rate (60.65%) is the second lowest in South Asia, behind only Afghanistan, due to poverty, patriarchal structures, and systemic issues.  

**Poland’s education system is structured into compulsory education up to age 18, following an 8-year primary school (introduced in 2017, replacing the previous 6+3 system) and a 4-year junior secondary stage, with upper secondary education beginning after primary school. Students can pursue general education in liceum (leading to Matura for higher education), vocational training in technikum or zasadnicza szkoła zawodowa, or specialized education in profiled licea or schools for disabled students. Tertiary education follows the Bologna model: three-year bachelor’s, two-year master’s (with exceptions for medicine and pharmacy). University grading uses a 2.0–5.0 scale (3.0 as minimum passing), with semester-based evaluations. Foreign language instruction has been expanded, requiring two languages (e.g., English and another) in lower secondary schools, with exams influencing upper secondary admissions. Historically, Poland has a strong educational tradition, marked by the founding of Jagiellonian University (1364) and the Commission of National Education (1773). Compulsory education was introduced in 1919 and expanded post-WWII, reducing illiteracy to 1.2% by 1978. A major 1999 reform restructured the system into 6+3+3 stages, improving performance in reading, science, and math in OECD rankings. In 2017, a major reform shifted the structure from 6+3+3/4 to 8+4/5 years of schooling, emphasizing Polish and European history, political leaders, and nobility while downplaying social classes and communism. Additionally, a 2006 "zero tolerance" reform was introduced after a school-related suicide, granting teachers civil servant status and imposing penalties on those who fail to report violence, reflecting a growing focus on school safety and teacher accountability.**
