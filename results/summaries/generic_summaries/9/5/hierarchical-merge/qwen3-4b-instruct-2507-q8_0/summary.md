# **Merged Summary: Educational Systems in Mexico, New Zealand, Japan, Saudi Arabia, the Netherlands, Bangladesh, Russia, the Czech Republic, Poland, and Pakistan (2024–2025)**

## **Mexico, New Zealand, Japan, Saudi Arabia, the Netherlands, and Bangladesh: Diverse Models with Equity and Access Challenges**

Mexico offers an inclusive but unequal education system rooted in pre-Hispanic and colonial traditions. It features compulsory primary through preparatoria (1–12), with dual academic and vocational pathways. Despite high primary attendance (97.5% by 2020) and literacy gains, only 45% complete secondary education and just 25% reach higher education. Rural disparities, weak teacher training, and low indigenous enrollment (1–3%) persist. The system follows a U.S.-style higher education structure (Licenciatura, Maestría, Doctorado) aligned with the Bologna Process, supported by over 150 international schools offering English-medium curricula. Tensions between secular state schools and Catholic institutions continue to influence policy.

New Zealand’s system evolved from pre-European *wānanga* traditions into a national, secular, and free structure. It includes primary (Years 1–8) and secondary (Years 9–13), culminating in the National Certificate of Educational Achievement (NCEA). The national curriculum emphasizes eight learning areas, with Māori language revitalization efforts such as *kōhanga reo* and *kura kaupapa Māori* leading to official recognition. Māori students in immersion programs perform better, yet disparities in math and numeracy persist. Challenges include poor foundational numeracy, widespread bullying, and inadequate teacher training. Funding is tied to student numbers and socio-economic decile, with private schools relying on tuition and facing limited support.

Japan maintains a highly centralized, exam-driven system with nearly 99% completion through lower secondary education. Public schools are tuition-free, emphasizing core subjects and standardized testing. The *shū to fuka* grading system and widespread use of private cram schools (*juku*) contribute to intense academic pressure, bullying, and mental health issues—particularly suicide. The 1980s "Yutori" reforms aimed to reduce stress but were reversed in 2011. Since then, students have shown improved performance in math and science (TIMSS). The academic year runs from April with long breaks; universities follow a two-semester structure.

Saudi Arabia’s system is grounded in Islamic values, with Quranic studies and national identity central in early education. Compulsory primary education (ages 6–12) has expanded to include stronger science and vocational training through reforms like *Tatweer* (≈$2 billion), reducing rote learning and promoting critical thinking. Textbook reforms in 2021 removed extremist and anti-Semitic content, enhancing inclusivity. Women’s participation in higher education has surged—60% of university students are female—though labor force participation remains low at 14.4%. The government supports STEM through programs like the Summer Enrichment Program (SEP), serving over 6,000 students. Challenges remain in teacher training, curriculum differentiation, and special education support.

The Netherlands operates a decentralized, student-centered system under municipal oversight. Compulsory education begins at age 5 and ends at 18, with students choosing from three secondary pathways: **VMBO** (pre-vocational), **HAVO** (general), or **VWO** (university-preparatory). These lead to higher education at **HBO** or **WO** universities. The system follows a Bologna-aligned three-cycle structure (bachelor’s, master’s, PhD) using ECTS credits, with only NVAO-accredited programs receiving government funding. Emphasis is on individual development, critical thinking, and inclusive support. Despite early tracking and socioeconomic disparities, it demonstrates strong labor market alignment and academic resilience.

Bangladesh’s system is access-driven but faces structural challenges. Pre-primary to tertiary education is managed by central and regional bodies, with government schools under the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education. The PEC and JSC exams have been discontinued, and the government plans to build 1,000 primary schools in char areas. Secondary education is administered by 10 regional Boards, conducting SSC and HSC exams. Tertiary education includes 43 public, 103 private, and 2 international universities. Technical and vocational education is overseen by TEB and DTE. Despite poor teacher performance (15.5% absenteeism), high dropout rates, and outdated curricula, gender parity is achieved in primary and secondary education. Literacy has improved from 51.77% (2011) to 74.66% (2022). Refugee education (e.g., UNICEF’s 1,600 centers) and NGO-led non-formal education (e.g., BRAC) play key roles in equity.

## **Russia, the Czech Republic, Poland, and Pakistan: Performance, Reform, and Structural Barriers**

Russia’s secondary education is compulsory for nine years (grades 1–9), with a standardized curriculum and fixed grading (2–5). Students may leave at 15–18 with parental consent or enter vocational training (three years). The Unified State Examination (USE) is mandatory for university entry, replacing inconsistent regional exams. Tertiary education follows a Bologna-aligned structure, though critics argue the transition remains superficial. Postgraduate degrees—Candidate of Sciences (2–4 years) and Doctor of Sciences (up to 10 years)—retain Soviet roots, requiring substantial research output. A 2024 law mandates Russian language proficiency for foreign students, promoting national integration. Special education is supported, but teacher shortages and equity issues—especially for students with combined disabilities—remain. "Patriotic education" has drawn criticism for infringing on constitutional rights.

The Czech Republic offers a well-structured, performance-driven system. Primary education spans Grades 1–9, with students transitioning to grammar schools, vocational schools, or lyceums. The **maturita exam** is required for university admission and includes Czech, world literature, and either math or a foreign language. Higher education features institutions like Charles and Palacký Universities, with public universities offering free Czech-language study until age 26; private universities charge €2,000–3,000 annually. Degrees follow a bachelor’s (3 years), master’s (2–5 years), and PhD structure, with final exams and theses required. The academic year runs from September to June, with key holidays including Christmas and Easter. Special and practical schools now emphasize self-sufficiency and integration into mainstream education.

Poland’s system is reformed and equitable, with compulsory education from age 6 to 18. Since 2017, primary education has been expanded to eight years (Grades 1–8), replacing the old six-year primary and three-year gimnazjum structure. Upper secondary offers general (liceum), vocational (technikum), and profiled pathways, with students preparing for the Matura exam or vocational certification. Tertiary education follows the Bologna model: three-year bachelor’s and two-year master’s, with exceptions for medicine and pharmacy. Grading uses a 2.0–5.0 scale (3.0 is passing), assessed semestrially. Foreign language education is mandatory, influencing upper secondary admissions. A 2006 "zero tolerance" policy granted teachers civil servant status and increased penalties for violence, though it has drawn legal and ethical scrutiny. The system benefits from strong historical foundations and performance improvements in OECD rankings, supported by over 439 international schools.

Pakistan’s education system is English-inspired, with a primary completion rate of 67.5%. Students progress through pre-school, junior (Years 1–5), and middle school (Years 6–8), then enter secondary education with SSC-I and SSC-II exams. Streams include pre-medical, pre-engineering, and humanities. Alternative qualifications like GCE and IGCSE are widely used. Technical education (TSC, DAE) offers vocational training, with DAE equivalent to the 12th grade. Tertiary education includes professional degrees (MBBS, BE, LLB) and general degrees (BA, BSc), with Honours degrees replacing Pass degrees. However, gender disparities persist: women have lower enrollment, literacy (42% vs. 67% for men), and access, especially in STEM and leadership. Male dominance in teaching and leadership continues, and women face systemic barriers in medical and professional careers. Cultural, economic, and familial norms, along with poor infrastructure and teacher shortages, hinder progress. Textbooks often promote extremist narratives, omitting key historical events like the Holocaust and reinforcing ideological bias. Despite improvements in institutional performance (e.g., NUST ranked 91st in QS Asia), low education expenditure (≈2% of GDP), outdated curricula, and political instability remain major obstacles. Distance learning (e.g., AIOU) improves access, especially for women in remote areas, but faces challenges in continuity and teacher compensation.

---

**Overall Observations:**  
Across all countries, education systems reflect deep historical, cultural, and political influences. While many have adopted international standards (e.g., Bologna Process), structural inequities—particularly in access, gender, and teacher quality—remain persistent. Systems vary from highly centralized (Japan, Russia) to decentralized (Netherlands, Czech Republic), with performance outcomes shaped by policy, funding, and societal values. Vocational and inclusive education are gaining traction in several nations, signaling a shift toward equity and relevance. However, challenges such as mental health, teacher training, curriculum rigidity, and gender disparities underscore the need for sustained investment, reform, and culturally responsive policies to achieve inclusive, high-quality education for all.
