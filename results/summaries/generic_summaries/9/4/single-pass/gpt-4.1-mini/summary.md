# Education System Summaries by Country

---

## Japan
Education in Japan began in the 6th century influenced by Chinese culture, emphasizing Buddhist and Confucian teachings. The Edo period introduced samurai schools and terakoya for merchants, with a rise in literacy. Post-Meiji Restoration, Western educational models were adopted, compulsory education extended, and universities established. After WWII, reforms modeled after the American system decentralized education, promoted coeducation, and extended compulsory schooling, later facing challenges such as exam pressure and stress, leading to reforms like “Yutori education.”

The academic year starts in April with levels including six years of elementary (compulsory), three years of lower secondary (also compulsory), and three years of upper secondary education. The post-secondary system comprises universities, junior and vocational colleges, with strong emphasis on entrance examinations often supported by private cram schools ("juku"). Special needs and inclusive education programs have developed gradually since the 1990s. Extracurricular activities are significant due to competitive exam culture. Criticisms include high student stress, bullying incidents, limited fostering of independent thinkers, and concerns over excessive reliance on cram schools.

## Mexico
Mexico's basic education is divided into primaria (grades 1-6), secundaria (7-9), and preparatoria (10-12). Primary and secundaria are compulsory, while preparatoria became recently compulsory. Vocational and distance education options exist at secundaria level. Educational integration since 1993 aims to include special needs students in regular classrooms, but challenges persist in role clarity, accessibility, and teacher training.

The quality of education faces criticism due to infrastructure deficits, low completion rates (only 45% finish secondary), and below-average OECD performance in key subjects. Reforms initiated in 2013 sought to reduce union control and improve governance. Higher education follows a US-like model with bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees; however, part-time public university enrollment is limited. Intercultural Universities address indigenous enrollment disparities. As of 2015, around 151 international schools operate to cater to diverse curricula.

## Netherlands
Education is coordinated by the Ministry of Education with municipal involvement. Compulsory education begins at age five, ending between 16-18. Public, special (religious), and general-special schools receive equal government funding. Early education (“basisschool”) covers ages 4-12 with standardized tests (e.g., Cito test) guiding secondary placement.

Secondary education divides into VMBO (vocational, 4 years), HAVO (5 years, general higher education preparation), and VWO (6 years, university-preparatory) with flexible movement between tracks. Vocational (MBO), professional (HBO), and research university (WO) pathways follow secondary education, with distinct curricula and credits aligned with the Bologna process. Accreditation is managed nationally.

Criticisms include early tracking of students around age 12, potentially misstating abilities, and declining international performance. As of 2015, the country had 152 international schools offering English-medium or other international curricula.

## New Zealand
Education evolved from Māori wānanga to a national compulsory system established in 1878. Early childhood education is diverse and licensed but not government-owned. Primary education runs Years 1–8; secondary is Years 9–13. The curriculum is national and bilingual options are offered, including Māori-medium programs.

Three school types exist: state (government-funded), state-integrated (former private with “special character”), and private schools. Enrolment schemes define “home zones” for state schools. Māori language education has revived since the 1980s with immersion schools (kura kaupapa).

Tertiary education includes universities and vocational training regulated by a unified qualifications framework. Funding combines government subsidies and student loans/allowances. Educational standards face challenges in mathematics and inequities in achievement, notably for Māori and Pacific students. Bullying is a significant concern with governmental responses ongoing.

## South Korea
Rooted in Confucian values and the seodang system, Korea’s education system shifted heavily during Japanese occupation and post-liberation to centralized, meritocratic structures emphasizing academic rigor and universal basic education. The system includes six years primary, three years middle, and three years high school (academic or vocational tracks), with fierce competition for university admission driving societal “education fever.”

Vocational education, once stigmatized, is being revitalized through “Meister Schools,” apprenticeship-based vocational training inspired by German systems. Higher education offers bachelor's through doctoral degrees, with university admission heavily reliant on national college entrance exams (CSAT).

English education is emphasized, often supplemented by private tutoring (“hagwons”). Criticisms include extreme academic pressure, social stigma against vocational education, mental health concerns, and a mismatch between education and job markets, though reforms aim to foster competency-based learning.

## Saudi Arabia
Educational origins link to Islamic teachings via kuttabs. Modern education began expanding after 1926 with the establishment of ministries and universities. Education is free and centrally managed, with a focus on Islamic studies alongside sciences and languages. Girls’ education began in the 1960s under separate administration.

Compulsory primary education lasts six years, followed by three years each of intermediate and secondary education, including specialized and vocational tracks. Higher education expanded rapidly since 1957 and is funded by the state, with a large female student population, especially in humanities and education.

Special education and gifted programs have been developed recently, focusing on inclusion and enrichment. Private schools exist but face infrastructure and regulatory challenges. International schools, including community-based for expatriates, operate with varying oversight.

Criticism targets heavy rote learning, religious indoctrination, lack of critical thinking, and intolerance in curricula. Recent reforms seek modernization and curriculum moderation.

## Russia
Russian education comprises general (pre-school, primary, basic, and secondary education) and professional levels (vocational and higher education). Nine-year compulsory education was extended to eleven years in many areas. Primary education begins at age 6 or 7. Secondary education offers specialized schools and vocational options.

Higher education traditionally followed a specialist diploma model (5-6 years) and is transitioning to Bologna-process-style bachelor’s and master’s degrees. Postgraduate scientific degrees include Candidate of Sciences and Doctor of Sciences, akin to PhD and habilitation. University admission increasingly uses standardized unified state exams.

Special education includes schools for physical and mental disabilities with varying degrees of integration. Concerns include shortages of resources, regional disparities, and controversies over censorship and patriotic education. Russia has many schools offering instruction in numerous minority languages.

## Bangladesh
Education is divided into pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (9–12), and tertiary levels. Instruction may be in Bengali or English. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board governs standardized tests and curricula. Students choose academic streams (Arts/Humanities, Commerce, Science) from grade 9.

Government and private schools coexist, alongside madrasa (Qawmi and Alia) systems offering Islamic education. Technical and vocational education is overseen by dedicated boards and institutions. Challenges include infrastructure, teacher quality, outdated curricula, and gender disparities—though primary and secondary gender parity has improved. Literacy rose to 74.66% in 2022.

International and refugee education programs exist for minority groups. Grading uses GPA on a 5-point scale with optional subjects affecting scores.

## Pakistan
The education system mirrors the British structure with primary (years 1–5), middle (6–8), secondary (9–12), and tertiary levels. Primary completion is about 67.5%. Secondary education leads to SSC and HSSC certificates after standardized exams, with streams like pre-medical and pre-engineering. Vocational and technical education is growing via boards and institutions.

Tertiary education includes bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees, but issues of quality, gender disparity, and access persist. Madrasas provide religious education, especially where public schools are unavailable or inadequate.

Gender disparity is significant, with lower female enrollment and literacy. Teacher training suffers from shortages and outdated methods. COVID-19 prompted distance learning challenges due to technology access gaps.

Notable alumni include Nobel laureate Abdus Salam and several distinguished scientists. Public education funding hovers near 2% of GDP, with ambitious government targets remaining unrealized.

## Canada
Education is provincially managed, with compulsory schooling generally from ages 5 (kindergarten) to 17-18 (grade 12), varying by province. Programs include early childhood, elementary, secondary, and post-secondary education (universities and vocational institutions). French and Indigenous language education are supported constitutionally.

Post-secondary education offers bachelor's, master's, doctoral degrees, as well as diplomas and certificates from colleges and vocational schools. Admissions weigh academic records, standardized grades, and extracurriculars. Canada has globally ranked universities, including University of Toronto and McGill.

Private and religious schools coexist with public schools, with varied funding and differing policies across provinces. Efforts target gaps in achievement and inclusion, with debates on equity data collection and standardized testing.

## Poland
Children start primary school at age 6-7; primary education now lasts 8 years after recent reforms abolished the 3-year junior high (“gimnazjum”). Secondary education includes general secondary schools (liceum) and vocational/technical schools, culminating in the Matura examination for university entry.

Tertiary education aligns with the Bologna process: bachelor’s (3 years), master’s (2 years), and doctoral programs. Academic grading uses a 2.0–5.0 scale. Foreign languages commonly studied include English, German, and French. Historical education roots date back to the 12th century, with the world’s first education ministry established in 1773.

Recent reforms shifted schooling structures and curriculum content with debates on nationalism and historical perspectives. Responses to school violence include legal measures increasing penalties for offenses against teachers.

## Czech Republic
Elementary school lasts nine years in two stages: first five years with a single teacher, followed by four years with subject specialists. Secondary education requires passing entrance exams; options include vocational, professional schools, lyceums, grammar schools, and academies.

The “maturita” exam is the standardized final qualification necessary for university. Higher education includes public and private universities and professional tertiary schools. University programs range from 2–6 years for bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees.

The school year runs from September to June with holidays around national events and typical Western holidays. Special schools and integrated education support students with developmental disabilities.
