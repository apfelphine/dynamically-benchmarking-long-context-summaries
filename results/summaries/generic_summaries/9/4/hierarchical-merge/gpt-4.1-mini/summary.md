# Education in East and Southeast Asia

**South Korea** has developed a centralized, merit-based education system critical to its development, consisting of 6 years primary, 3 years middle, 3 years high school (academic or vocational), and 4 years university. It emphasizes rigorous academics and English proficiency, with the CSAT exam governing university admissions. Despite high secondary completion, intense competition and private tutoring place mental health strains on students. Vocational tracks face social stigma despite labor market demand, prompting reforms balancing academic rigor and welfare.

**Japan** requires nine years of free compulsory schooling with focus on math, science, Japanese, and English. The system is internationally respected but contends with student stress and bullying. Inclusive education supports students with disabilities, and the academic year begins in April. Curriculum rigor and grading standards are maintained amid ongoing efforts to enhance well-being.

**Bangladesh** maintains a multi-tiered education system managed by various authorities, with instruction in Bengali and English alongside government and private madrasahs. Secondary education offers Arts, Commerce, Science, and vocational streams, supported by initiatives aimed at improving female enrollment and literacy, including stipends and food programs. Literacy rates have risen significantly, yet challenges persist in teacher absenteeism, poverty, curriculum reform, and rural disparities.

**Pakistan** struggles with low literacy (~60%) marked by gender and regional gaps. Public education faces underfunding, quality issues, and cultural barriers, especially for females and rural populations. The system includes religious madrassas and vocational training, with several universities gaining regional recognition. Efforts continue to address these inequalities and expand higher education capacity.

# Education in Europe

**The Netherlands** mandates education from ages 5 to 18, progressing from primary school to stratified secondary tracks—vocational (VMBO), general (HAVO), and pre-university (VWO)—with mobility between tracks encouraged. Quality assurance via inspections exists alongside challenges such as early selection and grade repetition. Post-secondary options include vocational MBO, applied sciences HBO, and research universities WO complying with the Bologna Process. Over 150 international English-language schools highlight the system’s cultural diversity.

**Russia** provides nine years of compulsory education followed by two senior secondary years and opportunities for vocational or academic post-secondary study. The Unified State Examination standardizes university admissions, mitigating corruption. Higher education aligns with Bologna standards, though bachelor degrees often necessitate master’s for employment readiness. Inclusive education supports disabled and minority language students. Current policies promote patriotic education, quality improvement, and foreign student language requirements, though institutional consolidation may reduce access in remote areas.

**Poland** restructured its system to an 8-year primary education followed by upper secondary options: 4-year liceum, 5-year technical, and 3-year vocational schools, with compulsory education until age 18. Students typically learn two foreign languages, and university admission depends on the Matura exam. Higher education offers bachelor’s and master’s degrees and emphasizes national history and minority languages.

**The Czech Republic** features a 9-year elementary school split into phases with gradual foreign language introduction. Secondary education offers vocational schools, professional schools with maturita exams, lyceums, and grammar schools. Since 2016, entrance exams in math or languages are nationally administered. Special education promotes integration despite overrepresentation of Romani children in specialized classes. Public universities provide tuition-free Czech-language programs, while private schools charge fees and are generally regarded as lesser quality.

# Education in Oceania

**New Zealand** comprises 13 year levels divided into primary (Years 1–8) and secondary (Years 9–13), with national curricula in English and Māori. Senior students pursue NCEA certifications or alternatives like Cambridge and IB. Schooling types include state, state-integrated (fee-based), private, and homeschooling overseen by elected boards. Māori immersion programs aid language revitalization. Challenges include achievement gaps for Māori and Pacific students, declining math performance, and bullying. The system reflects a strong commitment to inclusivity and free secular education.

# Education in the Middle East

**Saudi Arabia** operates a predominantly government-run system grounded in Islamic tradition while incorporating modern curricula emphasizing STEM and vocational skills. Enrollment exceeds 90% through pre-primary to secondary levels with separate general and vocational tracks. Female education has advanced significantly, now comprising the majority in universities. Literacy among youth is over 98%. Reforms target teacher quality, inclusivity, reduced rote learning, and expanded private and international schooling under ministry regulation.

# Education in North America

**Canada’s** education is provincially governed, leading to variations in school starting age, grade structures, and compulsory education length (generally until ages 16–18). Secondary education usually ends at Grade 12 (Grade 11 in Quebec) with options such as IB and Advanced Placement. Public funding equals about 5.2% of GDP. Efforts include improving Indigenous education and collecting data on student identities to address achievement gaps. Post-secondary institutions offer diverse degree pathways, with some private schooling focused largely in Quebec. The system facilitates international student pathways but faces criticism for underemphasizing arts and soft skills.

This synthesis highlights global education structures shaped by regional traditions, economic capacities, and cultural values, with ongoing reforms addressing equity, quality, inclusion, and labor market alignment.
