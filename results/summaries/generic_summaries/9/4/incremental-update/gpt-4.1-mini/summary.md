# Updated Summary of Education in Japan, Mexico, the Netherlands, New Zealand, South Korea, Saudi Arabia, Russia, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Canada, Poland, and the Czech Republic

## Education in Japan  
Japan’s education system, rooted in 6th-century samurai schooling, follows a 6-3-3-4 structure with an academic year from April to March. Curricula encompass academics, arts, physical and moral education, with compulsory English since 2011. Upper secondary education offers academic, technical, and vocational tracks. Inclusion employs Resource Rooms and legal frameworks, though bullying and mental health issues persist. University admission hinges on competitive exams, fostering a large private tutoring industry. About 30–40 international schools operate as miscellaneous schools.

## Education in Mexico  
Mexico’s education reflects prehispanic and colonial influences, achieving nearly 95% literacy by 2018. It includes preschool; compulsory primary and junior high; and high school with academic, vocational, and specialty tracks. Special needs education mainly uses separate CAM schools with limited training. Indigenous and rural access remains constrained. Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, dominated by UNAM and Monterrey Institute. A national free textbook program promotes cultural identity. Numerous English-medium international schools serve diverse populations.

## Education in the Netherlands  
Following 1963 reforms, Dutch secondary education features simplified streams and equal funding for public and religious schools. Elementary education uses assessments to place students in VMBO, HAVO, or VWO tracks; adult education (VAVO) grants diplomas. Support teams address health and social issues; staggered holidays reduce overcrowding. Early tracking raises concerns about gifted and disadvantaged students amid declining international test scores. Postsecondary pathways include vocational (MBO), professional (HBO), and research universities (WO), all Bologna-aligned and NVAO-accredited. There are 152 mainly English-medium international schools.

## Education in New Zealand  
New Zealand’s system integrates Māori oral traditions and European influence, with free secular compulsory education since 1878. The bicultural early childhood curriculum (Te Whāriki) supports Māori identity. School spans Years 1–13 (ages 5–19), with compulsory schooling ages 6–16 and a school year from January to December. Māori-medium schools serve 2.2% of Māori students, who achieve higher results though 60% lack access. From Year 11, students pursue NCEA, with some schools offering Cambridge or IB curricula. Most schools are state or state-integrated; enrollment zones raise equity concerns. Persistent achievement gaps exist for Māori and Pacific students alongside widespread bullying. Post-school education ranges from community to postgraduate levels under the NZ Qualifications Framework, with mixed funding and centralized teacher pay.

## Education in South Korea  
South Korea’s Confucian meritocracy roots evolved from colonial restrictions to near-universal literacy and vocational training after 1945. Education covers infant to high school (Grades 0–12), emphasizing academics, moral education, and English; mobile phones banned since 2023, corporal punishment ended in 2011. Secondary completion nears 98%, with general and special-purpose high school tracks; vocational and Meister schools integrate academics and guaranteed employment. A 2025 credit system will expand course choices. Higher education is 87.3% private; admissions rely on the CSAT exam. English instruction shifted toward communicative proficiency since 1994. Widespread private tutoring (hagwons) supports intense exam culture. Challenges include vocational stigma, academic pressure affecting health and creativity, teacher dissatisfaction, school violence, limited extracurricular activities, and health initiatives like coffee bans.

## Education in Saudi Arabia  
Saudi education, evolving from Islamic kuttabs, has rapidly expanded since 1926, with the first government girls’ school in 1964 and King Saud University founded in 1957. Free education administered by the Ministry of Education and Technical and Vocational Training Corporation emphasizes literacy, gender inclusion, Islamic values, technical skills, and ICT. Kindergarten is low-enrollment and non-compulsory; primary education (ages 6–12) is compulsory, covering Arabic, Islamic studies, math, science, arts, and mandatory English since 2021; about 9% attend private schools. Intermediate and secondary education (three years each) include general and specialized tracks with consolidated religious studies. Twenty-four government universities offer up to PhD degrees, with women predominating enrollment and staff. Gifted education focuses on STEM via the Mawhiba foundation. Special education employs trained teachers with uneven facilities. Private education is tightly regulated; over 200 international schools mainly serve foreign communities. Literacy has improved but regional and gender disparities persist. Reforms seek to modernize teaching, reduce rote learning, enhance English, and secularize curricula by removing intolerant content.

## Education in Russia  
Russia’s system includes general (preschool to secondary) and professional (vocational to postgraduate) education. Preschool enrollment rose from 67% in 1999 to 84% in 2005 but declined by 2020 due to demographics and facility shortages. Secondary schooling covers elementary, middle, and senior stages; compulsory schooling extends to nine or 11 years depending on region. Fixed curricula use a 5-step grading scale; vocational schools offer secondary equivalence after nine years. The Unified State Examination (USE), mandatory since 2009, standardizes university admissions but faces transparency criticisms. Special education includes schools for physical disabilities and segregated provision for mental disabilities, with limited integration. Tertiary attainment exceeds 50%, transitioning from Soviet-era 5–6 year diplomas to Bologna-aligned bachelor-master degrees, though standalone bachelors are undervalued and student mobility is low. Postgraduate education retains Soviet-style Candidate and Doctor of Sciences degrees requiring original research. Military postgraduate education runs parallel. Since 2021, a Science Outreach Law regulates outreach amid censorship concerns. Post-2020, “patriotic education” promotes national identity and traditional values through programs like "Important Conversations," drawing criticism for militarization and dissent suppression. Minority languages such as Tatar and Yakut are taught in thousands of schools. Starting December 2024, foreign citizens must pass a Russian language exam to enroll in schools.

## Education in Bangladesh  
Bangladesh’s education spans pre-primary to higher education under multiple boards, with primary education overseen by specialized ministries and secondary/higher education by the Ministry of Education and DSHE. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board distributes over 347 million free textbooks. Primary public exams (PEC, JSC) were discontinued in 2023. Secondary education involves ten regional boards conducting SSC and HSC exams. Higher education comprises 43 public, 103 private, and 2 international universities; public universities have competitive admissions and government subsidies, while private institutions require regulatory compliance. Technical and vocational education offers diplomas enabling university entry.

Alternative systems include government-subsidized Alia and private Qawmi madrasahs serving millions, NGO-run non-formal schools mainly for vulnerable groups, and limited NGO-UNICEF-supported refugee education. Curricula include multi-religious education, often insufficient for non-Muslim students. Passing requires 33%, GPA on a 5.0 scale. Government initiatives promote compulsory primary education, free girls’ education up to class 10, stipends, and free textbooks. Challenges encompass poor human resource management, 15.5% teacher absenteeism, limited tertiary capacity, rising costs, poverty effects, rural dropout/repetition, and persistent rural gender disparities despite urban gender parity. Literacy rose from 51.77% (2011) to 74.66% (2022). Issues include rote learning, elitism, and lack of outcome-based education, critical for development in densely populated Bangladesh.

## Education in Pakistan  
Pakistan’s system, modeled after the English system, spans primary, secondary, and tertiary education. Primary completion is 67.5%, with secondary lasting four years and SSC exams covering sciences, arts, commerce, GCE/O/A Levels, and vocational tracks. Spending is about 2–2.2% of GDP, mainly on lower levels, with unfulfilled targets aiming for 7%. Challenges include low attendance (66%), uneven quality, rural and gender disparities, and limited subsidies. Punjab faces an infrastructure crisis with unsafe school buildings.

Tertiary education includes two- to four-year bachelor’s degrees, masters, and MPhil/PhD with research focus. Teacher education lacks qualified staff, funding, and updated curricula, often relying on rote learning. Reflective methods aim to improve human rights awareness.

Non-formal education includes vocational centers, apprenticeships, NGO and corporate initiatives. Madrassas provide free education with Quran memorization focus, sometimes promoting militant Islam, outnumbering public schools in poor rural areas. 1980s Islamization entrenched religious content; resistance to scientific theories persists, with misconceptions among teachers. Literacy rates among religious minorities are generally below national averages except for Ahmadis.

Education is intertwined with religion: textbooks often depict Jews and Israel negatively, omit Judaism and the Holocaust, sometimes praise Hitler without referencing Nazi crimes, and promote ideological narratives framing jihad morally and marginalizing minorities. Curriculum emphasizes Pakistan’s Muslim leadership role and traditional gender roles.

Literacy improved from 17.9% (1951) to 60.65% (2023), with male literacy at 68% and female at 52.84%. Urban literacy (74.09%) exceeds rural (51.56%). Provincial rates vary: Punjab (~66.25%), Sindh (~57.54%), Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (~51.09%), Balochistan (~42.01%). Districts like Islamabad and Azad Jammu & Kashmir have higher rates. Youth literacy is higher, with some districts exceeding 85%. Mean schooling years rose from 2.28 (1990) to 5.16 (2018), highest in Islamabad and Azad Jammu & Kashmir, slower in Balochistan and FATA.

Pakistan’s literacy is second lowest in South Asia, impacted by poverty, feudalism, gender disparities, poor quality schools, teacher absenteeism, and weak governance. It lags behind Malaysia (89%) and Indonesia (90%), reflecting higher poverty and inequality.

Internationally, Pakistan had 439 international schools (2015); many students pursue higher education in China via bilateral agreements.

Tertiary institutions are costly and often misaligned with labor market needs. Technical and vocational education struggles with access and management. Special education teachers report higher job satisfaction; teacher well-being correlates with workplace mood. COVID-19 accelerated distance learning via Teleschool, limited by internet access. Allama Iqbal Open University leads flexible learning; digital tools assist curriculum and outcomes.

### Notable Alumni  
Alumni include Nobel laureate physicist Abdus Salam; chemist Atta-ur-Rahman; neurosurgeon Ayub Ommaya; Human Development Index co-developer Mahbub-ul-Haq; mathematician Ismat Beg; software prodigy Arfa Abdul Karim Randhawa; brain-computer interface pioneer Naweed Syed; astrophysicist Nergis Mavalvala; and techno-entrepreneur Muhammad Irfan-Maqsood.

## Education in Canada  
Canada’s education is provincially governed with local school boards administering publicly funded schools following provincial curricula; Alberta also permits independent charter schools. Provincial differences exist in grade groupings and terminology (e.g., middle schools, junior secondary schools). Quebec’s college system overlaps secondary and post-secondary education. Graduation requirements typically span grades 10–12, varying by province. Each province and territory has its own education ministry or department.

Compulsory education starts between ages 5 and 7, lasting until 16 to 18. Kindergarten is typically one year at age 5, with some two-year pre-K programs. Secondary usually ends at Grade 12 (Grade 11 in Quebec). Advanced programs like IB and AP are widely available. Alternative education exists for suspended or expelled students, and adults may attend specialized secondary schools.

Education stages include early childhood (ages 3–6), elementary (Grades 1–6), intermediate (Grades 7–9), and secondary (Grades 10–12), with provincial variation.

Post-secondary education divides into universities and vocational colleges. Universities specialize in research, offer undergrad and graduate degrees, with admission based on senior secondary grades and other criteria for selective programs. Vocational education is delivered through community colleges and technical institutes with accessible two-year programs offering certificates, diplomas, or associate degrees in skilled trades and technical careers. Apprenticeships combine classroom and practical training, leading to national certification.

About 6% of Grade 10 students attend private schools, mainly in Quebec, achieving higher academic results influenced by expectations. Private universities, historically religious, now operate under provincial legislation amid debates over access, funding, and faith-based policies. Funding for religious schools varies; Ontario publicly funds Catholic schools, while other provinces partially fund various faith-based or charter schools differently.

Canada’s university sector features research-intensive institutions, denominational universities, liberal arts colleges, art schools, online universities, military academies, and those serving rural communities. Education spending is about 5.2% of GDP, with a 51% tertiary completion rate among young adults. Publicly funded religious and minority language schools are constitutionally protected. Indigenous education programs exist but remain regionally limited. Concerns include grade inflation, lowered academic standards, and insufficient critical thinking instruction, though provinces like Alberta maintain rigorous standardized exams yielding positive outcomes. Immigration policies facilitate fast-track permanent residency for foreign students with Canadian work experience.

## Education in Poland  
Poland’s education features compulsory primary education lasting eight years (grades 1–8) following the 2017 abolition of the 3-year gimnazjum (junior high). Upper secondary education comprises general secondary schools (liceum), technical schools (technikum), and vocational schools, with pathways to the Matura exam for university admission. Tertiary education follows the Bologna process with three-year bachelor’s and two-year master’s degrees or longer integrated programs for some fields. University grading uses a 2.0–5.0 numeric scale, where 2.0 is failing and 3.0 passing. Foreign language learning is widespread, especially English and German, beginning in lower secondary with exams impacting secondary school admission.

Education has a long history, with the Jagiellonian University (1364) among Europe’s oldest and the 1773 Commission of National Education as the first state education ministry. Compulsory education was introduced in 1919 and now extends to age 18. Significant reforms include the 1999 shift to a 6-3-3 system improving OECD rankings and the 2017 reversion to an 8+4/5 system, along with a revamped, nationalistic history curriculum and terminology changes. Since 2006, "zero tolerance" policies address school violence, enhancing legal protections for teachers and penalties for unreported incidents.

## Education in the Czech Republic  
Elementary education lasts nine grades in two stages: the first stage taught mostly by one teacher, and the second including two foreign languages and a broader curriculum. Students continue in elementary or specialized schools after grade 9. Since 2016/17, entrance tests in math, Czech, or English are required for maturita-track secondary schools, with about 12% failing.

Secondary education includes vocational schools (2–3 years, some with maturita), professional schools (4 years, technical focus), lyceums (general academics), and grammar schools preparing students for university without a final qualification. The maturita exam, a universal secondary leaving exam, is mandatory for university admission; it includes compulsory exams in Czech, literature, and either mathematics or a foreign language plus school-chosen subjects.

Special schools for developmentally disabled children were replaced by elementary and practical schools prioritizing practical skills for self-sufficiency. Integrated education with support teachers increasingly serves under-performing or mentally handicapped children. However, Romani pupils remain overrepresented in special education.

Tertiary education features historic universities like Charles University (1348) and Palacký University (1576), alongside many new institutions post-Czechoslovakia. Higher professional schools offer vocational tertiary credentials (title "DiS."). Universities provide Czech-language free tuition to first-time students under 26; private and English-language programs charge fees. Degree paths are Bachelor’s (~3 years, with maturita, thesis, final exam), Master’s (2–6 years, depending on field, requiring thesis and final exam), and Doctorate (Ph.D.), demanding rigorous exams and thesis defense; various academic titles correspond to qualification and field.

The academic year runs from September to June, divided into two semesters with exams. Holidays include autumn, Christmas, a mid-term break, spring, Easter, and long summer breaks. Children’s Day on June 1 is celebrated with activities though not an official holiday.

Common themes include exam-driven pathways, varying tracking systems, multilingual instruction, gender and rural-urban disparities, public and private education balances, and adapting to globalization, national identity, and social equity concerns.
