# Education in the Netherlands, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Russia, New Zealand, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Poland, Bangladesh, the Czech Republic, and Canada: Comparative Overview

## Education in the Netherlands  
Compulsory from ages 4/5 to 16+, the Dutch system includes public, special religious, general-special (neutral), and private schools. After eight years of elementary education, students are tracked into diversified secondary pathways (VMBO, HAVO, VWO), based on assessments like the Cito test, leading to vocational (MBO) or higher education (HBO, WO). Quality assurance involves NVAO accreditation and welfare teams. Persistent challenges include socio-economic disparities in tracking, underplacement of gifted students, and declining international test performance.

## Education in Japan  
The academic year begins in April with compulsory elementary (grades 1–6) and lower secondary (7–9) education, featuring low dropout rates (<2%). English instruction became mandatory in 2011 amid reforms for inclusivity. Upper secondary (10–12) offers academic, technical, and vocational tracks, with university admission reliant on high-stakes entrance exams and cram schools ("juku"). The system faces issues of high-pressure environments fostering mental health problems and bullying, alongside challenges in accreditation for English-language international schools.

## Education in South Korea  
Rooted in a Confucian meritocracy, South Korea offers six years each of primary and secondary education transitioning to a credit system by 2025. Vocational Meister schools provide apprenticeships with near-universal employment, despite stigma. Elite "SKY" universities dominate via the CSAT exam. English education emphasizes communication, aided by private "hagwons" and overseas study. The system contends with health impacts from academic pressure, educational inequality, and elitism via special-purpose high schools.

## Education in Mexico  
Influenced by colonial legacies, Mexico emphasizes secular, compulsory public education with indigenous inclusion amid resistance. Reforms in the 20th century improved literacy (~95%) and access via decentralization; primary and junior high are compulsory, but secondary completion lags in rural zones. Higher education comprises public and private universities with low indigenous participation. Resource scarcity affects special needs and bilingual programs. Since 2015, English-medium international schools expanded.

## Education in Russia  
Compulsory nine-year education uses a 5-point grading scale. Prestige schools provide advanced subjects; post-compulsory education bifurcates into general and vocational tracks, often delaying military service. The Unified State Examination (USE) standardizes university admission amid critique. Over 50% of students enter higher education, twice the OECD average, including many CIS students. Post-Soviet reforms merged universities into federal centers; Bologna Process adoption is partial, with bachelor degrees lacking full recognition. Postgraduate education retains Soviet models. Since 2020, foreign students must pass Russian language exams. Patriotism-themed lessons intensified post-Ukraine invasion, and minority language instruction serves millions amid geopolitical tensions.

## Education in New Zealand  
Formal education began by 1878, complementing pre-European Māori oral traditions. Early childhood education includes center-based and parent-led programs following the *Te Whāriki* curriculum, with over half of educators qualified. Education is free and compulsory (ages 6–16), aligned with the National Curriculum and qualifications (NCEA, Cambridge, IB). State schools (85%) are secular and government-funded; state-integrated religious schools charge dues; private schools rely on tuition fees. Māori language revitalization since the 1970s enhanced outcomes across communities. Special education is well funded. Tertiary education is subsidized with no-interest loans. Despite variable international rankings, targeted interventions address persistent achievement gaps and bullying.

## Education in Pakistan  
With a 60.65% literacy rate, Pakistan ranks low regionally due to poverty, gender disparity, teacher absenteeism, and weak governance. Literacy varies regionally; expenditure is low (~2%). Net primary attendance is ~66%, with 67.5% completion. Co-education is rare beyond urban centers; sexual education is limited. Secondary education offers SSC, HSSC certificates in academic and vocational tracks, including British GCE O/A Levels. Higher education completion is low but targeted for improvement. Teacher education emphasizes rote learning. NGOs and madrassas deliver Islamic and some secular education; madrassas marginalize minorities and omit topics like evolution. Patriarchal norms deepen gender gaps: female literacy at 52.8% lags male enrollment (68%), with STEMM and workforce barriers despite legal protections. Challenges include teacher shortages, rote curricula, poor infrastructure, expensive higher education, and management struggles in vocational education. COVID-19 accelerated distance learning platforms amid digital divides and funding cuts. Pakistan hosts 439 international English-medium schools and strong international student outflows. NUST Islamabad ranks #91 QS Asia Top 100 (2018), with six globally ranked universities and notable alumni including Nobel laureates and scientists.

## Education in Saudi Arabia  
Illiteracy declined from 13.7% (2007) with remaining regional and gender disparities. Rooted in Islamic teachings, historic rote Qur’an memorization has been criticized for limiting innovation and fostering intolerance. Modernization programs like the King Abdullah Project and Tatweer reform curricula, teacher training, vocational training, critical thinking, and reduce extremism, removing anti-Semitic and anti-women content. Pre-primary enrollment was 10.8% (2022). Primary education (6 years) is compulsory; English mandatory from grade one since 2021. Intermediate and secondary stages (3 years each) maintain high enrollment (~90–96%) with general and specialized tracks. Higher education expanded rapidly to 24 government universities, offering diploma to PhD levels; female participation surpasses males, with women leading in education employment. Girls’ schooling began in 1955 amid opposition; currently, women are 60% of university students. Gifted education policies support STEM and innovation. Special education serves severe disabilities in specialized institutions and milder cases via mainstreaming, though resources are limited. Private schools complement government education under stricter recent oversight. Saudi Arabia hosts about 203 international schools, including American, British, and Philippine community schools.

## Education in Poland  
Compulsory education starts at age 6 or 7. A 2017 reform integrated the three-year gimnazjum into an 8-year primary school (grades 1–8). Upper secondary includes 4-year general secondary (liceum) culminating in the Matura exam, 5-year technical schools (technikum) awarding Matura, and 3-year basic vocational schools granting competence certificates. Profiled general and special schools offer vocational training and serve special needs students. Tertiary education follows Bologna standards: mostly 3-year bachelor’s and 2-year master’s degrees; doctorates last about 3 years. Grading uses numeric scales with retake options. Students study 1–2 foreign languages, mainly English (~68%). Poland’s education reflects a tension between OECD-aligned structures and nationalistic, Eurocentric curricula, with strict anti-violence policies since 2006.

## Education in Bangladesh  
The multi-tiered system includes pre-primary, primary, secondary, higher secondary, and tertiary levels, governed by multiple agencies. Primary schools fall under the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education and Directorate of Primary Education; secondary schools are regional via ten boards. Tertiary institutions include public, private, and international universities regulated by the University Grants Commission. Technical and vocational education offers qualifications from short courses to diplomas. School types comprise national curriculum, madrasahs—with government-subsidized Alia blending general and Arabic curricula up to “Kamil” degree—and private Qawmi madrasahs. NGOs run non-formal schools targeting vulnerable children. Religious education is mandatory but often self-studied by non-Muslims due to teacher shortages. Rohingya refugee children receive limited Myanmar curriculum schooling in camps, supported by UNICEF and government. Challenges include enforcing compulsory education, expanding free education for girls, stipends, and reform implementation. Persistent issues include rote learning, weak outcome- and industry-orientation in higher education, and insufficient practical training. Public expenditure is about 2% of GDP; teacher absence rates at 15.5%. Gender parity at primary and secondary levels has been achieved, with girls outperforming boys despite rural dropout and repetition. Literacy rose from 51.77% (2011) to 74.66% (2022).

## Education in the Czech Republic  
Elementary education lasts nine grades in two stages: grades 1–5 with mainly one teacher, and 6–9 with subject specialization and foreign languages. Students attend elementary schools or specialized academies (e.g., physical education, languages). Secondary education requires entrance tests (math, English, Czech) for schools offering the universal “maturita” exit exam, which had a 12.3% fail rate in 2025. Secondary pathways include 2–3-year vocational schools, 4-year professional schools (with maturita), lyceums (general), and grammar schools (university preparatory). Special and practical schools serve developmentally disabled students, with growing inclusion. Tertiary education includes historic Charles University (1348) and others awarding diplomas and various doctorates. University education is free for Czech-speaking public students under 26; tuition applies to international and private students. Degrees cover 3-year bachelor’s, 1–3-year master’s, and doctoral programs. The academic year runs September to June, divided into two semesters.

## Education in Canada  
Education is provincially managed with standard structures including early childhood (Pre-Kindergarten to Kindergarten, ages 3–6), elementary (Grades 1–6, ages 5–12), intermediate (Grades 7–9, ages 11–15), and secondary (Grades 10–12, ages 14–18), with Quebec finishing secondary at Grade 11 and offering CEGEP as a bridge to university. Tertiary education includes colleges (community and technical) offering diplomas/certificates and universities providing undergraduate to doctoral degrees. Provincial variations exist in grade grouping, inclusion of middle schools, and governance styles; Alberta uniquely funds secular charter schools. Public schools are funded and governed by elected local district boards within provincial curricula, with bilingual education supporting Indigenous studies. Private schools, enrolling about 6% of grade ten students (notably in Quebec), tend to outperform public peers mainly due to higher expectations. Private universities have grown since 1999 legislation, including secular institutions like Quest University, while faith-based private universities enforce community covenants that sometimes raise human rights concerns. Funding of private religious schools varies: Ontario fully funds Catholic schools constitutionally but not other faith schools; British Columbia funds independent schools up to 50% of public school costs. Education typically begins at age 5 and ends after Grade 12, though extended options exist. Alternative programs such as night and online schooling support diverse student needs. International student enrollment at secondary and post-secondary levels is increasing. Canada ranks highly internationally for university quality and enrollment. Vocational education is provided through colleges and apprenticeships combining classroom and practical training, culminating in nationally recognized certifications. Criticisms include overemphasis on tested subjects, grade inflation, and pedagogical challenges, with Alberta noted for its rigorous standardized exams influencing university admission.

---

This comparative overview reveals global education systems shaped by distinct cultural legacies, governance structures, reforms, and societal challenges. These range from socio-economically stratified multi-track systems in the Netherlands; exam-driven, high-pressure models in East Asia; indigenous inclusion efforts in Mexico and New Zealand; centralized post-Soviet restructuring and patriotic curricula in Russia; gender and resource disparities amid religious traditions in Pakistan and Saudi Arabia; structural reforms with nationalistic emphases in Poland; complex agency interplay blending secular, vocational, and Islamic schools in Bangladesh; structured examinations and historic university traditions in the Czech Republic; to federated, bilingual, religiously diverse, and internationally acclaimed systems in Canada.
