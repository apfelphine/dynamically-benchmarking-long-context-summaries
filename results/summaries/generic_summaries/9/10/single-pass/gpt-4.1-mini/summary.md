# Education in the Czech Republic  
Elementary education is divided into a nine-grade system with two stages: the first comprises grades 1–5 taught mostly by individual teachers; the second comprises grades 6–9 with a broader curriculum including two foreign languages. Secondary education includes vocational, professional, lyceum, and grammar schools culminating in the maturita exam, required for university entry. Since 2016, entrance tests in mathematics or English and Czech are compulsory. Tertiary education features public and private universities and higher professional schools offering bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. The academic year runs from September to June, with several holidays including summer and Christmas breaks.

# Education in Poland  
Compulsory education lasts eight years of primary school (extended from six years in 2017) followed by secondary education, formerly divided by three years of gimnazjum (junior high school) until its abolition in 2019. Secondary education includes general secondary schools (liceum), technical schools (technikum), vocational schools, and special schools. Higher education follows the Bologna process with bachelor’s and master’s degrees, doctoral studies, and teacher training colleges. Polish university grading is numeric from 2.0 (fail) to 5.0 (highest), with retakes common. Students typically learn one or two foreign languages, mostly English and German. The education system has undergone reforms, notably in 1999 and 2017, with historical emphasis on literacy and state control.

# Education in Canada  
Education is provincially managed, with federal involvement limited to military and Indigenous education. Compulsory education typically starts at age 5–7 and continues to grade 12, with variations by province. Early childhood education includes kindergarten programs. Post-secondary education consists of universities and vocational colleges, offering degrees from certificates to doctorates. Universities have competitive admissions largely based on secondary school performance and entrance criteria. Private schools enroll about 6% of students, with some having religious affiliations. The Canadian system offers English and French-language education, protected constitutionally, and hosts diverse student populations. Education spending is about 5.2% of GDP, with ongoing debates on equity-focused data collection and academic standards.

# Education in Pakistan  
Education follows a tiered system: pre-school, primary (years 1–5), middle (6–8), and secondary (9–12), with compulsory national examinations administered regionally. Alternative British-based qualifications like O/A levels are common in private sectors. Tertiary education includes professional degrees and is overseen by various accreditation councils. Gender disparity remains significant, with women underrepresented despite improvements; cultural and infrastructural barriers persist. Technical and vocational education targets workforce development but faces quality challenges. Teacher training programs lack consistent quality and resources. Distance learning expanded during COVID-19 but faces access issues. Public expenditure on education is low (~2% of GDP), with infrastructure and quality concerns. Madrassas provide religious education and fill gaps in underserved areas. Notable Pakistani alumni have made contributions in sciences and technology.

# Education in Bangladesh  
Education is structured into pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (9–12), and tertiary levels, with a national curriculum offered in Bengali and English versions. Students choose among Arts, Commerce, and Science streams starting from class 9. Standardized examinations exist at primary, junior secondary, secondary, and higher secondary levels. The system includes general, madrasah (religious), and international schools. Secondary and higher education oversight is by regional boards and the University Grants Commission. Technical and vocational education is controlled by dedicated authorities offering diploma and degree pathways. Non-formal education is significant, mainly through NGOs. Literacy rate was approximately 75% in 2022, with ongoing challenges in rural attendance and gender parity.

# Education in Russia  
Russia’s education system divides into general and professional types. General education entails preschool, primary (4 years), basic general (5 years), and secondary general (2 years). Vocational training follows secondary education with multiple duration options. Higher education includes bachelor’s (4 years), specialist (5–6 years), and master’s (2 years) programs, with postgraduate degrees such as Candidate and Doctor of Sciences. Preschool enrollment is widespread but uneven. Secondary education is compulsory for nine years, with an option to continue to 11 years. State exams unify university admissions to reduce corruption. Special education exists for physical and mental disabilities but integration remains limited. Tertiary enrollment is high, with ongoing reforms towards Bologna standards. Recent legislative changes have raised concerns over censorship and increased patriotic content in schools.

# Education in Saudi Arabia  
Education is free and primarily managed by the Ministry of Education, with compulsory primary education lasting six years starting at age six. The system integrates religious studies heavily alongside general subjects. Intermediate and secondary education last three years each, with options for general or technical tracks. Higher education includes 24 government universities and private institutions, with scholarship programs for study abroad. Female participation in education, especially higher education, has increased, with women constituting a majority of university students. Gifted education and special education programs have been developed recently. Private schools require government approval and must meet infrastructure standards. International schools serve expat communities, including numerous Philippine schools. Criticisms focus on rote learning dominance, excessive religious content, gender disparity, and slow modernization efforts.

# Education in South Korea  
Historically influenced by Confucianism, South Korea’s education system features six years of elementary, three years of middle, and three years of high school, with vocational and general tracks. Vocational education is undergoing revitalization via Meister schools to address labor market demands. Higher education includes diverse institutions with strong competition for university admission largely based on the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT). English education is emphasized, with high investment and private tutoring prevalent. High school is nearly universally completed. Criticisms include social stigma against vocational education, excessive exam-driven pressure, health concerns from intense study, and mismatch between majors and employment. Private tutoring (hagwons) is a major expenditure and social phenomenon.

# Education in New Zealand  
Education historic roots include Māori traditional teachings and European missionary schools. Early childhood education is widespread, licensed, and may be teacher or parent-led. Compulsory education runs between ages 6 and 16, with free primary and secondary schooling available until 19. School years are numbered 1 to 13, with transitions from primary to intermediate to secondary varying by area. The national curriculum covers eight learning areas with qualifications like NCEA, Cambridge, and IB offered. Schools are state, state-integrated (mostly religious), or private with varying fees. Māori language education is available in immersion and English-medium programs, with ongoing revitalization efforts. Funding is governmental with additional parental contributions. Bullying remains a significant concern, prompting increased regulatory efforts.

# Education in the Netherlands  
Compulsory education starts at age five. Children attend eight years of elementary school followed by secondary education, stratified into VMBO (vocational), HAVO (general), and VWO (pre-university). Secondary education includes various tracks and profiles preparing for vocational training, higher professional education (HBO), or research universities (WO). A central final test (Cito) at elementary level guides secondary placement, though recommendations are not binding. Vocational and higher education align with the Bologna system, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Schools may be public, special (often religious), or private. The education system faces criticism regarding early tracking and its impact on gifted students. International schools and quality assurance through accreditation exist.

# Education in Mexico  
Mexico’s basic education comprises six years of primary, three years of junior high (secundaria), and three years of high school (preparatoria). Bilingual education and vocational tracks exist. Educational integration policies mandate inclusion of special needs students but face implementation challenges. School attendance has increased, but dropout rates remain high after primary. Quality concerns include infrastructure deficits and low funding. Public and private higher education mirror the U.S. system with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees; indigenous students remain underrepresented. The national teachers’ union (SNTE) holds significant influence, occasionally resisting reforms. International and intercultural universities serve diverse populations.

# Education in Japan  
Formal education began in the 6th century and was shaped by Buddhist and Confucian traditions, evolving through the Edo period and Western influences after the Meiji Restoration. Post-WWII reforms established a system modeled on the American system: six years of elementary, three years of lower secondary, three years of upper secondary, and four years of university. Compulsory education lasts nine years. Japanese education emphasizes entrance exam preparation, with extensive use of juku (cram schools). Higher education includes national, public, and private universities offering a range of degrees. Special needs education and inclusive policies have developed since the 1990s. Criticisms focus on academic pressure, bullying, inadequate fostering of creativity, and inequalities in educational opportunity. A small but active international school sector exists, mostly teaching in English.
