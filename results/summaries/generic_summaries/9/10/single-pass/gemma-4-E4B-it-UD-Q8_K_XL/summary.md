# Global Education System Overviews

## Czech Republic Education
**Elementary School:** The nine-grade elementary system has two stages. The first five years feature single-teacher instruction and foundational subjects like Czech, math, and science. The final four years are the compulsory stage, introducing more specialized subjects and two foreign languages. Students may remain in the elementary school or attend an eight-year or six-year academy.

**Secondary and Tertiary Education:** Secondary schools graduating with a "maturita" degree must pass standardized tests in mathematics or English/Czech. Vocational certificates are awarded after 2- or 3-year courses, with some schools offering an academic maturita. Grammar schools prepare students for university without granting a qualification. Higher education includes public and private universities; public universities offer free Czech-language study, while private institutions charge annual tuition ranging from 2,000 to 3,000 euros.

## Poland Education
**Compulsory Education:** Primary school (8 years) typically begins at age seven, replacing the former 6-year primary + 3-year junior high system after the 2017 reform. Upper secondary education prepares students for the labor market or tertiary education.

**Higher Education:** Following the Bologna scheme, most tertiary programs involve a three-year bachelor's followed by a two-year master's. Grading is numerical (2.0 failing to 5.0 highest), assessed semesterly.

**History and Reforms:** Poland's education history dates back to the Jagiellonian University (1364). Major reforms include the 1999 restructuring (6+3+3) and the 2017 reform, which extended primary school to eight years.

## Canada Education
**Structure and Authority:** Education is primarily a provincial matter, leading to variation across the country. Compulsory education timelines differ by province, generally running from Kindergarten (age five) through Grade 12.

**Tertiary Education:** Higher education is delivered by universities (offering bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees) and vocational institutions (offering diplomas and associate degrees). Universities are internationally recognized, with strong research capabilities.

**Equity and Inclusion:** Section 15(2) of the Charter allows for identity-based data collection to address achievement gaps among racialized students, though critics question its transparency.

## Pakistan Education
**Stages and Challenges:** Formal education includes pre-school, junior school (Years 1–5), and middle school (Years 6–8). Secondary education spans four years, culminating in an SSC, followed by HSSC for intermediate college.

**Gender and Quality:** The system faces significant gender disparity, with low female enrollment and lower literacy rates for women. While literacy rates are improving, the quality of education is criticized for being rote-learning based, lacking modern relevance, and being severely impacted by chronic underfunding (public expenditure around 2% of GDP).

## Bangladesh Education
**System Structure:** The system has four levels: Pre-primary (K-2), Primary (3–8), Secondary (9–12), and Tertiary. Students can opt for English or Bengali medium.

**Curriculum and Grading:** The National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB) dictates standardized testing. Grading uses a GPA system (highest 5.0), where a passing grade is 33% or higher.

**Institutions:** Public institutions are heavily subsidized, while most universities are private. Madrasah education exists as a significant religious component.

## Russia Education
**Levels and Structure:** The system is divided into general and professional education. General education progresses through preschool, primary (4 years), basic (5 years), and secondary (2 years).

**Higher Education:** Historically, education was in a single-stage specialist diploma format. Russia is transitioning toward the Bologna Process, adopting a four-year Bachelor's followed by a two-year Master's.

**Policy and Control:** The state controls the curriculum, with standardized testing (Unified State Examination, USE) implemented to reduce bribery in admissions. There is an increasing focus on "patriotic education."

## Saudi Arabia Education
**Core Values and Goals:** Education is free for all levels and rooted in Islamic principles. The goal is to reduce illiteracy while educating students according to social, economic, and Islamic norms.

**System and Reform:** The system is managed by the Ministry of Education and TVTC. Reforms, like the Tatweer program, aim to shift teaching from rote memorization to critical thinking. While religious studies dominate, modern instruction also covers sciences and arts.

**Gender and Inclusion:** Despite strong female participation in higher education (60% of college students), gender disparity persists in the labor force, and the system struggles with the integration of students with disabilities.

## South Korea Education
**Historical Context and Pressure:** Rooted in Confucian values, Korean education has historically prioritized meritocracy through intense examination systems. This has led to immense academic pressure.

**Structure and Vocational Focus:** Primary education is grades 1–6, and middle school (7–9) is compulsory. High school is not mandatory, but high enrollment rates are noted. A significant movement to modernize vocational training has led to the introduction of "Meister Schools" to address the social stigma against manual labor.

**Competition and Health:** The intense competition, fueled by expensive private tutoring (hagwons), results in high rates of student stress, sleep deprivation, and mental health concerns.

## New Zealand Education
**Structure and Principles:** All citizens are entitled to free primary and secondary schooling from age five until the year after age 19. The system is secular and free.

**Curriculum and Language:** The national curriculum (NZC) is followed, though Māori-medium education (Te Marautanga o Aotearoa) is actively supported as one of the three official languages.

**Funding and Standards:** State schools receive per-student funding, with low-decile schools receiving extra support. However, international rankings have shown declining performance in basic numeracy and reading.

## Netherlands Education
**Structure and Pathways:** Compulsory education starts at age five. Secondary education offers three main tracks: VMBO (pre-vocational), HAVO (higher general), and VWO (scientific education), with different pathways leading to tertiary education.

**Assessment and Criticism:** Assessment is annual, with the Cito test heavily influencing secondary placement. A key criticism is the early separation of students into tracks around age 12, which may prevent some gifted children from reaching their potential.

## Mexico Education
**System Stages:** Basic education is divided into primary (grades 1–6), junior high (grades 7–9), and high school/preparatoria (grades 10–12).

**Integration and Quality:** Educational integration mandates that students with special needs are supported in regular classrooms (USAER). However, challenges remain regarding professional training for support staff and school accessibility. Quality is criticized due to low secondary completion rates (45%) and underinvestment, despite a high rate of primary school attendance.

## Japan Education
**Structure and Competition:** The system is highly rigorous, emphasizing university preparation, which drives intense competition fueled by after-school cram schools (*juku*). Compulsory education ends at grade nine.

**Culture and Reform:** While early education was influenced by Confucian values, post-WWII reforms aimed for democracy. Recent reforms sought to reduce academic burden (*Yutori education*), but the shadow education industry remains dominant.

**Student Well-being:** Extreme academic pressure has led to reports of school violence and high rates of student suicide.
