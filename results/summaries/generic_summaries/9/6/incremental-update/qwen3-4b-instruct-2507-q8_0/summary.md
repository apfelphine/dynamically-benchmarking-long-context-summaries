# **Final Summary**

New Zealand's education system is free, compulsory, and secular for citizens and permanent residents, beginning at age 5 and continuing until age 19. It evolved from Māori wānanga and early European missionary schools to a national system established in 1878, influenced by Victorian ideals. Early childhood education includes licensed centres (e.g., kindy, day care) and parent-led options (e.g., Kohanga Reo, Playcentre), all following the *Te Whāriki* framework. Primary education spans Years 1–8, secondary Years 9–13, with hybrid models such as composite or intermediate schools commonly used. The national curriculum (NZC or *Te Marautanga o Aotearoa*) guides instruction, and students prepare for NCEA or international qualifications.

Schools are categorized as state (government-funded, free), state-integrated (government-funded with religious or special character, charging attendance dues), or private (fee-paying). Around 85% of students attend state schools, 10% state-integrated, and a small number attend private schools. Private schools operate on tuition fees, while home-schooling is rare and regulated. Charter schools (2014–2018) were phased into state-integrated schools. Enrolment schemes, introduced in 1991, grant geographic priority access to schools with strict rules to prevent address fraud, though they are criticized for perpetuating inequity. Māori language education has experienced a revival since the 1970s, driven by cultural advocacy and legal recognition—including the 1987 Māori Language Act—and the establishment of *kōhanga reo* and *kura kaupapa*. Māori students in kura kaupapa achieve higher NCEA Level 3 outcomes than their peers in mainstream schools. Despite stable overall enrolment, participation in Māori language education has declined since 2004. School terms run from late January to mid-December, divided into four ten-week terms. Manual training schools, once common, are now integrated into the curriculum. Tertiary education is regulated under the New Zealand Qualifications Framework and funded through government tuition grants and student loans/allowances; high fees and inflation have led to criticism and skills shortages. Student allowances are means-tested and cover living costs; student loans cover tuition and living expenses, with interest waived for NZ residents since 2006. Educational standards have declined since 2001, with poor performance in basic math and reading, despite a 2012 Pearson report ranking NZ 8th globally—controversially due to flawed testing. Māori and Pacific students lag behind Pākehā in academic achievement. School bullying is widespread, with serious incidents prompting government action, though policies remain inconsistent and underdeveloped.
