## Summary

Education systems in countries such as New Zealand, Saudi Arabia, Mexico, Bangladesh, Japan, Canada, the Netherlands, Czech Republic, South Korea, Poland, Russia, and Pakistan reveal diverse historical legacies, governance frameworks, and reform priorities, all aiming to address resource disparities, regional and gender gaps, curriculum bias, and inclusivity.

### New Zealand
Schooling is compulsory and secular, shaped by Māori and missionary influences, with Māori-medium instruction and local Boards of Trustees. Persistent challenges include achievement gaps, bullying, equity issues, and declining international rankings.

### Saudi Arabia
Tuition-free, compulsory education separated into religious, secular, and vocational streams, overseen by the Ministry of Education. Reforms focus on teacher quality, skill development, inclusivity, and access, including distance learning.

### Mexico
Education is compulsory, rooted in colonial and socialist philosophies, with general, vocational, and indigenous tracks. Progress in literacy and attendance continues but rural inequalities and infrastructure gaps remain pronounced.

### Bangladesh
Multiple agencies administer public, madrasah, and international schools, targeting gender parity and literacy amid high dropout rates and resource shortages. Rote learning and exam-centric methods persist, prompting reforms in standards and employability.

### Japan
An exam-driven, compulsory system with Chinese and Western influences, facing student stress and mental health issues. Recent reforms target both equity and English proficiency.

### Canada
Decentralized, province-driven governance emphasizes Indigenous education, antiracism, and varied learning pathways.

### Netherlands
Features compulsory education with public/religious options, ability-based streaming, holiday schedules, and Bologna-compliant higher education. Reforms aim to address streaming and inclusivity.

### Czech Republic
Nine-year elementary schooling leads to academic or vocational tracks, with the maturita exam as a university gateway. Efforts focus on inclusion and expanded tertiary access.

### South Korea
A competitive, Confucian-influenced system emphasizing university entrance and English, with common private tutoring. Vocational routes are undervalued; reforms address status, employment, and tertiary expansion where most institutions are private.

### Poland
Eight-year primary education, criticized for Eurocentrism and nationalism, offers flexible entry, streaming, strong anti-violence norms, and Matura-based university pathways.

### Russia
Education spans preschool to postgraduate, uses streaming and multi-shift scheduling, supports linguistic diversity (excluding some disabled groups), and boasts high tertiary attainment amid university consolidation and rising censorship.

### Pakistan
Pakistan offers formal, non-formal, and informal education systems, with pathways spanning primary to tertiary and multiple tracks, including O/A Levels and vocational programs. Major challenges are chronic underfunding (about 2% of GDP), outdated curricula, insufficient teacher training, and poor infrastructure, particularly in Punjab and Balochistan. Madrassas fill gaps left by the public system. Literacy rates have climbed to 60.65% (2023), still the second lowest in South Asia after Afghanistan, and significantly lower than regional comparators like Indonesia and Malaysia. Gender, social, and urban-rural disparities persist, exacerbated by poverty and weak government oversight. University rankings are improving, but curricula remain contested for religious, ideological, and gender bias.

Some districts now report youth literacy rates above 85% (with Upper Chitral at 95%), and the national mean years of schooling have grown from 2.28 (1990) to 5.16 (2018), though Balochistan lags in attainment. Notable figures like Abdus Salam and Arfa Karim highlight achievements amid systemic barriers. Distance education (Teleschool, AIOU) is expanding access, tempered by digital divides. As of 2015, 439 international schools operated in Pakistan, and many students pursue education abroad, particularly in China.

---

### Shared Features and Challenges

These countries universally emphasize compulsory schooling, formal governance, curriculum modernization, and reforms for marginalized communities. Common ongoing barriers include urban-rural inequities, teacher/resource shortages, streaming and vocational bias, prevalence of private tutoring, student mental health challenges, and substantial gender and social disparities in educational quality and access.
