# Education in New Zealand
New Zealand’s education began with Māori wānanga led by elders teaching traditional knowledge. European-style schooling was introduced in the early 19th century by missionaries who taught in Māori. A national secular, compulsory, and free education system was established in 1878. The system includes early childhood education (kindergartens, kōhanga reo), primary (Years 1–8), and secondary (Years 9–13) education. Schools follow national curricula, including The New Zealand Curriculum for English-medium and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa for Māori-medium schools. Education types include state, state-integrated (mostly religious), and private schools. Māori language education has seen revival after historic suppression, with immersion schools and language programs available. Funding comes mainly from government allocations, with voluntary donations for state schools and compulsory attendance dues for state-integrated schools. Bullying remains a concern, with ongoing policies to address it.

# Education in Saudi Arabia
Saudi education is rooted in Islamic traditions with early schools (kuttabs) linked to mosques. Modern state education began in 1926, emphasizing Islamic as well as social and economic norms. Education is free and managed by the Ministry of Education alongside other agencies. Pre-primary education is optional; primary education is compulsory for ages 6–12, with English introduced early to improve outcomes. Intermediate and secondary education last three years each, with religious and scientific subjects taught. Higher education expanded rapidly since 1957, with 24 government universities and scholarship programs abroad. Girls’ education developed separately under conservative oversight but women now make up 60% of university students. Gifted education programs have advanced since 2002, incorporating enrichment and specialized classes. Special education caters to disabilities with inclusion efforts ongoing. Private and international schools operate under government supervision. Criticism includes the emphasis on rote religious education, lack of innovation, and promotion of intolerance, though reforms aim to modernize curricula and remove extremist content.

# Education in Mexico
Mexico’s basic education is divided into primary (grades 1–6), junior high/secondary (secundaria, grades 7–9), and high school (preparatoria, grades 10–12). Education is compulsory through secundaria, with high school recently made compulsory. Vocational and general programs coexist, and bilingual education may be offered. Educational integration promotes inclusion of special needs students, supported by specialized professionals, but challenges include collaboration, accessibility, and teacher preparation. Higher education follows the Bologna model with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Intercultural universities address indigenous enrollment disparities. Education reforms have sought to decentralize control from the teachers' union. While primary enrollment is high, dropout rates increase in secondary education. Mexico hosts numerous international schools following various curricula.

# Education in Bangladesh
Bangladesh’s education system is structured into pre-primary, primary (class 3–8), secondary (class 9–12), and tertiary levels. Education is offered in both Bengali and English mediums, including international and religious-affiliated schools. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board manages curricula and public exams up to class 12, with compulsory standardized testing, although primary and junior school certificates are being phased out. Secondary education is governed by regional boards with public exams leading to SSC and HSC certifications. Tertiary education includes public, private, and international universities regulated by the University Grants Commission. Technical and vocational education is provided by polytechnic institutes and technical boards. Non-formal and madrasah education coexist, with a sizable madrasa system catering especially to Islamic study. Literacy rates have improved but challenges remain including rural-urban disparities, gender gaps in some regions, and infrastructure deficits. Religious education incorporates multiple faiths, though non-Muslim instruction faces teacher shortages. Distance learning expanded during COVID-19, supported by government initiatives.

# Education in Japan
Japan’s education has roots in 6th-century Chinese influence, evolving through the Edo period with samurai schools and terakoya. The Meiji Restoration introduced Western-style compulsory education and established universities. Post-WWII reforms modeled schooling on the American system, extending compulsory education to nine years with further expansion and reform in curriculum and pedagogy. The system comprises elementary (6 years), junior high (3 years), and upper secondary schools (3 years), with a focus on academic rigor and entrance examinations. Higher education includes universities and junior colleges with research universities highly ranked internationally. Special needs education emphasizes inclusion. Extracurricular shadow education (juku) is widespread but criticized for increasing student pressure. Bullying remains a notable issue despite government efforts.

# Education in Canada
Education in Canada is provincially managed with variation across regions. It generally covers early childhood, elementary, intermediate, secondary (up to Grade 12), and post-secondary education. Compulsory schooling typically starts at age five or six and ends between ages 16-18 depending on province. Canada boasts high tertiary enrollment and spends about 5.2% of GDP on education. The public system includes secular and religious (notably Catholic) funding arrangements. Assessment is coordinated nationally through bodies like the Canadian Education Statistics Council and OECD’s PISA. Post-secondary education includes research universities, vocational institutions, and private universities, with multiple pathways to degrees and certifications. Private schools serve about 6% of students, generally performing well academically. Minority language and faith-based schools operate under constitutional protections. Education boards govern local schooling, delivering provincially set curricula.

# Education in the Netherlands
The Dutch education system is coordinated nationally with municipal input. Compulsory education starts at age five (often four) and ends at 18 or upon diploma completion. Elementary school (basisschool) runs eight years (groups 1–8), starting at age four. Secondary education is stratified: VMBO (4 years, vocational), HAVO (5 years, higher general), and VWO (6 years, university preparatory), with pathways allowing movement between levels. Students take a national primary test (Cito) in group 8 to guide secondary placement. Vocational (MBO), professional (HBO), and research university (WO) pathways address tertiary education needs. The Bologna process governs higher education degrees. Special education and Islamic schools exist alongside public and special (religious) schools receiving equal funding. Early tracking at age 12 is critiqued for limiting late-developing gifted students.

# Education in the Czech Republic
Elementary education is nine years split into two stages (1–5 and 6–9). Secondary education includes vocational schools (2–3 years), professional schools, lyceums, grammar schools, and academies. The maturita exam is a universal leaving qualification required for university entry, involving Czech language, literature, mathematics or a foreign language. Special and practical schools provide adapted education for disabled or underperforming students. Higher education includes public and private universities offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral programs with various professional and academic titles. The school year runs from September to June with several holidays. The system emphasizes standardized exit exams and diversified secondary education pathways.

# Education in South Korea
Korean education has been shaped by Confucian values emphasizing meritocracy and rigor. Historically dominated by village schools and private tutoring, modern education was influenced by Japanese colonial policies and post-liberation US reforms. The system includes six years elementary, three years middle school (compulsory), and three years high school, with specialized and vocational tracks. High school is not mandatory, but over 97% complete it. Higher education follows a US model with competitive university admission via the CSAT exam. Vocational education was historically stigmatized but revamped through Meister schools modeled on German vocational systems. English education emphasizes oral proficiency but faces challenges despite heavy investment. Criticism includes intense academic pressure, entrance exam obsession, social inequality in vocational education, mental health concerns, and teacher dissatisfaction.

# Education in Poland
Compulsory education begins at age six or seven with eight years of primary school, after which students attend upper secondary schools (liceum or vocational). The 2017 reforms abolished the middle school (gimnazjum), extending primary to eight years and secondary accordingly. Upper secondary includes general and vocational tracks, ending with the Matura exam required for university entrance. Tertiary education adopts the Bologna process with bachelor's and master's degrees and doctorates. The grading system uses a 2 to 5 numeric scale. Foreign languages are taught widely, with English being most common. Historically, education evolved from medieval foundations, with secularization and centralization challenges. Recent reforms aim to improve quality and access despite political debates and varying curricula focus.

# Education in Russia
Russia’s education comprises general (preschool, primary, basic, secondary) and professional (vocational and higher education) levels. Preschool is widespread but capacity varies, with declining kindergarten availability post-Soviet era. Secondary education is compulsory for nine years (with optional grades 10-11), with options for vocational training. The Unified State Examination was introduced in 2007 as a standardized university entrance test. Special education serves physical and mental disabilities in specialized schools and programs, but full inclusion is limited. Higher education includes bachelor’s, specialist, master’s, and doctoral degrees, transitioning to the Bologna system with challenges in recognition and implementation. Science outreach and government-led patriotic education are politically significant. Education in minority languages persists in some regions, though under pressure. Issues of quality, infrastructure, and access remain.

# Education in Pakistan
Pakistan's education system mirrors British influences and is structured into pre-primary, primary, middle, secondary (matriculation), intermediate, and tertiary levels. Primary education coverage is incomplete; secondary leads to SSC and HSSC certifications, with streams in science, commerce, humanities, and vocational tracks. Alternative systems include GCE O and A Levels and Madrassas focusing on Islamic education. Gender disparities remain pronounced, especially in rural areas, despite progress in female enrollment and literacy. Education quality suffers from outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and infrastructure deficits. Technical and vocational education aims to meet economic demands but faces challenges including societal undervaluation. Distance learning expanded during COVID-19. Criticism centers on religious influences, gender inequality, and systemic underfunding, with education expenditure hovering near 2% of GDP. Notable Pakistani scholars have made global contributions despite systemic hurdles.
