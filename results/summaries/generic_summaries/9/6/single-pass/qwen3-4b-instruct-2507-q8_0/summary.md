**Education in New Zealand**  
New Zealand’s education system began with traditional Māori knowledge transfer via *wānanga*, taught by respected elders, before European-style schooling emerged in the 19th century. The London Missionary Society established the first formal schools, emphasizing Māori language and literacy. The 1878 national system introduced free, compulsory, and secular education, influenced by the Canterbury model. Māori language education has seen a revival through *kōhanga reo* and *kura kaupapa* schools, now recognized as official. The curriculum follows the *New Zealand Curriculum* (NZC) or *Te Marautanga o Aotearoa* (TMoA), with students progressing through 13 year levels (Years 1–13). Early childhood education includes kindergarten, playgroups, and licensed centers. Primary and secondary education is free and compulsory until age 19, with students having options for home schooling. The system includes state, state-integrated, and private schools, with state schools being the majority. Māori language education remains a key focus, with government policies promoting its integration into mainstream schooling.  

**Education in Saudi Arabia**  
Saudi Arabia’s education system is rooted in Islamic teachings, with early schools (*kuttabs*) teaching Quran and basic literacy. A modern system began in 1926 with the Department of Education. The curriculum emphasizes religious studies, with 9–23 class hours per week dedicated to religion, while science and math receive fewer hours. The system is free and state-run, with a strong focus on religious instruction. Pre-primary and primary education are compulsory, with secondary education lasting three years. Girls’ education expanded in the 20th century, with dedicated institutions and government support. The education system is highly centralized, with religious content dominating. While criticized for rote learning and lack of innovation, recent reforms aim to modernize curricula and promote technical and vocational training. Private schools and international curricula (e.g., British) are growing, especially for non-Muslims.  

**Education in Mexico**  
Mexico’s education system is structured into primary (Grades 1–6), junior high (Grades 7–9), and high school (Grades 10–12). Primary education is compulsory, with a focus on core subjects. Secondary education includes a national curriculum, with students choosing from arts, commerce, or science tracks. The system has faced criticism for poor infrastructure, high dropout rates, and lack of teacher training. The government has implemented reforms to improve equity and access, including the integration of students with special needs. A significant reform in 2013 transferred control from teacher unions to the central government. The education system has seen progress in access and enrollment, with over 90% of children attending primary school. Challenges remain in rural areas and in the quality of teaching.  

**Education in Bangladesh**  
Bangladesh’s education system is divided into pre-primary, primary (Grades 3–8), secondary (Grades 9–12), and tertiary levels. The national curriculum is taught in both English and Bengali, with students choosing between streams (Arts, Commerce, Science). The system is highly centralized, with the Ministry of Education overseeing curriculum and exams. The government has implemented reforms to improve access and equity, including free textbooks and scholarships. A significant challenge is gender disparity, with girls’ performance improving in recent years. The system faces criticism for low quality, teacher shortages, and lack of innovation. The government has introduced vocational and technical education to meet economic needs. Literacy rates have improved, rising from 51.77% in 2011 to 74.66% in 2022.  

**Education in Japan**  
Japan’s education system began with Chinese-influenced learning during the Asuka and Nara periods, evolving into a modern system under the Meiji Restoration. Compulsory education began in 1872, with a focus on discipline and academic excellence. The system is highly structured, with a strong emphasis on standardized testing and academic performance. Primary education (Grades 1–6) is followed by junior high (Grades 7–9) and high school (Grades 10–12). Students are assessed through a rigorous grading system, with a focus on academic achievement. The system has faced criticism for excessive pressure, high stress levels, and a lack of creativity. The government has introduced reforms to reduce academic stress and promote holistic development. The education system is known for its high academic standards, with students excelling in math and science.  

**Education in Canada**  
Canada’s education system is decentralized, with provinces managing education. Compulsory education begins at age 5 and ends at age 18. The system includes early childhood, primary, secondary, and tertiary education. The curriculum is standardized but varies by province. Canada spends approximately 5.2% of GDP on education, with strong investment in tertiary education. The system promotes diversity and inclusion, with language-based and religious schools recognized under constitutional protections. Challenges include teacher shortages, funding disparities, and access to quality education. The education system is highly regarded internationally, with strong performance in international assessments.  

**Education in the Netherlands**  
The Netherlands has a well-structured education system with compulsory education starting at age 5 and ending at age 18. The system includes primary (Grades 1–8), secondary (Grades 9–12), and tertiary education. The curriculum is standardized, with a focus on practical and academic skills. The system emphasizes inclusivity and student autonomy, with a strong focus on extracurricular activities. The education system is known for its high quality and international competitiveness. The government has introduced reforms to improve equity and access, including better support for students with special needs. The system has faced criticism for early academic tracking and limited flexibility in student pathways.  

**Education in the Czech Republic**  
The Czech Republic’s education system is structured into primary (Grades 1–9), secondary (Grades 10–13), and tertiary levels. Primary education is divided into two stages, with a focus on core subjects. Secondary education includes general and vocational tracks, with students preparing for university or the workforce. The system emphasizes academic rigor and practical skills. The education system has seen reforms to improve access and equity, with a focus on modernizing curricula. The government has invested in teacher training and infrastructure. Challenges remain in rural areas and in the quality of vocational education.

**Education in Russia**  
Russia’s education system includes general and professional education. Primary education (Grades 1–4) is followed by secondary education (Grades 5–11), with a focus on standardized testing. The system emphasizes academic rigor and practical skills. The education system has faced criticism for outdated curricula, lack of innovation, and gender disparities. The government has introduced reforms to improve access and equity, including better support for students with special needs. The system is known for its high quality in certain fields, such as engineering and medicine.  

**Education in Pakistan**  
Pakistan’s education system is structured into primary (Grades 1–8), secondary (Grades 9–12), and tertiary levels. The system is inspired by the English model, with a focus on core subjects. The government has introduced reforms to improve access and equity, including better teacher training and infrastructure. The system faces challenges in gender disparity, teacher shortages, and outdated curricula. Literacy rates have improved, rising from 43.92% in 1998 to 60.65% in 2023. The education system is known for its high performance in certain fields, such as science and technology.
