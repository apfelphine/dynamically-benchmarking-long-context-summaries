# Education Systems Overview

---

## New Zealand

**History and Structure:**  
New Zealand’s education evolved from Māori wānanga, passing tribal knowledge, to missionary-led, European-style schooling in the 19th century. Free, compulsory, secular state education was established in 1878. The system comprises early childhood (Te Whāriki curriculum), primary (Years 1–8), secondary (Years 9–13), and tertiary education.

**School Types:**  
Three main types—state (government-funded), state-integrated (religious or special character, with attendance dues), and private schools. Home-schooling and correspondence options exist. Enrolment is generally open, with geographic zoning in crowded areas.

**Curriculum and Qualifications:**  
National curriculum covers English/Māori, math, sciences, arts, health, languages, social sciences, and technology. NCEA is the main secondary qualification, with Cambridge and IB as alternatives.

**Māori Education:**  
Efforts revitalized Māori language education via kōhanga reo (pre-school), kura kaupapa (full immersion). Māori children in Māori-medium schools outperform those in English-medium.

**Funding and Standards:**  
Schools receive government funding, adjusted for size and decile, plus voluntary parental donations. Tertiary education is partly government-funded, with loans and allowances for students. Recent concerns involve declining numeracy/literacy and school bullying rates.

## Saudi Arabia

**Historical Context:**  
Rooted in Islamic Kuttabs, Saudi Arabian education has expanded since the 20th century, now managed primarily by the Ministry of Education. Education is free for all, with significant government investment.

**Structure:**  
Pre-primary (kindergarten, not compulsory) leads to compulsory primary (6 years), then intermediate (3 years) and secondary (3 years). Secondary paths include general and technical/vocational tracks.

**Higher Education:**  
Saudi Arabia has 24+ universities and robust scholarship programs for overseas study. Women constitute most university students, reflecting significant growth in female education.

**Special and Gifted Education:**  
Gifted education is prioritized via enrichment programs (like Mawhiba), though access is limited. Special education includes both mainstreaming and specialized institutions, facing challenges in resources, teacher training, and facilities.

**Private and International Schools:**  
A substantial private sector operates, with strict regulations. Numerous international schools serve expatriates and diplomatic communities, implementing diverse curricula.

**Criticism and Reforms:**  
The system is criticized for rote learning, lack of critical thinking, and religious intolerance, though recent reforms have aimed to address these issues and modernize content.

## Mexico

**Structure and Integration:**  
Basic education consists of primary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and high school (10–12). Primary and secondary are compulsory. Integration of special needs students is mandated but faces implementation challenges due to access, teacher training, and resources.

**Quality & Progression:**  
Although most complete primary, progression rates drop sharply at higher levels, linked to rural infrastructure and budget constraints. Education reform in 2013 reduced teacher union control.

**Higher Education:**  
Includes public/private universities and vocational institutions, structured around undergraduate (Licenciatura) and postgraduate degrees (Maestría, Doctorado), resembling the Bologna Process. Indigenous enrollment is low, addressed by "Intercultural Universities".

**History:**  
Mexico’s education roots trace from pre-Hispanic schooling, through colonial church-led systems and state secularization, up to contemporary reforms focusing on teacher unions and literacy.

**Literacy and Rankings:**  
Literacy rates have increased substantially, with disparities by region and age. Top institutions include UNAM and ITESM, with strong business schools.

## Bangladesh

**System Structure:**  
Education spans pre-primary, primary (classes 3–8), secondary (9–12), and tertiary. Bengali and English mediums are available. Curriculum choices begin late; science track is most popular.

**Schools and Boards:**  
Numerous boards administer public exams; secondary boards oversee SSC and HSC. NGOs play a major role in informal education, especially for dropouts.

**Madrasah and Refugee Education:**  
Madrasahs (Qawmi and Alia) teach Islamic and general subjects. Refugee education, notably for Rohingya children, is largely supported by international NGOs.

**Challenges:**  
Low per-capita spending, high teacher absenteeism, lacking infrastructure, poverty, and limited seats inhibit access and quality. Gender parity has improved but urban/rural gaps remain.

**Literacy and Reforms:**  
Literacy rates have risen to 74.66% (2022). Government initiatives like compulsory primary education and stipends have increased access.

## Japan

**Development:**  
Education transitioned from Buddhist/Confucian traditions to Western-style schooling after the Meiji Restoration. Modern system includes 6 years of elementary, 3 of lower secondary, 3 of upper secondary, and university.

**Schooling and Curriculum:**  
Education is highly standardized, with rigorous entrance exams. Compulsory education lasts 9 years; nearly all proceed through upper secondary. National curriculum includes Japanese, maths, sciences, languages, social studies, arts, and PE.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities and junior colleges have national, public, and private options. Prestigious universities (University of Tokyo, Kyoto) rank globally.

**Special Needs and Criticism:**  
Special/inclusive education has improved. Criticism targets exam pressure, mental health, overuse of cram schools (juku), bullying, and lack of international outlook.

## Canada

**System and Administration:**  
Education is provincially managed, with variation in structure and curriculum. Federal involvement is limited to military/aboriginal education.

**Structure:**  
Most provinces follow K–12 (kindergarten–grade 12), though Quebec, Ontario, and others differ. English and French public schooling is constitutionally protected.

**Types and Funding:**  
Public, private, religious, and charter schools operate; funding and policies vary widely. Canada spends about 5.2% of GDP on education.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities and vocational institutions grant bachelors, masters, doctorates, and certificates. Canadian universities rank highly internationally.

**Issues:**  
Data collection for equity, standardized exams, and indigenous education are ongoing concerns. Tuition fees are rising; literacy rates are among the highest globally.

## Netherlands

**Structure and Segregation:**  
Education is compulsory from age 5 to 18, with early streaming into vocational (VMBO), general (HAVO), or academic (VWO) secondary tracks. Funding is equal for public and special (religious) schools.

**Curriculum and Progression:**  
Primary education lasts 8 years; secondary pathways are determined by aptitude tests (Cito) and teacher recommendations. Movement between levels is possible but challenging.

**Vocational and Higher Education:**  
Post-secondary includes MBO (junior college), HBO (universities of applied sciences), and WO (research universities). Degrees are structured in accordance with the Bologna Process.

**Criticism:**  
Early tracking can misplace gifted or disadvantaged children; educational performance has declined in recent international rankings.

## Czech Republic

**Stages:**  
Compulsory education is divided into a nine-year elementary stage, followed by various secondary types: grammar schools, vocational, professional schools, and lyceums.

**Maturita Exam:**  
Completion of secondary (via four-year lyceum/grammar/professional schools) requires passing Maturita, mandatory for university admission.

**Higher Education:**  
Comprises professional schools (VOŠ), public and private universities, and includes bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral programs.

**Inclusivity:**  
Special schools for disabilities exist; integrated education is increasing in mainstream schools.

## South Korea

**History and Structure:**  
Heavily influenced by Confucian meritocracy; rapid modernization post-World War II. System includes elementary (6 years), middle (3), high school (3), and tertiary (university/college).

**Exams and Competition:**  
Intense competition, exam focus (CSAT for university entrance), and widespread private tutoring (hagwons) characterize the education system.

**Vocational/Meister Schools:**  
Recent reforms promote skilled trades and vocational education; social stigma remains but employment rates for graduates are high.

**Criticism:**  
Excessive focus on university entrance, student mental health challenges, neglect of creativity/physical education, and skill mismatch persist.

## Poland

**Structure:**  
Education starts with primary (now 8 years), then upper secondary (liceum, technikum, vocational) following reforms in 2016–2019.

**Matura and Tertiary:**  
Matura exam at the end of secondary school enables access to higher education, which follows the Bologna cycles (bachelor, masters, doctorate).

**History and Literacy:**  
Poland has a storied tradition with long-established universities. Literacy rates and educational rankings have improved in recent decades.

## Russia

**Levels and Structure:**  
Education includes preschool, primary (4 years), basic (5 years), and secondary (2 years), with professional/vocational routes and higher education (bachelor, specialist, master).

**Schools and Exams:**  
Ninety-six percent complete lower secondary; the Unified State Examination standardizes university entry. Vocational schools, colleges and technicums provide alternative routes.

**Higher Education:**  
Includes specialist, bachelor’s, and master’s degrees; post-graduate “Candidate of Sciences” and “Doctor of Sciences.” Recent reforms align degrees with the Bologna Process.

**Inclusivity and Outreach:**  
Special schools and programs for physical and mental disabilities are present but not always fully integrated; outreach and patriotic education initiatives are expanding.

## Pakistan

**Stages:**  
Comprises primary (grades 1–5), middle (6–8), secondary (9–12), and tertiary education, with SSC and HSSC exams. English and Urdu mediums, plus regional languages and madrassas, are common.

**Quality and Challenges:**  
Primary net attendance is 67.5%, lower than neighbors. Universities offer a range of bachelor, master, and doctoral degrees; growing international recognition but quality concerns remain.

**Gender and Literacy:**  
Significant gender disparity, especially in rural areas. Female literacy and educational participation are rising but still lag. Transgender individuals were formally protected in 2018.

**Funding and Reform:**  
Education spending is about 2% of GDP, with ambitious but unmet targets for increase. Chronic infrastructure and resource deficits impede progress.

**Achievements:**  
Pakistan has produced notable scientists and scholars in global academia.

**Religion in Education:**  
Madrasas fill gaps left by inadequate public schools, with associated controversies about content and extremism.
