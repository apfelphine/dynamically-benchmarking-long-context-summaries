### **Education in New Zealand**

#### **Historical Context**
New Zealand’s education system evolved from Māori traditional knowledge systems, which emphasized oral traditions, spiritual understanding, and practical skills, to formal European-style schooling introduced in the early 19th century. Missionaries established the first schools in the Bay of Islands, initially teaching in Māori. By the 1850s, Māori trade schools were established, but conflicts, such as the New Zealand Wars, disrupted progress. The system transitioned to English-medium instruction, though Māori-medium education persisted in some areas.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Early Childhood Education (ECE):** Includes kindergarten, Puna Reo, and licensed early childhood education centers. At least 50% of teachers must be qualified, with some services offering cultural or language-specific curricula (e.g., Montessori, Steiner).
- **Primary and Secondary Education:** Free and compulsory from age 5 to 19, with 84% of students staying until at least age 17. The school year runs from late January to mid-December, divided into four terms. Curriculum follows the *New Zealand Curriculum* (NZC) or *Te Marautanga o Aotearoa* (Māori-medium).
- **Types of Schools:**
  - **State Schools:** Government-funded, free to citizens.
  - **State-Integrated Schools:** Former private schools integrated into the state system, retaining religious or specialized character.
  - **Private Schools:** Funded by tuition fees, with less government support.
  - **Alternative Schools:** Charter schools (now state-integrated) and home-schooling options.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** English, mathematics, science, social sciences, technology, and health/physical education.
- **Māori Language:** Revitalization efforts include kura kaupapa (Māori-medium schools) and immersion programs. Enrollment in Māori-medium education has declined slightly but remains significant.
- **Grading System:** Uses a 1–5 scale (1 = fail, 5 = excellent), with additional grading for optional subjects.
- **Exams:** National Certificate of Educational Achievement (NCEA) for senior students, with some schools offering Cambridge International Examinations (CIE) or the International Baccalaureate (IB).

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** State and state-integrated schools receive per-student funding, with additional support for low-decile schools. Private schools rely on tuition fees.
- **Challenges:** Bullying is a widespread issue, with high rates of cyberbullying and physical violence. Educational standards have faced criticism, particularly in math and science, with declining international rankings post-reform.
- **Māori Education:** Specialized schools and programs aim to improve outcomes for Māori students, with notable success in kura kaupapa schools.

### **Education in Saudi Arabia**

#### **Historical Overview**
Education in Saudi Arabia traces back to Islamic teachings, with schools (kuttabs) established by wealthy Muslims and later by the Ottomans. The first Department of Education was founded in 1926, and the first university, King Saud University, was established in 1957. The system emphasizes religious studies, particularly Quranic memorization, alongside secular subjects. Criticisms include rote learning, limited critical thinking, and a focus on rote memorization over innovation.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Pre-Primary Education:** Kindergarten for ages 3–5, though attendance is not compulsory.
- **Primary Education:** Compulsory for ages 6–12, covering Arabic, Islamic studies, mathematics, science, and art. English was introduced in 2021.
- **Intermediate and Secondary Education:** Three years each, with options for general or specialized secondary education (e.g., technical secondary institutes).
- **Tertiary Education:** Includes government universities (e.g., King Saud University) and private institutions. Women constitute 60% of university students, with a focus on STEM fields.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** Arabic, Islamic studies, mathematics, science, social studies, and physical education.
- **Religious Focus:** Islamic studies dominate, with memorization of the Quran and interpretation (Tafsir) as core components.
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for primary and secondary levels, though criticism exists over rote learning and lack of critical analysis.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Free for all students, with public spending on education rising significantly. Private schools receive subsidies and free textbooks.
- **Challenges:** Criticisms include poor teacher training, low retention rates, and a focus on rote learning over innovation. The system has been accused of promoting intolerance and stifling critical thought. Recent reforms aim to modernize education, including a shift toward problem-solving and vocational training.

### **Education in Mexico**

#### **Historical Context**
Mexico’s education system dates back to pre-Hispanic times, with schools like calmecac (for elites) and cuicacalli (for priests). Spanish colonization introduced Catholic schools, and liberal reforms in the 19th century sought to separate church and state. The system evolved through periods of centralization and decentralization, with significant reforms in the 20th century.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Basic Education:** Divided into three stages:
  - **Primary School (Primaria):** Grades 1–6 (ages 6–12).
  - **Junior High School (Secundaria):** Grades 7–9 (ages 12–15).
  - **High School (Preparatoria):** Grades 10–12 (ages 15–18).
- **Vocational Education:** Técnica and telesecundaria provide distance learning and vocational training.
- **Higher Education:** Includes public and private universities, with a Bologna Process alignment. Popular fields include engineering, law, and education.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** Spanish, mathematics, science, social studies, and physical education.
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for primary and secondary levels, with recent reforms focusing on national curriculum standards.
- **Challenges:** Low completion rates for secondary education (45% finish), with rural schools lacking infrastructure. Criticisms include grade inflation, lack of critical thinking, and overemphasis on rote learning.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Primarily provincial, with federal support for Indigenous education. Recent reforms aim to decentralize control and improve teacher training.
- **Challenges:** Persistent issues include low literacy rates, rural school shortages, and teacher union influence. Recent reforms have faced resistance, particularly from the SNTE (National Union of Education Workers).

### **Education in Bangladesh**

#### **Historical Context**
Bangladesh’s education system was influenced by British colonial rule, with a focus on English-language education. The National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB) developed textbooks in both Bengali and English. Madrasahs (Islamic schools) also play a significant role, particularly in rural areas.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Pre-Primary to Secondary:** Divided into three levels:
  - **Pre-Primary (Class K–2):** Ages 3–5.
  - **Primary (Class 3–8):** Ages 6–12.
  - **Secondary (Class 9–12):** Ages 13–16.
- **Tertiary Education:** Regulated by the University Grants Commission, with public, private, and international universities. Madrasahs offer Islamic education alongside general curriculum.
- **Alternative Education:** International schools (English/French), religiously affiliated schools, and NGO-run non-formal schools.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** Bengali, English, mathematics, science, social studies, and religious education (Islam, Hinduism, Christianity).
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for PSC (5th grade), JSC (8th grade), SSC (10th grade), and HSC (12th grade). Grading uses a 5.0 scale (A+ = 5.0).
- **Challenges:** Low primary school completion rates, gender disparity, and lack of infrastructure in rural areas. Madrasahs are a growing alternative, particularly for the poor.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Public expenditure on education is low (2% of GDP), with significant disparities between urban and rural areas. Private schools rely on tuition fees.
- **Challenges:** High dropout rates, especially in rural areas, and gender inequality. Recent reforms aim to improve access and quality, including free textbooks and stipends for female students.

### **Education in Japan**

#### **Historical Context**
Japan’s education system began with Buddhist and Confucian teachings during the Asuka period. The Meiji Restoration (1868) introduced Western-style education, with compulsory schooling modeled after Prussia. The system emphasized discipline, academic rigor, and social order, leading to high literacy rates and strong academic performance.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Early Childhood Education:** Kindergarten (ages 6–7) and preschool (ages 3–5).
- **Primary and Secondary Education:**
  - **Elementary School (Shōgakkō):** Grades 1–6 (ages 6–12).
  - **Junior High School (Chūgakkō):** Grades 7–9 (ages 12–15).
  - **Senior High School (Kōtōgakkō):** Grades 10–12 (ages 15–18).
- **Higher Education:** Universities (daigaku), junior colleges (tanki daigaku), and colleges of technology (kōtō senmon gakkō). The system is highly competitive, with intense focus on entrance exams.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** Japanese, mathematics, science, social studies, English, and physical education.
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for elementary and junior high schools, with rigorous entrance exams for universities.
- **Challenges:** High pressure on students, leading to stress, bullying, and mental health issues. Criticisms include overemphasis on exams, lack of creativity, and limited free time.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Publicly funded, with heavy investment in higher education. Private schools are rare.
- **Challenges:** Bullying is widespread, with high rates of cyberbullying. The system has been criticized for fostering passive learners and lacking critical thinking. Recent reforms aim to reduce exam pressure and promote holistic education.

### **Education in Canada**

#### **Historical Context**
Canada’s education system was introduced by British and French colonizers, with compulsory education introduced in the late 19th century. The system is decentralized, with provincial and territorial governments managing education. Recent reforms focus on equity, inclusivity, and standardized testing.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Early Childhood Education:** Kindergarten (ages 4–5) and pre-primary (ages 3–4).
- **Primary and Secondary Education:**
  - **Elementary School:** Grades 1–6 (ages 5–11).
  - **Junior High School:** Grades 7–9 (ages 11–14).
  - **Senior High School:** Grades 10–12 (ages 14–17).
- **Tertiary Education:** Universities and vocational institutions, with a focus on research and applied education. The system aligns with the Bologna Process.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** English, French (where applicable), mathematics, science, social studies, and physical education.
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for provincial certification, with recent reforms focusing on equity and inclusivity.
- **Challenges:** Grade inflation, lack of critical thinking, and overemphasis on standardized testing. Recent reforms aim to improve teacher training and student engagement.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Provincial and territorial, with federal support for Indigenous education. Public spending on education is high (5.2% of GDP).
- **Challenges:** Gender disparity in enrollment, particularly in rural areas. Recent reforms aim to address equity issues, including identity-based data collection for disadvantaged groups.

### **Education in the Netherlands**

#### **Historical Context**
The Netherlands introduced compulsory education in the early 19th century, with reforms aimed at modernizing society. The system is stratified, with schools categorized as public, special (religious), or general-special (neutral). Recent reforms focus on reducing grade repetition and improving teacher autonomy.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Early Childhood Education:** Kindergarten (ages 4–6).
- **Primary Education:** Grades 1–8 (ages 5–12).
- **Secondary Education:**
  - **VMBO (Vocational):** 4 years, prepares for vocational training.
  - **HAVO (General):** 5 years, prepares for university.
  - **VWO (Academic):** 6 years, prepares for university.
- **Higher Education:** Universities of applied sciences (HBO) and research universities (WO), with a focus on vocational and academic training.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** Dutch, mathematics, English, history, and science.
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for primary and secondary levels, with recent reforms focusing on teacher autonomy and student engagement.
- **Challenges:** Grade repetition is common, leading to social and academic disparities. Recent reforms aim to reduce grade repetition and improve teacher training.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Publicly funded, with private schools rare. Recent reforms aim to reduce grade repetition and improve teacher autonomy.
- **Challenges:** Bullying and social disparities are significant issues. Recent reforms focus on equity and inclusivity.

The Czech Republic’s education system dates back to the 14th century, with the Jagiellonian University in Prague founded in 1364. The system was influenced by the Communist era, with recent reforms focusing on academic rigor and vocational training.

- **Secondary Education:**
  - **Vocational Schools:** 2–3 years, prepares for vocational training.
  - **Grammar Schools:** 4 years, prepares for university.

- **Tertiary Education:** Universities and higher professional schools, with a focus on research and applied education.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** Czech, mathematics, science, social studies, and foreign languages.
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for elementary and secondary levels, with recent reforms focusing on academic rigor.
- **Challenges:** Low teacher salaries and lack of resources in rural areas. Recent reforms aim to improve teacher training and student engagement.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Publicly funded, with recent reforms focusing on academic rigor and vocational training.
- **Challenges:** Bullying and social disparities are significant issues. Recent reforms focus on equity and inclusivity.

### **Education in South Korea**

#### **Historical Context**
South Korea’s education system was heavily influenced by Confucian values, with a focus on meritocracy and academic achievement. The system has undergone significant reforms in recent decades, with recent efforts to reduce academic pressure and promote vocational training.

#### **Education Structure**
- **Early Childhood Education:** Kindergarten (ages 3–6).
- **Primary Education:** Grades 1–6 (ages 6–12).
- **Secondary Education:**
  - **Middle School:** Grades 7–9 (ages 12–15).
  - **High School:** Grades 10–12 (ages 15–18).
- **Vocational Education:** Meister Schools and vocational high schools, with a focus on technical and vocational training.
- **Higher Education:** Universities and colleges, with a focus on research and applied education.

#### **Curriculum and Assessment**
- **Core Subjects:** Korean, mathematics, science, social studies, and foreign languages.
- **Assessment:** Standardized exams for primary and secondary levels, with recent reforms focusing on academic rigor and vocational training.
- **Challenges:** High academic pressure, leading to stress, bullying, and mental health issues. Recent reforms aim to reduce exam pressure and promote vocational training.

#### **Funding and Challenges**
- **Funding:** Publicly funded, with recent reforms focusing on academic rigor and vocational training.
- **Challenges:** Bullying and social disparities are significant issues. Recent reforms focus on equity and inclusivity, including the introduction of Meister Schools.
