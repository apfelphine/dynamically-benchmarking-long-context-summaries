# Education Systems: Concise Summary

---

## Portugal

**History and Structure:**  
Education in Portugal dates back to the 13th century, with major expansion efforts in the 20th century. The country experienced high illiteracy rates until state interventions, especially under the Estado Novo regime, which rapidly increased elementary education. Education now consists of pre-primary, basic (nine years, three cycles), and secondary (three years, with general, professional, and artistic tracks). Attendance in secondary education became compulsory in 2012.

**Higher Education:**  
Portugal has university and polytechnic subsystems, offering degrees up to PhD. The Bologna process reformed the structure in 2006, but higher education attainment remains low compared to EU averages, although PISA scores have improved. Admission is competitive and relies on national exams. Professional, recurrent, and apprenticeship education coexist alongside traditional paths.

**Issues:**  
Challenges include historical underfunding, rapid expansion sometimes at the expense of quality, persistent functional illiteracy, and reputation issues in private institutions. The system underwent significant modernization in the 2000s, but disparities remain.

## South Africa

**Structure and Policies:**  
South Africa’s education is governed by the Department of Basic Education and Higher Education, with grades grouped in phases from kindergarten (Grade R) through Grade 12. Funding is mainly public, with private and independent schools existing alongside government schools.

**Performance and Technology:**  
Performance remains low due to factors such as under-trained teachers and insufficient resources, leading to high dropout rates and school violence. ICT use is expanding but is hindered by poor infrastructure and teacher preparedness, though the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated digital adoption. School fees vary widely, affecting access for low-income families.

**Higher Education:**  
The sector is large and diverse but faces issues like graduate unemployment and historical inequalities. The system was restructured post-apartheid, resulting in mergers and a more centralized approach.

## Australia

**Structure:**  
Australia’s education system, administered mainly at the state level, features compulsory education from primary (usually age 5/6) through secondary (to age 15–17). The Australian Curriculum applies nationwide, and school types include government, Catholic, and private schools. Most students attend government schools.

**Specialized and International Education:**  
Special schools, selective schools, and international schools exist, supporting diverse educational needs. Indigenous education and bilingual programs address historical disadvantages and cultural needs.

**Tertiary Education:**  
Australian universities and TAFE (vocational colleges) are largely public and internationally competitive, with significant international student enrollment. Government and states fund most education, with recent major reforms in school funding agreements.

**Issues:**  
Declining PISA results point to challenges in performance, funding equity, rural access, and Indigenous education. Violence in schools and teacher retention are also ongoing concerns.

## Finland

**System and Philosophy:**  
Finland’s education system is highly egalitarian and publicly funded, emphasizing social equity, free lunches, small classes, and highly qualified teachers (master’s degree required). The structure includes early childhood education, nine years of comprehensive schooling, and compulsory upper secondary/vocational education (ages 7–18).

**Higher Education:**  
Tertiary education is split between research-oriented universities and universities of applied sciences. Admissions are strictly merit-based, with no tuition for EU/EEA students; non-EU students pay fees.

**Innovations and Outcomes:**  
Finland is recognized for its student-centered approach, lack of standardized testing, phenomenon-based learning, and high performance on international assessments. Ongoing reforms include curriculum changes and system-wide restructuring.

## Romania

**Structure and Progress:**  
Romania mandates 15 years of education, beginning with kindergarten, followed by primary, gymnasium, and high school (with multiple academic and technical tracks). Literacy campaigns in the 20th century eradicated most illiteracy.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities and colleges operate on the Bologna framework (three cycles: bachelor, master, PhD) and offer both domestic and internationally oriented programs. Admission is competitive, especially in state institutions.

**Challenges:**  
Education quality and accessibility vary, especially between urban and rural areas. Underfunding, classroom shortages, policy implementation delays, and inadequacies in rural education persist.

## United States

**History and Structure:**  
U.S. education developed through state and local initiatives, with compulsory K–12 education and diverse post-secondary options. Systems include public, private, and homeschool models, with curricula and funding varying widely across states.

**Higher Education:**  
U.S. higher education offers broad flexibility and deregulation, with a range of public and private universities, community colleges, and vocational institutions. Tuition costs are high, often resulting in significant student debt.

**Issues:**  
Challenges include inequality in educational opportunity, performance gaps based on race and socioeconomic status, teacher shortages and low pay, standardized testing controversies, credential inflation, and systemic funding disparities. Educational achievements correlate closely with income, yet PISA scores lag behind other developed countries.

## Malaysia

**History and Structure:**  
The Malaysian education system consists of preschool, primary, secondary, and post-secondary (pre-university and tertiary) levels. Language of instruction and school types reflect Malaysia’s multicultural demographics (Malay, Chinese, Tamil, and English-medium schools). The Dual Language Programme (DLP) promotes bilingualism in science and math.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities, polytechnics, and colleges operate both publicly and privately, with multiple international branch campuses. Quotas and merit-based admissions affect participation, especially for non-Bumiputera groups.

**Issues:**  
Problems include disparities due to race-based quotas, language debates, classroom shortages, underfunding, rural/urban gaps, and gender imbalances. Recent reforms target curriculum modernization, equity, and increased compulsory schooling.

## Philippines

**History and Structure:**  
Philippine education has evolved through colonial influences to the current K–12 system (kindergarten, six years elementary, four years junior high, two years senior high). The system includes public and private schools, indigenous and religious provisions, and alternative learning systems for non-traditional learners.

**Higher Education:**  
Supervised by CHED, the sector encompasses state and private institutions with diverse academic programs. Tertiary education has free tuition in public universities, but access and completion rates vary.

**Challenges:**  
Persistent issues include inadequate funding, overcrowded classrooms, low teacher salaries, and poor learning outcomes, with regional, economic, and gender gaps. Affordability and alignment with labor market needs are significant concerns, as is the impact of disruptions like COVID-19 on student learning and wellbeing.
