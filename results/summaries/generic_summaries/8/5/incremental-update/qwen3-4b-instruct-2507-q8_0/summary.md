# **Final Summary of Portugal's, Romania's, and the United States' Education Systems (Including Malaysia and the Philippines)**

The education systems of Portugal, Romania, and the United States reflect distinct philosophies and structural realities—balancing equity, standardization, and access—while Malaysia and the Philippines provide critical comparative insights into bilingualism, cultural identity, and reform-driven adaptation rooted in historical and social context.

**Portugal** maintains a state-funded, exam-driven system with a nine-year Basic Education cycle (Grades 1–9), followed by tracks in general, professional, or artistic secondary education. National assessments and a diploma are required for higher education, which is divided into research-oriented universities and practice-focused polytechnics under the Bologna Process. Access remains selective and financially strained, especially post-2008, with private institutions criticized for inequity and international schools showing strong performance. Challenges persist in policy consistency, school violence, and resource disparities.

**Romania** emphasizes structured progression, early accountability, and public equity through compulsory kindergarten (ages 6–7) and a rigorous academic framework. The *Evaluarea Națională* (8th-grade test) and the National Baccalaureate Exam (*bac*) determine high school admission and graduation, with clear academic pathways in theoretical, technical, and vocational tracks. The system is highly standardized and transparent, though rural schools face underfunding, long commutes, and unequal access, reinforcing urban-rural disparities.

**Finland** stands in contrast with a holistic, equity-centered model that prioritizes well-being, teacher autonomy, and minimal competition. High access (60–70% of youth) and strong teacher support exist, though economic pressures have reduced student places, especially in applied sciences. Policy is guided by the Bologna Process and national equity goals.

**The United States** is marked by innovation and global influence but suffers from deep structural inequities. Key challenges include:  
- **Sex education**: Debates over comprehensive vs. abstinence-based models persist, reflecting parental and cultural tensions.  
- **LGBTQ+ inclusion**: At least 20 states have enacted restrictive laws (e.g., “Don’t Say Gay”), limiting LGBTQ+ curriculum access.  
- **Curriculum control**: States like Texas and California shape content through textbook selection, often promoting Eurocentric or mythologized narratives.  
- **Immigrant student placement**: Birthday-based grading disadvantages mid-year arrivals, leading to learning gaps and limited English proficiency.  
- **Racial and economic inequality**: A persistent achievement gap exists, with Black and Hispanic students underperforming and dropping out at higher rates; McKinsey estimates closing these gaps could boost U.S. GDP by 9–16%.  
- **School-to-prison pipeline**: Punitive policies disproportionately affect marginalized youth, undermining college access.  
- **Standardized testing**: Exacerbates inequities due to disparities in health, housing, and resources; achievement gaps remain systemic despite slight narrowing.  
- **Teacher shortages**: Driven by low salaries (average ₱25,000–₱30,000 in the Philippines, comparable to U.S. rural contexts), burnout, and poor job satisfaction; many teachers moonlight. Teacher education quality is low, with poor licensure exam performance and ineffective pedagogy.  
- **Parental and literacy challenges**: Limited parental involvement in literacy development correlates with higher incarceration rates.

**Malaysia** features a bilingual, medium-of-instruction-based system with a clear divide between National Schools (SK) and National-type Schools (SJK), emphasizing Malay and English as compulsory subjects. The **Dual Language Program (DLP)** promotes English in STEM to enhance global competitiveness, though concerns remain over Bahasa Melayu proficiency and strict implementation. Chinese Independent Schools offer Mandarin-medium education and the UEC, which is internationally recognized but not yet accepted by public universities. A push to make secondary education compulsory (2024–2025) and reforms under the 2013–2025 National Education Blueprint aim to improve equity, critical thinking, and multilingualism. However, racial polarization, underfunding, and a 90% Bumiputera quota in public universities perpetuate ethnic segmentation and limit access for minorities.

**The Philippines** offers a historical lens on education shaped by colonialism, nationalism, and reform. The K–12 system (introduced 2008, fully implemented 2012) expanded basic education to 12 years, including compulsory kindergarten and a mother-tongue-based multilingual approach in early grades. It includes eight core learning areas and four senior high tracks (Academic, TVL, Sports, Arts), with public schools free and accessible. However, the system faces severe challenges:  
- **148,000 classroom shortages** (2025), **poor infrastructure**, and **large class sizes** (high teacher-learner ratio).  
- **Underfunding** and **corruption** undermine policy implementation and educational quality.  
- **Low teacher salaries** (₱25,000–₱30,000), poor teacher education (low licensure pass rates), and widespread use of ineffective teaching practices.  
- **Affordability issues** in higher education, including informal fees and high cost recovery, limit access.  
- A **mismatch between education and labor market needs** leads to underemployment and brain drain.  
- The **pandemic caused a 61% loss of expected schooling**, worsening learning loss and mental health outcomes.  
- Reforms such as Republic Acts No. 11713 and 11997 aim to improve teacher training and provide allowances, but systemic underinvestment continues to hinder progress.

Despite progress in access and curriculum reform, all systems—especially in the Global South—struggle with **underfunding, teacher shortages, inequity, and policy implementation gaps**. The path to equitable, high-quality education requires sustained investment, culturally responsive curricula, teacher support, and structural reforms that address both immediate needs and long-term systemic barriers.
