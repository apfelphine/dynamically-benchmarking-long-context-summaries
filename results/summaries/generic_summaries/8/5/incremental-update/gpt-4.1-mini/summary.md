# Final Summary of Education in Portugal, South Africa, Australia, Finland, Romania, the United States, Malaysia, and the Philippines

## Education in Portugal

Portugal’s education system evolved from its ecclesiastical 13th-century origins to modern public education aiming to reduce high adult functional illiteracy (~48%). Post-1950s reforms expanded access; the 2006 Bologna Process standardized higher education degrees. The system features nine years of basic and three years of secondary education with general, artistic, or professional tracks and combines national exams with continuous assessment. Challenges include regional disparities, policy instability, dropout rates, and quality concerns for private and polytechnic institutions. Higher education divides into theory-focused universities and practice-oriented polytechnics, with over 35% of 20-year-olds enrolled mainly in public institutions. Teacher training mandates Licenciado degrees and master's qualifications since 2007. Distance education and adult learning coexist with private expansions and the “Escola Segura” initiative for safety.

## Education in South Africa

Rooted in Dutch and British missionary schools, South African education was shaped by apartheid-era segregation and underfunded Bantu Education, prompting resistance such as the 1976 Soweto Uprising. Post-apartheid reforms unified governance, recognized 12 official languages, and removed oppressive curricula, though English remains predominant early on. Most students attend government “No Fee” schools, while private schools provide better quality but limited accessibility. University education requires Matriculation Endorsement, but challenges include graduate unemployment and low enrollment in STEM and health fields. ICT expansion aims for inclusion and broadband targets by 2030, yet digital divides persist, especially in rural settings. Socioeconomic problems—teen pregnancy, crime, violence—continue, worsening under COVID-19.

## Education in Australia

Compulsory since late 19th/early 20th century, Australia’s over 4 million students attend predominantly government (64%) and Catholic/private (36%) schools. The Australian Curriculum spans from preschool (optional) to Year 12, with retention around 80%. Senior secondary yields certificates or the International Baccalaureate; literacy and numeracy are monitored via NAPLAN. Aboriginal bilingual education promotes two-way bilingualism to improve outcomes amid enduring achievement gaps. School violence incidents are increasing. Tertiary education comprises universities and TAFE institutes with over a million enrollments, hosting the world’s highest share (26.7%) of international students. Federal and state funding support equitable reforms.

## Education in Finland

Originating from Lutheran literacy work and the 1640 University of Helsinki, compulsory education began in 1921 with a comprehensive 1–9 grade system in the 1970s. Universal free daycare and preschool emphasize holistic development. Basic education is municipal, free, non-selective, with small classes, multilingualism, minimal homework, and integrated free meals and healthcare. Upper secondary education, either academic or vocational, is also free with selective admission. Teacher education demands master’s degrees, conferring high professional status. Higher education splits between theoretically oriented universities and practical universities of applied sciences, following Bologna Process reforms. Adult lifelong learning is supported via Open Universities. Recent curricula focus on phenomenon-based learning, scientific skills, and AI innovation. Despite challenges like academic unemployment and Swedish language requirements, Finnish methods are globally influential.

## Education in Romania

Compulsory education covers 15 years from kindergarten through grade 12, achieving near-universal literacy (~99%), though minorities like Romani and Turkish face higher illiteracy. Kindergarten expansion with compulsory middle and large groups responds to public capacity shortages and private foreign-language offerings. Elementary and gymnasium education are mostly free but overcrowded, with double shifts common. Secondary tracks include theoretical, bilingual, technical, vocational, and service-oriented schools, culminating in a complex Baccalaureate exam. Rural high dropout rates (16–25%) and underfunding contribute to access issues beyond grade 8. Illegal parental fees exist despite tuition-free public schooling. Roman Catholic and Orthodox religious instruction is optional. Brain drain persists among the youth studying abroad. Over 531,000 tertiary students attend 56 public and 41 private universities with Bologna standards.

## Education in the United States

With origins in pioneering free public education and compulsory laws by 1917, the US system integrates local governance with federal funding supporting disadvantaged groups. Key reforms include desegregation, special education laws, and standardized testing mandates (e.g., No Child Left Behind and Every Student Succeeds Act). In 2022, 89% of adults completed high school; 34% hold bachelor’s or higher degrees, though 14% have very low literacy skills. High school graduation rates hover around 82%, with gender and socioeconomic disparities impacting outcomes. Education covers elementary through high school (84–85% public enrollment), with over 4,495 colleges and universities offering diverse degrees. Rising tuition and soaring student debt (average $23,200 in 2010) strain students. Adjunct faculty shortages and turnover affect quality. Persistent racial achievement gaps, teacher shortages, safety concerns, curriculum disputes, and immigrant student issues remain. Affirmative action policies face judicial challenges.

## Education in Malaysia

From Islamic pondok schools to British-influenced colonial education, Malaysia’s diverse system includes Malay, Chinese, Tamil, and English medium schools. Post-independence reforms favored Malay dominance by the 1970s while preserving vernacular rights, upheld by courts. The school year is semestral, covering preschool (mostly private), primary, secondary (Forms 1–5), pre-university, and tertiary education. Malay National Schools dominate, with Chinese Independent High Schools maintaining distinct curricula and exams (UEC) accepted internationally though not by local public universities. Secondary education is becoming compulsory by 2024, with entrance quotas heavily favoring Bumiputeras (up to 90%), disadvantaging Chinese and Indian minorities. Tertiary education is subsidized, with private-college partnerships and brain drain concerns. Islamic religious schools coexist alongside vernacular debates. Uniform mandates will lessen in 2024. National Education Blueprints emphasize equity, cultural integration, language balancing, and infrastructure improvements, but racial and language tensions persist, alongside gender imbalances in technical fields.

## Education in the Philippines

The Philippines progressed from informal indigenous education through Spanish and American colonial influences to a modern system embodying multilingual, multicultural frameworks. Governance is split among DepEd (basic), CHED (higher), and TESDA (technical-vocational). The 2017 K–12 reform extended basic education to 12 years and made kindergarten compulsory. Enrollment reached 28 million in 2022–23, though about 6% remain out of school with pronounced regional disparities. Completion rates vary across levels; nationwide learning outcomes remain low, with reading and math proficiency under 20% of students. Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education is implemented in early grades. Secondary education offers academic, technical-vocational, sports, and arts tracks, with selective science high schools serving gifted students. Higher education comprises nearly 2,000 institutions, mostly private, with significant foreign student presence. The Alternative Learning System targets out-of-school youth and adults; indigenous education policies promote culturally relevant curricula.

Recent data highlight notable challenges: public classrooms face severe shortages and poor conditions; about 148,000 classrooms are needed due to underfunding and corruption. Teacher numbers exceed 876,000 with heavy workloads and low pay (₱20,754–₱30,000 monthly for public teachers, less for private). New laws promote teacher scholarships, special needs centers, and allowances amid calls for better wages and conditions. Despite free public education, affordability barriers persist, contributing to dropouts and high tertiary cost recovery. Employment mismatches occur with oversupply in some fields and shortages in STEM and vocational sectors, exacerbating brain drain. The COVID-19 pandemic caused significant learning losses and mental health issues due to closures and limited remote learning. Underinvestment affects infrastructure, salaries, and materials. Learning poverty exceeds 90%; low licensure exam pass rates and ineffective teaching methods hinder performance.

In sum, the Philippine education system grapples with infrastructural inadequacies, funding deficits, affordability concerns, and educational outcomes, despite ongoing reforms to improve teacher support, inclusivity, and quality learning experiences.
