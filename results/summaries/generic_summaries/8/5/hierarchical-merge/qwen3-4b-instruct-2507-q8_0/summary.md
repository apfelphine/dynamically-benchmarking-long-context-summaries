# **Master Summary: Comparative Overview of Education Systems in Portugal, South Africa, Australia, Finland, Romania, the United States, Malaysia, and the Philippines**

## **Portugal: Modern Public System with Persistent Inequities**  
Portugal’s education system is a state-funded, publicly accessible model established since the 1960s, marked by the eradication of illiteracy and the expansion of compulsory education from ages 6 to 12. Since 2010, PISA scores have improved steadily, and the adoption of the Bologna Process (2006) has standardized higher education through a three-tier structure (Bachelor, Master, Doctorate). The system follows a clear progression: early childhood (Jardim de Infância, ages 3–5), basic education (Grades 1–9), and secondary education (Grades 10–12), with national exams at key stages and a *Diploma de Ensino Secundário* required for university entry. Admission is competitive, with strict minimum scores and quotas. Challenges persist, including high dropout rates, functional illiteracy (48% of adults), and illiteracy (7.5%), as well as school violence and inequities in private institutions. The *Novas Oportunidades* program supports adult learning based on experience but is criticized for lacking depth.

## **South Africa: Legacy of Inequality and Systemic Disparities**  
Rooted in apartheid-era segregation, South Africa’s education system continues to face deep structural inequities. The 1953 Bantu Education Act institutionalized unequal access, and post-apartheid reforms failed to fully address disparities. Basic education (Grades 1–12) is divided into GET and FET phases, with poor learner-to-teacher ratios (30:1) and unequal funding—poorer schools receive significantly higher per-child grants. English is taught from Grade 1, and the Outcomes-Based Education (OBE) curriculum failed by 2006. Access to ICT remains limited, especially in rural areas, worsening during the pandemic. School-related violence is widespread: 40% of children report crime, and over a fifth of sexual assaults occur in schools. Despite initiatives like *Hlayiseka* and *South Africa Connect*, underfunding and regional gaps persist. Higher education faces high graduate unemployment, driven by a preference for humanities over STEM and health fields.

## **Australia: Structured, High-Performing System with Equity Gaps**  
Australia’s education system is highly standardized through the **Australian Curriculum** (2010), with free government schools and a mix of Catholic and private institutions. Compulsory attendance spans ages 5–6 to 15–17, and the national Year 10–12 retention rate is 79.9%. Bilingual programs—especially in remote Indigenous communities—have proven effective in improving outcomes. Despite strong policy frameworks and a top-tier higher education index (2nd globally in UN rankings), PISA 2022 shows performance gaps: only less than 60% achieve proficiency in any subject, with Australia ranking 9th in reading, 10th in math, and below 60% in science. Key challenges include persistent disadvantages for Indigenous students, lower Year 12 attainment, and school safety concerns—48% of principals in Queensland and South Australia reported physical violence in 2024. Policy inconsistency and rural disparities remain unresolved.

## **Finland: Equitable, Student-Centered Model with Holistic Development**  
Finland is globally recognized for its equity, low stress, and holistic approach. Compulsory education spans 9 years (primary and middle school), with small class sizes, minimal homework, and a strong focus on arts, languages, and outdoor activities. All students receive free meals, health care, and books. Grading is detailed and percentile-based, with repetition rare and failing grades requiring improvement. Upper secondary education (ages 15–19) is now compulsory and offers vocational and academic tracks. The *Ylioppilastutkinto* (matriculation exam) is required for university admission. Teachers are highly trained, holding master’s degrees and entering through competitive processes. Tertiary education includes universities (theoretical) and universities of applied sciences (practical), with tuition fees introduced for non-EEA students. The government promotes lifelong learning through vocational pathways and "free education" institutions. Finland exports its model internationally via *Suomi-koulut* and phenomenon-based learning, emphasizing 21st-century skills. Despite high achievement, concerns exist over declining access in applied sciences and rising academic unemployment.

## **Romania: Centralized, High-Stakes System with Rural Disparities**  
Romania’s 14-year compulsory system features early academic pressure and frequent repetition. Students take the **National Evaluation** in 8th grade to determine high school entry, with top institutions requiring grades above 9.5. The **National Baccalaureate Exam (bac)** is a centralized, digitally graded exam in Romanian, a foreign language, and subject-specific areas, scored on a 1–10 scale with a minimum average of 6.00 per subject. Failure allows up to five retakes, with a September session available. Urban schools offer better resources, while rural schools suffer from underfunding, long commutes, and limited facilities. Class sizes range from 1 to 30, with repetition required for "Nesatisfăcător" grades. Religious education is optional after 2014, sparking debate. Literacy is nearly universal (0.83%), but rural school shortages—up to 25% of children aged 7–14 not attending—remain a major issue. Higher education includes 97 institutions (56 public, 41 private), aligned with the Bologna Process. International collaboration is strong, and Romanian degrees are recognized abroad. However, persistent inequities, illegal class funding (€70M annually), and a brain drain (over 50,000 youth study abroad) highlight systemic weaknesses.

## **United States: Diverse and Expansive System with Deep Structural Inequities**  
The U.S. system evolved from 19th-century reforms, with landmark legislation like *Brown v. Board of Education* and the *Elementary and Secondary Education Act* expanding access. By the 21st century, reforms such as *No Child Left Behind* and *Common Core* emphasized standardized testing, often at the expense of equity. Despite high post-secondary enrollment (89.8% of graduates attend college), only 34.9% earn a bachelor’s degree, revealing unequal outcomes. PISA data shows the U.S. ranks below average in science and math (24th in math, 19th in science), with 21–23% of adults struggling with basic literacy. Systemic inequities persist due to property-tax-based funding, leading to vast disparities between wealthy and low-income districts. Disadvantaged communities face higher dropout rates, lower test scores, and the school-to-prison pipeline. Higher education is diverse—ranging from Ivy League institutions to for-profit schools—but rising tuition and student debt (over $1.5 trillion) limit mobility. Affirmative action was ruled unconstitutional in *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard*, raising access concerns. Curriculum issues—such as lack of consistent sex education and restrictive laws like Florida’s "Don’t Say Gay" bill—reflect ideological influences. Instructional practices like the three-cue literacy system lack scientific support, and cheating rates reach up to 90%. Girls outperform boys in grades and graduation, though boys dominate in STEM.

## **Malaysia: Multilingual System with Ethnic and Language Tensions**  
Malaysia’s education system is shaped by colonial history and complex ethnic, linguistic, and political dynamics. British colonial policies initially marginalized Malay students, leading to the establishment of elite institutions like Malay College Kuala Kangsar (1905). The 1956 Razak Report introduced a multilingual framework, recognizing Malay, English, Chinese, and Tamil-medium schools, with vernacular schools protected by constitutional status. In the 1970s, many English-medium missionary schools transitioned to Malay-medium national schools, though religious groups retained ownership of land and buildings. The 1996 Education Act and subsequent reforms have guided policy evolution. The system includes two main types of primary schools: Malay-medium National Schools (SK) and non-Malay-medium National-type Schools (SJK), such as SJK(C) and SJK(T). All schools follow a common syllabus for non-language subjects, with mandatory instruction in Chinese or Tamil and in Malay and English. A **Dual Language Program (DLP)**, introduced in 2016, enables English instruction in STEM subjects to enhance global competitiveness. As of 2023, 2,420 schools (1,613 primary, 807 secondary) participate, though concerns persist over declining Bahasa Melayu proficiency and strict implementation rules. Primary education is compulsory from age 7 (Year 1–6), with promotion regardless of performance. The **UASA** (Year 4–6) replaced the UPSR, assessing students in Malay, English, Science, and Math within a school-specific context. Secondary education is primarily delivered through Malay-medium National Secondary Schools (SMK), where Science and Math are taught in Malay (from 2012). Students must pass UASA and PBS assessments and take the **SPM** (equivalent to GCSE O Levels), including an O Level English essay. Students from SJK(C) or SJK(T) must achieve a minimum **D grade in Malay** to enter Form 1, otherwise attending a transition class. Co-curricular activities are mandatory, with students required to participate in at least two (or three in Sarawak).  

Chinese Independent High Schools, funded by the Malaysian Chinese community through UCSCAM, offer Mandarin and the **UEC** (recognized internationally but not yet by Malaysian public universities), playing a key role in preserving ethnic identity and resisting government efforts to eliminate non-Malay language education. Private institutions offer international curricula such as A Levels, the International Baccalaureate (IB), and dual-degree "twinning" programmes with foreign universities. After the SPM, students may pursue either the **STPM** (equivalent to A Levels) or **matriculation**—a one- or two-year programme with a 90% Bumiputera quota.  

Tertiary education is largely government-subsidized, featuring 20 public universities categorized by specialization (research, TVET, Islamic education), alongside private universities with international recognition through global partnerships. A branch-campus model allows students to earn foreign qualifications locally at reduced costs. Polytechnics offer diplomas, advanced diplomas, bachelor’s degrees, and special skills certificates, with institutions like PUO, PSA, and PIS holding university status. Postgraduate programmes, including Master of Science and PhDs, are increasingly popular, especially in MBA and DBA fields.  

Key policy reforms have been introduced through the **2006–2010 National Education Blueprint**, which aimed to reduce racial polarization by introducing Mandarin and Tamil as elective subjects, improving access, and reducing class sizes. The **2013–2025 blueprint** promotes universal access, equity, and improved international performance (TIMSS/PISA), including extending compulsory schooling to 11 years, adopting the KSSM/KSSR curricula to emphasize critical thinking, making English compulsory for SPM, and improving teacher quality and inclusive infrastructure. Despite these efforts, challenges remain: language policy tensions between Malay as the national language and the preservation of Chinese and Tamil languages have led to protests and policy reversals; gender imbalance persists in higher education (2:1 male-to-female ratio in polytechnics); and although racial quotas in public universities were abolished in 2008, matriculation quotas still exist, raising concerns about underrepresentation of Indian students. A flexible uniform policy, effective from March 2024, reflects broader efforts to modernize school practices. Critics argue that government initiatives like "Vision Schools" reinforce racial polarization, as Chinese and Indian communities actively preserve their linguistic and cultural identities in schools.

## **Philippines: K–12 Expansion with Persistent Inequities**  
The Philippine education system evolved through distinct historical phases, beginning with pre-colonial oral traditions, followed by Spanish-era religious instruction and the 1863 Educational Decree mandating free and universal education. During the American period, a centralized system was established with U.S. teachers and the creation of high schools in 1910 to meet labor demands. Key reforms include the 1907 Gabaldon Law and the 1982 Education Act, which led to the 1994 tri-focalization of governance (DepEd, CHED, TESDA).  

The **K–12 curriculum**, implemented on **June 5, 2017**, expands basic education to 12 years—adding one year of Kindergarten, six years of elementary, four years of junior high (Grades 7–10), and two years of senior high (Grades 11–12). It emphasizes mother-tongue-based instruction in early grades and foundational skills in mathematics, Filipino, English, and social studies. The curriculum integrates Social Studies across grade levels and shifts from bilingual to multilingual instruction to improve learning outcomes and prepare students for tertiary education and employment. Senior high school offers four specialized tracks: **Academic** (ABM, HUMSS, STEM, GAS, Pre-Baccalaureate Maritime), **Technical-Vocational-Livelihood (TVL)**, **Sports**, and **Arts and Design**, aligned with student interests and aptitudes. Public senior high schools provide free education, with vouchers available for private institutions. TVL programs, administered by TESDA, lead to National Certificate Levels I and II after two years. Science high schools (e.g., PSHS, RSHS) offer advanced science and math instruction for gifted students. Students receive a permanent record and a secondary school certificate upon completion.  

The system includes diverse school types—public, private, international, and specialized institutions such as Chinese, Islamic, science, and laboratory schools. Islamic schools follow a standardized curriculum (SMC) including Arabic and Islamic studies, supported by government initiatives. The academic year runs from June to March, with breaks around holidays; a temporary shift during the pandemic in 2020 was reversed in 2021. School uniforms are common, though mandatory requirements were abolished in 2008.  

Higher education is overseen by the **Commission on Higher Education (CHED)**, with over 1,975 institutions—most private (88%)—offering a range of public and private programs. Foreign students, primarily from India and China, enroll in Philippine higher education. The **Alternative Learning System (ALS)** provides modular, non-formal education for out-of-school learners, enabling certification for further studies or employment. Indigenous peoples' education is promoted through culturally relevant curricula and learning materials.  

Despite structural advances, the system faces significant challenges: a **critical shortage of classrooms (148,000 as of 2025)**, inadequate funding, poor learning outcomes, and a high teacher-learner ratio. Even though public education is free, **affordability issues** such as transportation and learning materials contribute to dropout rates, especially among disadvantaged communities. Higher education suffers from an **employment mismatch**—oversupply in business and arts, shortages in technical and STEM fields—leading to brain drain. Teacher quality remains a concern due to low licensure exam passing rates and ineffective teaching methods. The pandemic exacerbated learning loss and mental health challenges. Bureaucratic inefficiencies, corruption, and insufficient investment hinder policy implementation. While reforms such as teacher salary increases, scholarships, and infrastructure improvements are proposed, **persistent funding gaps and political instability** remain major obstacles to systemic improvement.  

Additionally, in 2017, the **Universal Access to Quality Tertiary Education Act** mandated free public higher education. A revised **"Matatag curriculum"** was launched in 2023 to streamline learning areas and emphasize Filipino identity. However, disparities persist by region, gender, and socioeconomic status, with **low completion rates in upper secondary education (72.6%)** and **6% of school-aged children (1.6 million) out of school**. Functional literacy is high (93.1% basic, 70.8% functional), but foundational literacy and numeracy remain weak. Early childhood education participation is limited (30.5% of 3–4-year-olds).

### **Key Cross-Country Observations**  
- **Equity and Access**: All systems face persistent equity challenges—South Africa and Malaysia with racial and socioeconomic disparities, the Philippines with geographic and economic inequality, and the U.S. with deep structural inequities tied to property-based funding.  
- **Language Policy**: Malaysia’s multilingual system reflects ethnic tensions and identity preservation, while the Philippines emphasizes mother-tongue instruction to improve foundational learning.  
- **Teacher Shortages and Quality**: Teacher burnout, low salaries, and poor quality are common issues in the U.S., Malaysia, and the Philippines, requiring systemic reforms in pay, training, and support.  
- **Curriculum Reforms**: All three nations have implemented major overhauls—U.S. in testing and literacy, Malaysia in KSSM/KSSR and DLP, Philippines in K–12 and Matatag—to improve outcomes and global competitiveness.  
- **Higher Education Challenges**: Affordability, employment mismatch, and underrepresentation of certain groups remain pressing issues across all systems, though Malaysia and the Philippines have more formalized affirmative action policies.  

These comparative insights highlight the shared global challenges in education—equity, quality, and access—and underscore the need for context-sensitive, inclusive, and sustainable reforms.
