# Education in Malaysia
Malaysia's education evolved from Islamic *sekolah pondok* and madrasah in its early history. British colonialism introduced English-language prestigious schools and Malay elite training colleges but limited Malay access to secondary education. Post-independence reforms led to a national school system with Malay, English, Chinese, and Tamil mediums. Missionary schools originally English-medium assimilated into the Malay-medium national system but retain ownership of facilities. The Dual Language Program (DLP), launched in 2016, allows teaching science and math in English to enhance bilingualism and global competitiveness, though it faces challenges in language proficiency and resource allocation. Education spans preschool to tertiary levels, including national, Chinese independent, and international schools. Vernacular schools' constitutionality was upheld in 2021. Issues include racial quotas in university admissions, language policy debates, and the balancing of multicultural education.

# Education in Romania
Romania’s education system, influenced by French and Soviet models, has compulsory schooling extending to 14 years, including newly added early years of kindergarten and completion of high school. Preschool programs are available but unevenly distributed, with rural areas underserved. Elementary and gymnasium education cover grades 1–8 with comprehensive curricula in core subjects, foreign languages, and optional religious education. High schools offer theoretical, technical, vocational, and military programs, with admission determined by nationwide tests. The Baccalaureate exam is required for university admission. Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, including bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees, and accommodates international exchanges via EU programs. Despite high literacy overall, rural education suffers from attendance challenges and dropout rates especially after grade 8.

# Education in the Philippines
The Philippines’ education has pre-colonial oral traditions, Spanish-introduced formal schooling, and American-influenced systems. The US established English-medium schools and high schools; later reforms introduced K–12 (kindergarten plus 12 years of education) starting 2012, fully implemented by 2017, aligning with global standards. Education is compulsory and free at preschool, elementary, and high school levels with a curriculum emphasizing mother tongue-based multilingual education in lower grades. Secondary education divides into junior and senior high school with academic, technical-vocational, sports, and arts tracks. The Alternative Learning System (ALS) caters to out-of-school learners. Higher education, overseen by CHED, includes public and private institutions with diverse programs. Challenges include access disparities, underfunding, poor infrastructure, learning losses due to COVID-19, and educational mismatch with labor markets.

# Education in Australia
Australia's education system is state and territory regulated, with a national curriculum adopted since 2010. About 64% attend government schools; the rest attend Catholic or independent schools, most charging fees. Preschool (ages 3–5) focuses on early childhood education but attendance is not compulsory. Primary schooling typically covers Years K–6 (or 7), with secondary school spanning Years 7–12; compulsory attendance varies by state till age 15–17. Selective and specialist schools exist alongside comprehensive schools. Tertiary education includes 37 public universities, TAFE vocational institutions, and a large international student population. Australian students rank around the middle internationally in PISA outcomes. Indigenous education faces disparities. Religious instruction varies, with constitutional secularism upheld. School violence and funding distribution continue to be concerns.

# Education in the United States
The US developed compulsory public education by the late 19th century, with increasing high school and college enrollment over time. Education governance is decentralized among federal, state, and local authorities. Public education ranges from preschool to K–12, with compulsory attendance typically from ages 5/6 to 16–18. High school graduates proceed to diverse post-secondary institutions varying in selectivity and funding. Student loan debt and credential inflation are prominent issues. Standardized testing is widespread but controversial due to equity concerns. Racial and socioeconomic achievement gaps persist, linked to funding disparities and systemic inequalities. Teacher shortages, inadequate pay, and high turnover affect quality. School safety and violence are ongoing challenges. Higher education includes public and private universities, community colleges, and vocational education, with significant diversity in programs and costs.

# Education in Portugal
Portugal's education evolved from clergy-led beginnings with universities founded as early as 1290. Illiteracy was high until mid-20th century when literacy programs and education expansion improved rates. Basic education spans nine years divided into three cycles; secondary education offers general, technical, and artistic programs. Higher education comprises universities and polytechnic institutes, with recent reforms aligned to the Bologna Process. Admission to university is via national exams, with alternative paths for mature students. Private education exists but public systems dominate; rankings vary, with better outcomes in urban centers. School violence has increased, with initiatives like Escola Segura enhancing safety. The system faces criticism over dropout rates and functional illiteracy. Several international schools operate, mostly in Lisbon and Porto.

# Education in Finland
Finland's education system emphasizes universal literacy rooted in Lutheran traditions. It offers free, high-quality early childhood education focusing on play-based learning and social skills. Basic comprehensive education is nine years (grades 1–9), recently extended to age 18 to include upper secondary school (academic or vocational). Upper secondary education culminates in a nationally graded matriculation exam. Higher education divides into research universities and universities of applied sciences, with admissions based on transparent, merit-based criteria. Teaching is respected, requiring master's degrees. Tuition is free for EU students; non-EU students pay fees. Finland leads globally in education quality, with low-stakes testing and emphasis on student well-being. Adult education and international dissemination of Finnish pedagogical models are active.

# Education in South Africa
South Africa’s education is managed by the Department of Basic Education (Grades R–12) and Department of Higher Education and Training. Basic education includes Foundation Phase (Grades R–3), Intermediate Phase (4–6), Senior Phase (7–9), and Further Education and Training (10–12). Education is compulsory up to Grade 9. Funding varies with socio-economic quintiles, with 'no fee' schools supported by the state. School fees vary greatly; exemptions exist for the poorest families. Private schools also operate, with some receiving government support. Higher education is diverse but challenged by enrollment imbalances favoring humanities over STEM fields. Historically, apartheid-era policies segregated education by race, with lasting disparities. Post-apartheid efforts include curriculum reforms and expanding language access. ICT use has been growing but impeded by infrastructure deficits, which the pandemic highlighted. School violence, teacher capacity, and poor academic performance remain significant concerns.
