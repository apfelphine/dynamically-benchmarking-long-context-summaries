# **Final Summary**

Malaysia’s education system is multilingual and state-led, marked by enduring racial and linguistic disparities—particularly in university admissions and SPM outcomes. Policies such as the 2003–2009 mixed-medium initiative and the 2016 Dual Language Program aim to balance language use and global competitiveness, though structural inequities, including a 90% Bumiputera quota in public universities, remain unresolved.

The Philippines transitioned to a standardized K–12 system emphasizing mother-tongue instruction and vocational tracks, yet persistent access gaps, weak foundational literacy, and underfunded public schools—especially in rural and regional areas—undermine learning. Low teacher pay, overcrowding, poor policy implementation, and pandemic-induced learning loss exacerbate learning poverty. Reforms face significant challenges in execution and equity.

Australia maintains a structured, internationally benchmarked system with strong national curriculum standards and high school retention. Despite strong PISA performance in reading and science, fewer than 60% of students meet proficiency standards. Bilingual education supports Indigenous communities, and reforms like Gonski aim to improve equity. Indigenous students continue to face disparities, and school violence remains a concern. International education is a major export, with high enrollment of international students. Teacher shortages are partially offset by support systems, but low pay and burnout persist.

The United States has a long history of public education reform, from Horace Mann to *Brown v. Board of Education*, yet systemic inequities in racial, economic, and immigrant student outcomes persist. Immigrant students face grade placement and English literacy barriers, while standardized testing reflects bias. A teacher shortage—driven by low pay and high burnout (44% of teachers moonlight)—contributes to turnover. Parental involvement is weak, especially in early grades, and poor reading skills correlate with high incarceration rates (70% of inmates cannot read above 4th grade). The 2023 *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* decision has raised concerns about equity in college admissions, and rising student debt further strains the system.

Portugal’s education system includes nine years of *Basic Education* (Ensino Básico), divided into three cycles with progressive subject depth and foreign language exposure. Secondary education (Ensino Secundário), mandatory since 2012/2013, leads to a *Secondary School Diploma* required for higher education. National exams in Portuguese and math assess performance at each cycle. Public schools are free; private schools receive partial state funding. Despite a dramatic rise in literacy under the Estado Novo, access to secondary and higher education remained limited until the 1980s. Higher education participation is modest (11% in 2007), and PISA results show underperformance. The 2008–2011 financial crisis led to severe cuts in university funding, staff reductions, and tuition hikes, undermining equity. Secondary education branches into general, technological, and artistic tracks, with flexible pathways including apprenticeships and professional schools. Higher education includes research-oriented universities (e.g., University of Coimbra, University of Porto) and polytechnics focused on practical training. The Bologna Process standardizes degrees into licenciatura (Bachelor), mestrado (Master), and doctorates. Admission is competitive, with a *numerus clausus* and ENES exams. Teacher education requires a *mestre* degree. Private institutions face criticism for lax standards and "diploma mill" practices, while school violence has increased, prompting safety programs like *Escola Segura*. International schools in Lisbon, Porto, and Algarve have strong reputations. Madeira’s *Escola da APEL* offers IB and Portuguese curricula.

Finland provides free, universal early childhood education from age 8 months to 5 years, emphasizing play-based learning and social development. Compulsory nine-year comprehensive schools (peruskoulu) feature small classes, minimal homework, and a focus on creativity and well-being. Students learn two additional languages and take diverse subjects. Grading is verbal initially, with numerical scales from 4 to 10. Upper secondary education (ages 15–19) is now compulsory and offers academic or vocational tracks. Academic students take the national matriculation exam (Ylioppilastutkinto), while vocational students earn certificates. Teachers must hold master’s degrees. Tertiary education includes research and applied science universities, both with merit-based admissions. Free public education is available to EU/EEA students; non-EEA students face tuition fees. Professional degrees like the Licentiate of Medicine and Doctor of Medicine are offered. The system emphasizes equity, holistic development, and student well-being. Since 2016, phenomenon-based learning has been mandated in basic schools to foster critical thinking and problem-solving. Finland is expanding its influence globally through Suomi-koulut schools and private institutions abroad, and leads in AI education with a free online course for 1% of the population. Bilingualism (English and Finnish/Swedish) is emphasized, though Swedish proficiency remains low.

South Africa’s education system is structured under the Department of Basic Education and Higher Education and Training, with a 12-grade system divided into General Education and Training (GET) and Further Education and Training (FET) phases. Despite equity-focused policies, challenges include high learner-to-teacher ratios (average 30:1), uneven school funding, and disparities due to school fees. Funding is tied to neighborhood poverty, with richer areas receiving more support. Some schools in the poorest areas offer "No Fee" status. Private schools, often run by trusts or churches, may charge fees but can receive state grants. Higher education is accessible to over a million students, though graduate unemployment remains high due to overrepresentation in humanities and underrepresentation in STEM and skilled trades. Historically, education was racially segregated, with colonial policies promoting English and British values. Post-1994 reforms, including university mergers and the National Qualifications Framework, aimed to improve equity. ICT use in education has progressed slowly due to poor internet access, inadequate infrastructure, and teacher digital skills gaps—especially in rural areas. The pandemic accelerated digital transformation, exposing deep inequalities in device and internet access. Despite efforts like South Africa Connect and Connect-ED, poor academic performance, unqualified teachers, outdated curricula, and socioeconomic factors continue to undermine outcomes. **Critically, 40% of children report being victims of school crime, with over a fifth of childhood sexual assaults occurring in schools. Gang violence involving weapons such as guns is prevalent, especially in Cape Town. In response, the Education Department and the Centre for Justice and Crime Prevention launched the Hlayiseka program to reduce school violence.**
