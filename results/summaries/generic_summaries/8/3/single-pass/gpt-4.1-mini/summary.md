# Education in Romania
Romania mandates 15–16 years of compulsory education from kindergarten through 12th grade, with limited private sector involvement outside preschool and tertiary levels. The system, influenced historically by French and Soviet models, faced high illiteracy until mid-20th century literacy campaigns. Kindergarten is divided into small, middle, and big groups; middle and big groups became compulsory in 2023 and 2020, respectively. Elementary education covers preparatory to 8th grade, with mostly public schools, free education, and a grading system shifting from qualitative in early years to numeric scales later. High school lasts four years, with various types such as National Colleges and Theoretical High Schools offering diverse academic programs. Graduation requires passing the National Baccalaureate Exam, a multi-part, nationally standardized test. Foreign languages, mainly English and French, are widely taught. Rural education struggles with access and resources. Higher education follows the Bologna system with public and private institutions; admission varies, and international cooperation programs exist. Literacy is near universal except for higher rates among minorities. Religious education is opt-in since 2014. Funding is limited, with illegal parental contributions common.

# Education in Australia
Education is regulated by states and territories, with around 64% attending government schools and 36% non-government. Preschool is widespread but non-mandatory, aiming for quality early childhood education. Primary education generally spans up to Year 6; secondary school covers Year 7 to 12, with some states having middle schools. The Australian Curriculum sets nationwide learning standards. School funding is a mix of state, federal, and parental contributions, with ongoing reforms like the Gonski reports aiming to improve equity. Indigenous education faces challenges and targeted programs address disparities. Bilingual education exists chiefly for Indigenous and immigrant populations but has faced policy shifts. Religious instruction varies by state, with secular governance upheld constitutionally. School violence remains an issue. Tertiary education includes 42 universities, with strong international student presence supporting the sector. Australia ranks highly in education indexes but has experienced declining PISA scores.

# Education in Malaysia
Malaysia's education has roots in Islamic and colonial-era missionary schools. Post-independence policy established national and vernacular schools (Malay, Chinese, Tamil mediums) with unified curricula. Preschool is mainly private and non-compulsory; primary education starts at age seven and lasts six years, followed by five years of secondary education (not yet compulsory but planned). The Dual Language Program offers Science and Math in English to improve bilingualism. Chinese independent high schools and Islamic religious schools coexist with the public system. Tertiary education includes public universities and a growing private sector with international partnerships. Challenges include language controversies, gender imbalances in higher education, and historical racial quotas influencing access. Education blueprints seek to improve infrastructure, inclusivity, and performance.

# Education in Portugal
Portugal has a long educational history dating to the 13th century, with major literacy improvements from mid-20th century under Estado Novo reforms. Basic education is compulsory nine years divided into three cycles, followed by a three-year secondary education with general, professional, or artistic tracks. The Bologna Process restructured higher education into university and polytechnic sectors. Public education is free with some private elite schools existing. National exams determine access to higher education. Adult education and non-traditional studies are significant. School violence exists but is mitigated by programs like Escola Segura. International schools serve expatriates. Criticism includes high dropout rates, functional illiteracy, and diploma mills in private institutions.

# Education in the Philippines
The Philippine education system evolved from pre-colonial oral traditions to Spanish religious schooling, then American-led public education introducing English and compulsory schooling. The current K–12 system (implemented fully in 2017) includes mandatory kindergarten, six years of elementary, four years junior high, and two years senior high specializing in academic, technical-vocational, sports, or arts tracks. Public education is free but faces challenges such as classroom shortages, inadequate funding, teacher quality, and learning disparities especially in conflict-affected and rural areas. Higher education is governed by CHED, with many private and public institutions. The Alternative Learning System offers modular education for dropouts. Indigenous and special needs education frameworks exist. Teachers' remuneration and working conditions remain issues, with ongoing reforms. Enrollment is over 27 million, but dropout and out-of-school rates persist.

# Education in South Africa
South Africa’s education is divided into Basic Education (Grade R to 12) and Higher Education. It has historical roots in apartheid-era segregation affecting equity and quality, with post-1994 reforms striving for uniform governance. Compulsory education covers grades R to 9 and further education and training phases cover grades 10 to 12. School funding combines government grants and parent fees, with fee exemptions for poor families. State and private schools vary widely in resourcing and performance. ICT adoption prior to COVID-19 was limited by infrastructure and teacher readiness; government initiatives aim to improve access. Challenges include poor academic outcomes, high dropout, violence including in schools, and inequities related to race and poverty. Higher education has undergone mergers and reforms but unemployment for graduates remains an issue.

# Education in the United States
The U.S. education system, decentralized and primarily state-controlled, includes compulsory education from about ages 5 to 16-18. It encompasses Pre-K to 12th grade, subdivided into elementary, middle/junior high, and high school, followed by optional higher education. Public education is free, supported mostly by local and state taxes, with federal aid targeting disadvantaged groups. The country has diverse private and public higher education institutions, with competitive admission and a high level of degree attainment. Challenges include racial and socioeconomic achievement gaps, teacher shortages and pay disparities, debates over standardized testing and curriculum, and high student loan debt. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated inequalities and impacted learning. The U.S. ranks lower in international assessments compared to other developed nations despite high spending.

# Education in Finland
Finland’s education system emphasizes equality, high teacher qualifications (master’s degree), free education, and comprehensive schooling for 9 years (grades 1–9) followed by either academic or vocational upper secondary education, which became compulsory by 2021. Early childhood education promotes play-based learning and social development. The system avoids streaming or selection in basic education, ensuring small class sizes and autonomy in teaching methods. Upper secondary education includes nationally graded matriculation exams required for university admission. Higher education divides into traditional universities focused on research and universities of applied sciences offering practical training. Since 2017, tuition fees apply to non-EEA students. Finland promotes lifelong learning through adult education programs and exports educational expertise internationally. Recent reforms include phenomenon-based learning supplementing traditional subjects to enhance 21st century skills.
