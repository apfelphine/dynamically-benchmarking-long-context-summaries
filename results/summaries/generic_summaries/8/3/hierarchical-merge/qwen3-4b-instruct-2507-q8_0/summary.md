# **Comparative Overview of Education Systems in Romania, Australia, Malaysia, Portugal, the Philippines, South Africa, the United States, and Finland**

## **Romania**
Romania’s 15-year compulsory education system (kindergarten to 12th grade) underwent major reforms in 2012 and 2020, introducing a preparatory year, expanded kindergarten access, and two additional years of high school. Public schools dominate elementary education with nearly 100% enrollment and free instruction, though parents may pay for materials. Elementary education includes mandatory subjects such as mathematics, Romanian, foreign languages, science, and civic education, with optional subjects selected under the *Curriculum la Decizia Școlii* framework. Assessment is continuous, with yearly averages; students scoring below 5 in two or more subjects may take a special exam, and failing three or more must repeat the year. A single teacher typically teaches most subjects, with specialized teachers for arts, languages, and physical education.

Admission to high school is based on the *Evaluarea Națională* (National Test) in Romanian and Mathematics. Five types of high schools offer university access—National Colleges, Theoretical (science/humanities), Military, Economic/Technical, and Liceu Tehnologic—each with distinct tracks. Students must pass the *Bacalaureate* exam with an average of at least 6.00 and a minimum of 5.00 in six to eight subjects. Urban schools offer better facilities and extracurriculars, while rural schools face teacher shortages, long travel distances, and high dropout rates (16–25% in early grades). Religious education is optional. Higher education includes 97 institutions (56 public, 41 private), with the University of Iași, Bucharest, and Cluj leading the field. Admission is based on grades, exams, or olympiad performance. Literacy is high (0.83%), with higher rates among Romani and Turkish minorities.

## **Australia**
Australia’s education system spans preschool, primary (pre-Year 1 to Year 6), and secondary (Year 7–12), with government schools (64%) being free and Catholic schools (19.7%) serving 750,000 students. Private schools (12% of schools, 16% of students) charge fees from under $100 to over $500,000 and offer diverse pedagogies, including Montessori and disability-focused programs. Compulsory schooling is from age 5–6 to 15–17, depending on the state. The Australian Curriculum ensures uniform standards, while national assessments like NAPLAN evaluate literacy and numeracy. Selective and specialist schools (e.g., James Ruse, Melbourne High) use academic or aptitude criteria. Senior secondary qualifications vary by state (e.g., HSC, VCE), with vocational pathways available through VET.

Australia ranked ninth in reading and science and tenth in mathematics in PISA 2023, though less than 60% of students met proficiency standards. Indigenous students face persistent disparities in outcomes, especially in Year 12 attainment and literacy. School violence is a concern, with 48% of principals reporting physical misconduct. Tertiary education is robust, with 1.18 million students enrolled in 42 institutions (37 public, 3 private), led by Monash University. The Group of Eight dominates research. Australia hosts the highest proportion of international students globally (26.7% in 2019, expected to reach 940,000 by 2025), primarily from China and India, making education a key economic asset.

## **Malaysia**
Malaysia’s multilingual system includes national schools, Islamic religious schools (e.g., SRA, SAR), Chinese independent schools (funded by the Chinese community), and international schools. Islamic schools teach religious subjects and award the STAM qualification; SAR schools remain influential in rural areas. Chinese schools use Mandarin and follow the UEC exam (internationally recognized but not accepted by public universities), emphasizing cultural preservation. The 2006–2010 and 2013–2025 National Education Blueprints aimed to improve access, reduce class sizes, and promote cultural awareness, including Mandarin and Tamil. Key reforms include extending compulsory education to 11 years, introducing the KSSM and KSSR curricula emphasizing critical thinking, and raising teacher standards.

Persistent challenges include a strong preference for Malay-medium instruction, a 2009 reversal of English science/math policy (replaced by a Dual Language Program), gender imbalance (2:1 male-to-female ratio in higher education), and a 90% Bumiputera quota in public universities—only 10% open to non-Bumiputera students. This policy limits access for Chinese and Indian students, with priority given to the B40 income group.

## **Portugal**
Portugal’s education system includes nine years of Basic Education (Ensino Básico), divided into three cycles (ages 6–13), with core subjects in Portuguese, Mathematics, and Science. National exams are administered at the end of each cycle. Since 2012/2013, secondary education (Ensino Secundário) has been mandatory and lasts three years, offering general, professional, or artistic tracks. The *Diploma de Ensino Secundário* is required for higher education. Pre-primary education (ages 3–5) is free and state-run. The school year runs from mid-September to mid-June.

Historically rooted in the Catholic Church, Portugal faced high illiteracy (over 80% in the 19th century), which declined to 40.4% by 1950 and was fully eliminated by the late 1950s. Public education expanded to ages 6–12 in the 1960s. However, higher education remains underdeveloped—ranked among the lowest in the EU—and PISA results reflect long-standing underperformance. Financial pressures from the 2008 crisis led to university budget cuts, rising tuition, and staff reductions. Since 2005, strict access thresholds (minimum 95/200 in ENES exams) have reduced public university admissions significantly.

Admission to public institutions is highly competitive, using a *numerus clausus* based on exam scores and secondary performance. Private institutions admit students at discretion. The Bologna Process (2006) standardizes education into a three-cycle structure (Bachelor’s, Master’s, Doctorate), though older degree names persist, causing confusion. The University of Coimbra is the oldest, and the University of Porto the largest. Challenges include high dropout rates, functional illiteracy (48% of adults), and general illiteracy (7.5%). Private institutions face criticism for low quality and poor regulation. Teacher qualifications are through *Licenciado* and Master’s degrees. The *Novas Oportunidades* program allows adults to earn grade equivalences based on life experience but lacks academic rigor. School violence is a growing concern.

## **Philippines**
The Philippine education system evolved from colonial frameworks to a modern, standardized structure. The 1987 Constitution enshrined compulsory elementary and free public secondary education. The 2008 **K–12 program** extended basic education to 12 years, including Kindergarten–Grade 3, Grades 4–6, Grades 7–10 (junior high), and Grades 11–12 (senior high). The latter offers specialized tracks: Academic (ABM, HUMSS, STEM, GAS), Technical-Vocational-Livelihood (TVL), and Science High Schools. The **Matatag curriculum** (2023) streamlined learning areas and emphasized Filipino identity and national values. Enrollment rose from 24 million (2013–2014) to 28 million (2022–2023), though 6% of school-aged children remain out of school.

Pre-elementary uses mother tongue-based instruction (MTB-MLE). Elementary education emphasizes core subjects like Mathematics, Filipino, and Science, transitioning to English. Secondary education includes TVL programs, which lead to National Certificate Levels I and II via TESDA assessments. The school year runs from August to March, with five-day weeks and breaks in October, December, and holidays. School uniforms are common, though abolished in public schools in 2008.

Despite structural strengths, challenges persist: a teacher-learner ratio of 1:25 to 1:29, over 1,975 institutions (88% private), and inadequate infrastructure (148,000 missing classrooms as of 2025). Poor policy implementation, a mismatch between education and employment, underemployment, and brain drain remain major issues. The pandemic exacerbated learning loss and mental health concerns. Weak outcomes in math, science, and reading, low teacher quality (evidenced by poor LET passing rates), and insufficient funding continue to undermine performance.

## **South Africa**
South Africa transitioned from a racially segregated apartheid system to a more inclusive framework since 1994, with the *South African Schools Act (1996)* unifying schools and reducing university numbers from 36 to 22. Despite reforms, deep structural disparities persist—especially in rural and low-income areas—where students face shortages of qualified teachers, limited resources, poor pass rates, and overcrowded classrooms. ICT integration remains slow; only about 22% of schools had reliable internet access by 2017. Programs like *South Africa Connect* and *Connect-ED* aim to close the digital divide, but the **COVID-19 pandemic** exposed severe access gaps. School violence is widespread, with 40% of children reporting being victims of crime, including sexual assault. The *Hlayiseka* program addresses safety, but systemic inequalities in access, quality, and graduate outcomes remain, compounded by high unemployment and over-enrollment in humanities, with underrepresentation in STEM, health, and business.

## **United States**
The U.S. operates a decentralized K–12 system serving 85% of students, funded by local and state sources, resulting in significant regional and socioeconomic inequities. Despite high per-student spending, education expenditure as a share of GDP remains low, and racial and economic achievement gaps persist. Black and Hispanic students consistently underperform compared to Asian and White students due to systemic underfunding, racial segregation, and institutional racism. Standardized testing—often biased and inaccessible to disadvantaged students—further disadvantages marginalized groups. Challenges include inadequate support for English-language learners, inconsistent instruction, and reliance on unscientific teaching methods (e.g., "three-cue" reading). Teacher shortages are worsening due to low salaries, burnout, and poor job satisfaction.

Higher education includes over 7,000 institutions, with public universities averaging $27,967 and private institutions $40,476 annually. Total student loan debt reached $1.5 trillion by 2018. Concerns about credential inflation and declining returns on investment—especially for non-STEM degrees—are growing.

## **Finland**
Finland stands as a global model of equitable and student-centered education. Compulsory education begins at age 7 and extends through age 18, with tuition-free, municipally run schools that do not use selective admissions or resource disparities. Teachers hold master’s degrees and are selected through competitive processes, granting them significant autonomy. Class sizes are small, and the curriculum emphasizes creativity, well-being, outdoor activities, and reading, with minimal homework and no high-stakes testing. Students learn two languages and take a broad range of subjects, with grades assessed incrementally and failing grades requiring improvement rather than repetition.

Upper secondary education offers academic or vocational tracks, and university entrance is based on the *Ylioppilastutkinto* exam with transparent, merit-based criteria—no essays or extracurriculars are considered. Tertiary education includes research universities and applied science institutions, with free access for EEA students and tuition fees for non-EEA students. Finland promotes lifelong learning through adult education, open university programs, and phenomenon-based learning. It leads in AI education, offering free online courses, and maintains high enrollment rates—60–70% of students pursue higher education annually. The country exports its model globally, demonstrating how equity, quality, and student well-being can be achieved through structural reform.
