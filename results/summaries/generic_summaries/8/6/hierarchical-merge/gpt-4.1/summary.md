## Global Education Systems: Key Features and Challenges

Education systems worldwide reveal diverse structures, reform efforts, and persistent challenges. South Africa continues to grapple with resource disparities and equity issues—especially in rural and marginalized communities—despite post-apartheid reforms and initiatives to expand ICT infrastructure. Finland’s lauded commitment to equality, highly qualified teachers, and broad curricula underpins its free higher education model, yet restrictive pathways and debates on curriculum and job readiness persist. Portugal’s post-revolutionary and Bologna reforms have driven gains in access and performance; however, dropout rates, functional illiteracy, and concerns over tertiary costs and private sector growth remain problematic.

The United States faces profound inequities rooted in funding models reliant on local property taxes, leading to achievement gaps and fluctuating standards shaped by political influences. Ongoing issues include teacher shortages, mental health concerns, underperformance on standardized tests, and soaring student debt. Australia’s internationally recognized system deals with declining student achievement, equity gaps—particularly for Indigenous learners—controversies around religious instruction, and recurring debates over resource allocation despite reforms like the Gonski Reports.

## Education in the Philippines, Romania, and Malaysia

The Philippine education system, shaped by colonial influences and recent reforms, employs a K–12 model offering multiple specialized tracks and alternative pathways. While universal access initiatives and curriculum updates have broadened reach, significant disparities persist across regions, economic groups, and genders, aggravated by infrastructure deficits, learning poverty, and teacher shortages. Diverse institutions—including indigenous and special needs schools—cater to varied learner populations, but chronic underfunding and misalignment between education and employment continue to challenge progress.

Romania’s education mandates 15 years of schooling, with notable expansion of compulsory education and rigorous exams structuring academic advancement. Although its Bologna-aligned system encourages international exchange, rural areas and ethnic minorities face steep obstacles including resource shortages, high dropout rates, and limited accessibility. Persistent underfunding, brain drain, and debates over religious curricula undermine systemic quality and equity, even as student activism and extracurricular engagement remain vibrant.

Malaysia’s multi-track system—rooted in colonial and religious foundations—includes national, vernacular, religious, and technical schools with reforms targeting unity, minority inclusion, and bilingualism. Ethnic divisions and race-based university quotas create significant inequalities, affecting non-majority populations and rural communities. While primary schooling is compulsory and secondary may soon follow, higher education faces gender imbalance, limited efforts to curb brain drain, and ongoing resource challenges. Expanding bilingualism and integration are central to current government policy drives.
