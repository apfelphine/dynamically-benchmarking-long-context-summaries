## South Africa

South Africa's education system comprises No Fee, fee-paying, and private schools across primary and secondary levels, culminating in matriculation for university entry. Challenges include resource disparities, achievement gaps, dropouts, graduate unemployment, STEM participation, teacher relations, and school safety. Interventions address curriculum reform, technology integration, rural access, and pandemic response.

## Finland

Finland offers universal, play-based education from ages 7 to 18, focusing on lifelong learning, teacher autonomy, and flexibility between academic and vocational pathways. Higher education is tuition-free for EEA citizens and merit-based. Current priorities include graduate employment, digitalization, interdisciplinary learning, and language policy reform.

## Portugal

Portugal mandates nine years of schooling followed by academic or vocational tracks determined by national exams, with higher mobility via the Bologna Process. Reforms aim to lower dropouts, reduce illiteracy, expand university access, improve PISA scores, boost teacher advocacy, and enhance inclusion and safety, supported by initiatives like Novas Oportunidades.

## United States

The US system features optional preschool, mandatory K–12, and diverse tertiary, vocational, and homeschooling options. Key issues include funding inequalities, achievement gaps, discipline and safety, testing debates, sex and LGBT education, immigrant language support, teacher retention, and rising student debt. US universities maintain strong global reputations.

## Australia

Australia’s education spans preschool to tertiary, governed by the Australian Qualifications Framework, with compulsory schooling from ages 5/6 to 15/17. Support exists for Indigenous and immigrant languages, but gaps persist in NAPLAN data for Indigenous students. Religious education is optional, safety concerns continue, and universities are internationally respected.

## Philippines

The Philippines' K–12 system provides free public education and specialized tracks, overseen by the Commission on Higher Education and Alternative Learning System. Persistent issues include infrastructure, affordability, policy enforcement, corruption, learning poverty, and teacher quality. Reforms focus on curriculum modernization and broadening access.

## Romania

Romania mandates 15 years of education with pathways selected via national exams, including compulsory high school and a required Baccalaureate. English and French are commonly taught; urban schools are better resourced than rural. Higher education follows the Bologna Process. Challenges include chronic underfunding, illegal fees, religious controversies, and brain drain.

## Malaysia

Malaysia’s multi-stream system includes compulsory primary education in Malay, Chinese, Tamil, and Islamic religious schools, alongside international options. Malay-medium schools receive favored funding, sparking equity debates, while bilingual instruction and integration are promoted through Vision Schools and the Dual Language Program. Chinese independent high schools teach in Mandarin and offer the UEC, though it is not accepted for public university entry; UCSCAM advocates for Chinese-language education. Islamic schools focus on Islamic studies. Uniforms are nearly universal, with recent attire updates.

Secondary education offers mainstream, SMJK, religious, technical, and elite tracks, progressing via UASA and exams such as SPM and O Level English. Post-SPM options include Form 6 (STPM) or government matriculation, with race-based quotas generally favoring Bumiputeras—though some have been abolished. University admissions rely on STPM, matriculation, and contentious selection practices under the Malaysian Qualifications Framework. Government-subsidized tertiary education is offered at 20 public universities specializing in various fields, with private and international campuses expanding choices. Polytechnics and twinning programs provide technical/vocational courses. Postgraduate degrees are common, and meritocracy reforms since 2002 allow non-postgraduates to lecture, though "brain gain" policies have had limited impact.

Malaysia’s National Education Blueprints (2006–2010, 2013–2025) target racial unity, improved quality, and closing achievement gaps, with the latest blueprint emphasizing universal access, better student outcomes, and enhanced teacher quality via curriculum reforms, compulsory English for SPM, expanded language options, infrastructure improvements, and annual reporting. Persistent challenges include shifting language policies, gender disparities at tertiary levels, and historical affirmative action favoring Bumiputeras. Co-curricular participation is mandatory and uniforms strictly regulated.

University admissions maintain a 90% quota for Bumiputeras, with increased seats and prioritization for lower-income groups. The matriculation program reserves the remaining 10% for non-Bumiputeras—specifically Chinese (5.43%), Indian (3.72%), and other minorities (0.85%).
