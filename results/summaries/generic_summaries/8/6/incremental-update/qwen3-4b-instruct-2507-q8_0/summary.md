Malaysia’s system is multilingual and multi-denominational, featuring national schools, Islamic (madrasah) and Chinese independent high schools (SJKC/SJK(T)), and international schools. Islamic schools offer the STAM qualification and remain active, especially in rural areas. Chinese independent schools use Mandarin, offer unique academic streams, and administer the internationally recognized UEC exam—though public universities have not yet fully accepted it. The government’s push to eliminate non-Malay language schools has been challenged by UCSCAM, which argues that such policies threaten ethnic cultural identity and advocate for multilingual education. The Dual Language Program (DLP) promotes English proficiency in STEM and core subjects but has struggled to ensure mastery of Bahasa Melayu and mother tongues. School uniforms are now flexible, reflecting 2024 reforms. The Ministry of Education oversees policy, with the 2006–2010 National Education Blueprint targeting access, class size reduction, and infrastructure improvement.  

Malaysia’s education policy, guided by the National Education Blueprints (2006–2010 and 2013–2025), aims to improve access, quality, equity, unity, and efficiency. Key initiatives include expanding compulsory schooling to 11 years, introducing the KSSM (Kurikulum Standard Sekolah Menengah) and KSSR (Kurikulum Standard Sekolah Rendah) curricula to emphasize critical thinking and higher-order thinking skills, and ensuring inclusive access for minority and disabled students. Language policy remains contentious, with ongoing efforts to balance Malay as the national language while preserving Chinese and Tamil instruction through elective courses and the Dual Language Program. Persistent issues include gender imbalance in higher education and the lingering effects of historical racial quotas in universities, which have been gradually phased out but still influence student representation.
