### Education Systems: Philippines, Romania, and Malaysia (Comprehensive Summary)

**Philippine Education: Structure, Administration, and Challenges**
The Philippine system operates under a dual framework managed by the **Department of Education (DepEd)** for basic education (K–12) and the **Commission on Higher Education (CHED)** for tertiary institutions. Shaped by colonial history and the 1987 Constitution, the system evolved through the 1994 tri-focalization, the 2008 K–12 curriculum, and 2023 "Matatag" updates emphasizing Filipino identity. The academic year runs from August to June (shifted to October–June in 2020) with a five-day schedule. The K–12 system comprises pre-elementary, elementary, junior high, and senior high (SHS), where public SHS is free and offers specialized tracks (Academic, TVL, Sports/Arts) alongside institutions like PHSA and Science High Schools. As of 2020, there were 1,975 institutions (12% public, 88% private), with foreign students entering via specific exams. The workforce includes ~876,842 public teachers with a student-teacher ratio of 1:25 to 1:29 and starting salaries of ~₱20,754 monthly. However, the system faces significant challenges, including a ~148,000 classroom deficit, high LET failure rates, legislative responses to improve teacher quality, and equity issues such as affordability barriers, an "education-employment mismatch," and pandemic-induced learning loss (61% by age 18).

**Romanian Education System: Comprehensive Overview**
Romania features a 15-year compulsory framework (kindergarten through 12th grade), historically influenced by French and Soviet models with illiteracy eradicated in the 1940s–50s. **Early Education** includes free public kindergartens with scarce places requiring six months' advance registration, followed by a mandatory preparatory school year. **Elementary School** (grades 1–8) uses a single teacher per subject in primary grades and specialized teams in gymnasium, with weekly hours ranging from 22–30. Grading shifts from qualitative (grades 1–4) to numerical (1–10, min 5) in grades 5–8, with specific remediation rules. **High Schools** (four years compulsory since 2020) require passing the **National Test** (Romanian and Math) for admission, with enrollment determined by a computerized ranking system. Tracks include National College (prestigious), Theoretical (Science/Humanities), Military, Economic/Technical, and Vocational options. The **National Baccalaureate (Bac)** is a centralized, competitive exam (2–3 oral, 4–5 written tests) requiring an average of at least 6.00; since 2023, grading is digitalized. Significant disparities exist between urban and rural schools, with rural children facing high dropout rates (16% aged 7–10, 25% aged 11–14) due to lack of high schools and commuting difficulties. **Higher Education** follows the Bologna process (Bachelor's, Master's, PhD) with 56 public and 41 private universities (as of 2016), offering autonomy and participation in EU programs like Erasmus Mundus. Challenges include underfunding, illegal parental contributions, and a brain drain of approximately 50,000 youth studying abroad.

**Malaysia Education System: History, Governance, and Policy Evolution**
Malaysia's education system evolved from early Islamic schools to a structure shaped by British colonialism and post-independence reforms. The **1956 Razak Report** established a framework with Malay, English, Chinese, and Tamil-medium schools. Post-independence, vernacular schools were nationalized but retained language rights, gradually shifting to Malay in the 1970s. Governance involves the Ministry of Education (split from Higher Education in 2004/2015) and the Ministry of Higher Education (2020). **Vernacular Schools** (Chinese and Tamil) were upheld by a 2021 High Court ruling, while missionary schools were converted to grant-in-aid status, with some re-established as private institutions in 2017. The system includes diverse types: National Schools (Malay-medium, government-funded), National-type Schools (vernacular, community-owned), Islamic schools, and international schools.

**Preschool and Primary Education**
Preschool (ages 3–5) is non-compulsory, mostly private, and lacks a fixed curriculum. **Primary Education** is 6 years (compulsive since 2003) and divided into National Schools and National-type Schools. Assessments shifted from PTS (2001) to UPSR (cancelled 2021) and then UASA (2022), testing Malay, English, Science, and Maths. Language policies have fluctuated: English-medium Science/Math (2003) were reverted to Malay (2009) due to backlash, with the Dual Language Program (DLP) launched in 2016 as a compromise to teach Science/Math in English. The DLP currently covers 2,420 schools but faces controversies over language proficiency and resource allocation.

**Secondary and Tertiary Education**
Secondary education is 5 years (Forms 1–5), planned to be compulsory from 2024, with Malay as the main medium of instruction. Admission to Form 1 requires a minimum D grade in Malay or a transition class for vernacular graduates. **Tertiary Education** is heavily subsidized and organized under the Malaysian Qualifications Framework (MQF). Admission to public universities follows a race-based quota system reserving 90% for Bumiputeras. The **Polytechnic system** has expanded significantly since 1969, offering diplomas and degrees, while a **Brain Gain Scheme** (1995) attempts to attract foreign talent.

**Malaysia Education Policies and Key Issues**
*   **National Blueprints:** The **2006–2010** blueprint aimed to reduce racial polarization but faced criticism for quality. The **2013–2025** strategy aims for universal access, top-tier international performance, and unity, expanding compulsory schooling to 11 years and standardizing curricula (KSSM/KSSR).
*   **Admission Quotas:** In 2019, the Minister confirmed the retention of the 90% Bumiputera quota while introducing seats for the B40 income group. By 2020, non-Bumiputera seats were divided by ethnicity: 5.43% Chinese, 3.72% Indian, and 0.85% others. This affirmative action continues to cause dissatisfaction among non-Bumiputera communities.
*   **Language:** A persistent political issue; English-medium schools were phased out for Malay by 1970. Policies to teach Science/Math in English were reversed in 2009. Chinese and Tamil are now taught as electives in national schools.
*   **Demographics:** A 2:1 boy-to-girl ratio exists in polytechnics and higher learning, narrowing after graduation.
*   **Criticism:** The racial division has led to proposed "Vision Schools" (integrated compounds), which faced community objections over language restrictions.
