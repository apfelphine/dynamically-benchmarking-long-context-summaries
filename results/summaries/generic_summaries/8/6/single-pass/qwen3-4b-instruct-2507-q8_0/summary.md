**Education in South Africa**  
South Africa’s education system is divided into Basic and Higher Education. The Department of Basic Education (DBE) oversees primary and secondary education, with policy set by Minister Siviwe Gwarube and Deputy Minister Reginah Mhaule. The DBE funds teacher salaries in government schools, while independent schools are privately funded. Schools are governed by School Governing Bodies (SGBs), which manage fundraising and include parents, teachers, and support staff. The DBE’s key legislation includes the South African Schools Act (1996), which promotes access and democratic governance, the National Education Policy Act (1996), and the Employment of Educators Act (1998), which sets teacher standards.  

Basic education spans Grades 1–12, grouped into General Education and Training (GET) and Further Education and Training (FET). The GET phase includes Grades R–9, and FET covers Grades 10–12. Class sizes average 30:1, with variations by province. School fees are not publicly disclosed, and while some schools charge high fees, exemptions exist for low-income families. The government grants are tied to poverty levels, and "No Fee" schools are available for children in the GED band.  

Higher education is managed by the Department of Higher Education and Training, with policy led by Minister Nobuhle Nkabane and Deputy Minister Buti Manamela. A "Matriculation Endorsement" is required for university entry. The system includes 1 million students, with challenges in graduate employment due to a shortage of STEM and vocational graduates.  

Historically, education was racially segregated under apartheid, with policies like Bantu Education (1953) and the Afrikaans medium decree (1974) limiting access and language. The 1994 democratic transition led to a restructured system with nine provincial departments and a national system. The Curriculum 2005 (OBE) was introduced but later abandoned due to poor outcomes.  

**Educational Technology**  
Pre-pandemic, ICT adoption in South Africa was slow due to infrastructure gaps, teacher inexperience, and unequal access. South Africa Connect (2013) aims to provide broadband to all schools by 2030. During the pandemic, digital learning was implemented, but disparities in internet access and device availability exacerbated inequalities.  

**Performance and Violence**  
South Africa faces poor academic performance, with some schools achieving 0% pass rates in national exams. Contributing factors include weak teacher quality, uncommitted staff, and outdated curricula. School violence is prevalent, with 40% of children reporting victimization, and the Hlayiseka program targeting violence.  

**Key Issues**  
- Inequitable access due to school fees and infrastructure.  
- Teacher shortages and low pay.  
- Outdated curricula and poor student outcomes.  
- Persistent school violence and safety concerns.  

South Africa's education system remains challenged by historical legacies, socioeconomic disparities, and infrastructure gaps, despite ongoing reforms.
