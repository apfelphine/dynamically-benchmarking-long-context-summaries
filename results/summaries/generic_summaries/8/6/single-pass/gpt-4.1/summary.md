# Comparative Summary of National Education Systems

## South Africa

South Africa’s education system is overseen by two main departments: Basic Education and Higher Education and Training. Primary and secondary schooling runs from grades R (reception) to 12; most schools are either primary (grades R–7) or secondary (grades 8–12). Public schools receive government funding supplemented with school fees, which can exclude poorer students despite “no fee” incentives for low-income families. The system faces significant disparities tied to historical apartheid legacies, funding quintiles, and teacher quality, with performance, dropout rates, and violence remaining issues. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of educational technology but exposed infrastructural inequities. Higher education is robust, with autonomous universities, but faces graduate unemployment due to subject choice imbalances. Policies and reforms since 1994 aim to address historic inequities, though challenges persist in funding, teacher quality, and ICT integration.

## Finland

Finland’s education system emphasizes equity and quality, with universal pre-primary care and a nine-year comprehensive school (grades 1–9) now extended to 18. Secondary education bifurcates into academic and vocational tracks, both tuition-free and supported by state and municipal funding. Teachers are highly qualified (master’s degree required), and curricula focus on holistic development, minimal homework, small class sizes, and multilingualism. Grading is relaxed, and dropout rates are low. Higher education encompasses traditional universities and universities of applied sciences, with free tuition for EU/EEA students. Education reforms encourage phenomenon-based learning and export Finnish educational expertise abroad. The system is undergoing restructuring, with ongoing debates about vocational-academic balance and funding amid demographic changes.

## Portugal

Portugal’s education system evolved from high illiteracy and limited access to broader participation, especially following the Estado Novo regime. Basic education consists of nine compulsory years divided into three cycles, followed by three years of secondary education (compulsory since 2012). The system includes general, professional, and artistic programs, and secondary school rankings are published annually. Higher education is split between university and polytechnic systems, with both public and private institutions. Admission to public universities is competitive and influenced by national exams. The Bologna Process reformed degree structures, but higher education rates remain low compared to peers. Major challenges include high dropout rates, persistent functional illiteracy, and criticism of private sector quality. School violence and disparities between public and private institutions also exist.

## United States

The U.S. education system is decentralized, regulated at federal, state, and local levels. Education is compulsory through age 16–18, with most students attending 12 years of primary/secondary school, followed by optional higher education. The country is notable for its large private and parochial school sector. Funding is a mix of local, state, and federal sources, with property-tax-based funding leading to inequalities. Performance varies, with high literacy but lower achievement in math and science compared to other developed nations. Issues include achievement gaps along racial and socioeconomic lines, rising college costs and student debt, teacher shortages, and ongoing debates about standardized testing, curriculum, and affirmative action. Higher education is diverse and internationally regarded, but faces credential inflation and financial pressures.

## Australia

Australian education is primarily regulated at the state and territory level, with significant federal funding for private and public universities. Primary and secondary education is compulsory between ages 5–6 and 15–17, with government, Catholic, and independent schools forming the core. The Australian Curriculum shapes content nationwide. Tertiary education includes universities and vocational training (TAFE), with university entry generally requiring a Senior Secondary Certificate. School funding disparity, Indigenous educational gaps, and violence are key issues. Australia is a major international education provider, with high ratios of international students and strong rankings for higher education, though PISA assessments indicate a decline in student performance.

## Philippines

The Philippines follows a K–12 system: one year kindergarten, six years elementary, four years junior high, and two years senior high school. Education is compulsory and free through secondary, and public school enrollment is high. Challenges include classroom shortages, learning poverty (students not meeting reading and math standards), low funding, and education-employment mismatches leading to brain drain. Higher education features both public and private institutions, with issues of affordability and underinvestment. Multiple alternative learning systems and indigenous education provisions exist to address access gaps. Teacher pay and quality concerns, poor learning outcomes, and infrastructural deficiencies remain central issues.

## Romania

Romania mandates 15 years of compulsory education, starting from preschool, with a predominance of public institutions. Kindergartens and elementary schools are increasingly compulsory; higher education is provided by universities and other institutions following the Bologna model. The country achieved near-universal literacy, but rural areas still lag in access and attendance. The education system uses grading scales and national exams for progression. Higher education is research-oriented and internationally connected, with growing numbers studying abroad contributing to brain drain. Underfunding, disparities between urban and rural education, and debates over religious education persist.

## Malaysia

Education in Malaysia covers preschool, primary (six years), secondary (five years), and post-secondary (two years pre-university). The system distinguishes between national (Malay-medium) and national-type (Chinese/Tamil-medium) schools; language of instruction is a core issue. The Dual Language Program (DLP) enables English-medium STEM teaching. Post-secondary education includes matriculation, form six, and university entrance, with both public (quota-based) and private higher education institutions. Funding disparities exist, as do challenges around racial quotas, gender imbalance, and implementation of affirmative action policies. International and independent schools, as well as Islamic and Chinese language schools, offer alternative pathways. School uniforms are mandatory but with recent relaxations.
