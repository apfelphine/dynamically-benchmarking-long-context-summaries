# Education in South Africa
South Africa’s education is governed by the Department of Basic Education and Department of Higher Education and Training, each led by appointed ministers and director-generals. Basic education spans from Grade R (age 5-6) to Grade 12 (age 17-18), culminating in a matriculation certificate for tertiary entry. The system is divided into General Education and Training (grades R–9) and Further Education and Training (grades 10–12). Schools receive government funding based on area poverty levels but often supplement income through fees; poorer students may be exempt or receive fees reduction. Private schools, some receiving state grants, operate independently. Higher education is autonomous, with universities offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, but faces challenges like graduate underemployment and historical segregation. ICT integration in education has progressed slowly due to infrastructure and resource gaps, with COVID-19 accelerating digital adoption but exposing inequalities. South African students face issues such as poor academic performance, violence, and high dropout rates.

# Education in Finland
Finland’s education system emphasizes equity and lifelong learning, rooted in a history of high literacy linked to Lutheranism. Early childhood education (up to age 7) focuses on play and social skills development, with almost universal attendance. Basic education consists of a compulsory nine-year comprehensive school (grades 1–9), expanding to upper secondary school or vocational education (ages 15–18), both free and tuition-free, administered by municipalities. Teachers require master’s degrees, and student-teacher ratios are low. Upper secondary education offers academic or vocational tracks, leading to matriculation exams critical for university admission. Higher education comprises traditional research universities and universities of applied sciences, with transparent, merit-based admissions. Finland actively promotes language learning and adult education. Recent curriculum reforms introduce phenomenon-based learning, and Finnish education is exported via official programs and inspired private institutions abroad.

# Education in Portugal
Portugal’s education history includes late literacy improvements, significant under the Estado Novo regime, and modern expansions in basic and higher education. Basic education covers nine years split into three cycles, followed by three years of secondary education with general, professional, or artistic tracks. National exams guide progression and university entrance, which follows a system restructured under the Bologna Process since 2006. Higher education has university and polytechnic subsystems, with distinct professional focuses. Private education exists alongside public, with debate over equity and quality. School violence is present but less prevalent in private schools. International schools serve expat communities. Criticisms include dropout rates, functional illiteracy, fluctuating policies, and disparities in private versus public education quality.

# Education in the United States
Education in the U.S. is decentralized with governance shared between federal, state, and local entities. It includes early childhood education (preschool), K–12 compulsory education, and optional higher education offered by diverse institutions, including public, private, community colleges, and for-profit universities. The U.S. spends heavily per student but faces achievement gaps linked to socioeconomic and racial disparities. Standardized testing is widespread but controversial. Issues include teacher shortages, pay disparities, and student loan debt. Historical developments include desegregation and legislation like No Child Left Behind and Every Student Succeeds Act. COVID-19 prompted a rapid shift to digital learning, exposing inequalities. Literacy rates are high but challenge remains. Schools face debates over curriculum content, sex education, and safety measures. Affirmative action policies have been recently restricted by the Supreme Court.

# Education in Australia
Australia’s education is the responsibility of states and territories, with a national curriculum framework implemented since 2010. Education includes early childhood (preschool), primary education (7 years typically), and secondary education (Years 7–12). Government schools educate about two-thirds of students, with the remainder in Catholic or independent schools. School funding is shared among federal, state, and local governments. Indigenous education aims to close attainment gaps. Specialized schools include selective and special needs institutions. Tertiary education consists mainly of 42 universities and vocational training colleges, with Australia being a top destination for international students. Issues include educational funding debates, disparities in Indigenous education, bilingual education programs, and school violence. School uniforms are standard, and the academic year generally runs February to December.

# Education in the Philippines
Education in the Philippines has evolved from pre-colonial vernacular and Islamic schooling to Spanish religious and public schools, then to a U.S.-modeled system with six years of elementary and four years of secondary education. The current K–12 system (implemented from 2012 to 2017) includes kindergarten and 12 years of basic education; senior high school offers academic, technical-vocational, sports, and arts tracks. Filipino, English, and mother tongue languages are used as media of instruction. Higher education includes public and private institutions regulated by the Commission on Higher Education. Challenges include classroom shortages, underfunding, quality of teachers, education-employment mismatches, and impacts from COVID-19 such as learning loss and inequities in access. Alternative learning systems and indigenous education frameworks address non-traditional learners and cultural relevance.

# Education in Romania
Romania mandates 15 years of compulsory education, including kindergarten to 12th grade. The system is primarily public with low private sector presence. Kindergarten became fully compulsory by 2023. Elementary education spans preparatory class through grade 8, followed by four years of secondary education with multiple academic and vocational tracks. Admission to high school depends on a national exam, leading to various school types including prestigious National Colleges. The National Baccalaureate exam is required for university admission. Foreign languages, mainly English and French, are taught widely. Urban schools offer richer resources and extracurricular activities compared to rural areas, where attendance and facilities lag. Higher education follows the Bologna system, with universities offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Romania has active international academic collaborations but faces issues like rural dropouts and debates on religious education in schools.

# Education in Malaysia
Malaysia’s education originated from Islamic religious schools and British colonial missionary schools. The national education system includes Malay-medium national schools and vernacular schools in Chinese and Tamil languages. Primary education is compulsory starting at age seven, lasting six years, followed by five years of secondary education (Form 1–5), with public secondary education primarily in Malay. The government implements initiatives such as the Dual Language Program to strengthen English proficiency in science and mathematics. Islamic religious schools and Chinese independent high schools provide alternative or supplementary education. Higher education includes public universities, private colleges, and polytechnics; admissions sometimes involve racial quotas favoring the Malay majority. School uniforms are widespread, and reforms target bilingual education, curriculum standardization, and reducing ethnic polarization in schools. Challenges include language policy debates, gender disparities in tertiary education, and the legacy of quota systems.
