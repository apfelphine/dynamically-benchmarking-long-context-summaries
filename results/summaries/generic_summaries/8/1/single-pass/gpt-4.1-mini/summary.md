# Education in the Philippines
Education evolved from oral traditions and vocational training in the pre-colonial period to formal schooling introduced by the Spanish, who established the first schools and universities primarily run by religious orders, and initiated free public education in 1863. The American period saw the establishment of a centralized public school system, introduction of English, and the arrival of Thomasite teachers to expand education. The K–12 system, implemented over nine years starting in 2008 and fully effective by 2017, modernized the curriculum to align internationally, adding kindergarten and two years of senior high school.

Contemporary education includes compulsory kindergarten, mother-tongue-based early education, and tracks in senior high school for specialization. Although enrollment has increased to over 32 million students, challenges remain with classroom shortages, funding, teacher quality, and learning outcomes, especially in disadvantaged regions. The government provides free tertiary education in public institutions, and alternative learning systems cater to out-of-school youth. Recent reforms include introducing the "Matatag curriculum" to reduce subject congestion and emphasize Filipino identity.

# Education in Malaysia
Education began with Islamic and Malay vernacular schools, with British colonial education establishing English-medium schools primarily for elites and civil servants. Post-independence efforts standardized education into national and national-type schools (Malay, Chinese, Tamil mediums), adopting the Razak Report framework for a unified curriculum. Private missionary schools transitioned gradually to Malay as the medium, yet retained their religious affiliations.

Malaysia’s education system includes preschool (optional), six years of primary education, and five years of secondary education, with national exams like UPSR and SPM (recently reformed). Secondary schools include national, national-type, selective, and Chinese independent high schools, the latter offering Mandarin-medium instruction and recognized external exams (UEC). The Dual Language Program offers selected Science and Mathematics instruction in English. Higher education is divided between public universities and private institutions, with a racial quota system historically affecting admissions. Challenges involve language policy debates, gender disparities favoring males in tertiary education, and the ongoing recognition of Chinese independent school qualifications.

# Education in the United States
The U.S. education system developed from locally funded public schools in the 19th century to a decentralized system with compulsory schooling laws adopted progressively. Education is divided into early childhood, K–12 (kindergarten through 12th grade), and post-secondary levels. Public education is funded primarily through local and state taxes, supplemented by federal programs, and administered mostly at the state and local levels, with federal oversight via laws like No Child Left Behind and the Every Student Succeeds Act.

Higher education is diverse, comprising public and private universities, community colleges, and vocational institutions; however, rising tuition and student debt are significant concerns. Educational attainment varies by socioeconomic and racial groups, with persistent achievement gaps. Issues include standardized testing controversies, teacher shortages and pay disparities, and debates over affirmative action. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed inequalities and accelerated online learning, impacting enrollment and mental health.

# Education in Romania
Romania mandates 15 years of compulsory education, including kindergarten through 12th grade. The system follows the French model, with reforms to increase compulsory schooling and harmonize curricula. Kindergarten is structured into age-specific groups, some of which became recently compulsory. Primary education includes a preparatory year and diversified curricula as students advance to gymnasium. High school admission is through national exams, with various academic and vocational tracks culminating in a national Baccalaureate exam required for university entry.

Higher education consists of public and private universities and polytechnics, aligned with the Bologna Process, and includes bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Challenges include rural education disparities, school shift systems, debates over religious education, and brain drain due to study abroad. Literacy rates are high overall but vary among minorities.

# Education in Finland
Finland emphasizes universal and egalitarian education, with compulsory basic education from grades 1 through 9 and recently extended to upper secondary education until age 18. Early childhood education prioritizes play-based learning and social development over formal reading or math instruction. Small class sizes, highly trained teachers (all requiring master's degrees), and autonomy characterize the system.

Upper secondary education offers academic or vocational tracks, with free tuition and strong university preparation. Higher education is divided into research universities and universities of applied sciences, following merit-based, transparent admissions without interviews or essays. Since 2017, non-EU/EEA students pay tuition fees.

Finland promotes bilingualism and foreign language proficiency. Adult education and lifelong learning are strongly supported. Finnish education is internationally acclaimed for quality and innovation, with efforts to export its pedagogy abroad.

# Education in Australia
Australia’s education system is managed primarily by states and territories, with federal funding support. Primary education covers preparatory years to Year 6 or 7, followed by secondary education up to Year 12. Preschool is not compulsory but widely attended from ages 3–5. The Australian Curriculum provides a national standard across subjects, while school types include government, Catholic, and private schools.

Selective and specialist schools exist, as do international schools following foreign curricula. School uniforms are common, though recent reforms plan for more flexible attire. Tertiary education includes 42 universities and numerous vocational providers, with Australia hosting a large international student population—a significant economic sector.

Challenges include addressing Indigenous educational disparities, funding reforms inspired by the Gonski Reports, and bilingual education efforts for Indigenous and immigrant communities. School violence and religious education delivery vary across jurisdictions.

# Education in South Africa
South Africa’s education system is governed by separate departments for basic and higher education. Basic education includes compulsory Grade R (reception) and Grades 1 through 12, grouped into General Education and Training (GET) and Further Education and Training (FET). Schools vary widely in resources and performance, with disparities along socioeconomic and racial lines.

Historically shaped by apartheid, the system suffered from segregation and unequal funding, with continued challenges in teacher qualification, school violence, and infrastructure. Technological integration has expanded slowly, hindered by connectivity and resource gaps, though government initiatives aim to improve ICT access.

Higher education comprises autonomous universities and colleges, with admissions tied to matriculation certificates. The sector continues to grapple with graduate unemployment skewed toward humanities. Violence and inequality remain significant issues within schools.

# Education in Portugal
Portugal’s education evolved from church-led instruction and endured high illiteracy into the 20th century, improved significantly under Estado Novo with compulsory schooling and adult literacy campaigns. The current system features nine years of basic education divided into cycles, followed by three years of compulsory secondary education with general, professional, or artistic tracks.

Higher education is split between universities and polytechnics, both public and private, with adherence to the Bologna Process since 2006. Admission to universities requires passing national exams, and degrees range from bachelor to doctorate. Private institutions coexist with public ones, with concerns over quality and access.

Challenges include high dropout rates, functional illiteracy, controversies over religious education, and school violence. Portugal hosts foreign international schools primarily in major cities and has ongoing debates over education policy reforms and equity.
