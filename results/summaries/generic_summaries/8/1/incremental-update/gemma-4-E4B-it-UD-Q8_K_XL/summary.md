## Comparative Education Systems: Global Overview

### System Profiles and Challenges
**United States:** Features a decentralized, locally funded K-12 system struggling with financial instability, inequality, racial disparities, teacher shortages due to low pay, and mental health concerns.
**Philippines:** Operates a 13-year K-12 structure challenged by funding limitations and teacher quality issues, resulting in low PISA scores and high Global Learning Poverty.
**Malaysia:** Employs race-based policies, including a 90% Bumiputera quota and mandated language options, causing linguistic friction stemming from the 1956 Razak Report.
**Romania:** Requires 15 years of compulsory schooling, influenced by French/Soviet models, using a 1-10 grading scale and a central *Bac* Exam. Challenges include urban/rural gaps, "brain drain," and tertiary education structured by the Bologna process, despite high literacy.
**Finland:** Prioritizes equity through comprehensive schooling until age 18 with low-stakes grading and strong student rights. The system promotes multilingualism and advanced tertiary cooperatives, with the 2016 curriculum focusing on phenomenon-based learning.
**Australia:** Features shared governance between states/territories and federal tertiary support (HECS), utilizing a national ACARA curriculum. While having high Education Index scores, PISA rankings are moderate, with ongoing efforts to benefit Indigenous and CALD students.
**South Africa:** Basic education (Grade 1-12) is managed by the DBE/DHET, using quintile-based grants linked to poverty. Post-apartheid reforms unified departments and support 12 languages. Major obstacles include STEM/healthcare mismatches, poor infrastructure, teacher competency gaps, union influence, and violence, exacerbated by COVID-19.
**Portugal:** Has seen historical progress in democratizing secondary and higher education. The system comprises optional pre-primary, nine-year Basic Education, and specialized Secondary Education. Tertiary education is divided between a theoretical University System and a practical Polytechnic System, both compliant with the Bologna Process, requiring rigorous teacher qualifications. While public access is high, the system contends with policy flux, high dropout rates, functional illiteracy, and increased school violence. Historically, Portugal faced criticism for the rapid expansion of low-standard polytechnical institutions in the 1990s/2000s, and the "Novas Oportunidades" policy granted educational equivalencies based on life experience, leading to concerns about artificially inflated statistics.

### Comparative Observations
Globally, educational governance varies from decentralized (US) to varied centralized models, with systems encountering diverse issues such as financial instability (US), learning poverty (Philippines), racial quotas (Malaysia), regional disparities (Romania), and infrastructural deficits (South Africa). Finland stands out for its equity focus, while Portugal illustrates literacy improvement alongside a dual, Bologna-compliant tertiary structure, even while navigating debates over quality, equity, and past expansion issues.
