# Education in Southeast Asia and Comparative Perspectives with the United States, Romania, Finland, Australia, South Africa, and Portugal

## Education in the Philippines

The Philippine education system evolved from pre-colonial informal learning to formal colonial education emphasizing Christianity and English. The 2008–2017 K–12 reform extended basic education to 12 years plus Kindergarten, offering academic and vocational streams. The 2023 Matatag curriculum prioritizes nationalism, inclusivity for indigenous and Muslim learners, and language transition from mother tongues to Filipino and English. Despite large enrollments, dropout and out-of-school rates remain high. Higher education is largely private, overseen by CHED, facing access, affordability, and labor market relevance challenges. Public schools suffer underfunding, infrastructure shortages, and pandemic-induced learning losses.

## Education in Malaysia

Malaysia’s education centers on vernacular primary schools (Malay, English, Chinese, Tamil) and Malay-/English-medium secondary schools shaped by the 1956 Razak Report. Since the 1970s, Malay instruction and compulsory English are emphasized. Vernacular schools hold constitutional protection but receive less funding. Education includes non-compulsory preschool, compulsory primary, full secondary education (from 2024), alternative secondary streams, and Chinese Independent High Schools with certificates not qualifying for university. National Blueprints address ethnic polarization, bilingualism, inclusivity, modernization, teacher quality, and Bumiputera preferences. Post-secondary pathways include matriculation, STPM, vocational polytechnics, and private international programs.

## Comparative Insights: Philippines and Malaysia

Both balance multilingual education integrating national languages, vernaculars, and English amid ethnic diversity. Challenges include equitable access, teacher quality, curriculum relevance, and ethnic identity. Malaysia grapples with ethnic-based funding disparities and bilingual debates; the Philippines emphasizes inclusive nationalist curricula.

## Education in the United States

Rooted in 19th-century reforms and civil rights advances, schooling is compulsory ages 5–14/18, with 85% attending public schools and widespread non-compulsory early childhood education. Over 7,000 higher education institutions exist with moderate graduation rates. Funding depends heavily on local property taxes, causing disparities favoring affluent districts; federal aid targets vulnerable groups. Teacher salaries contribute to burnout and moonlighting. College costs generate over $1.5 trillion in student debt; adjunct faculty predominate, raising quality concerns. Curricula vary; instruction is English-dominant; standardized tests carry biases affecting immigrant, Black, and Hispanic students. Harsh discipline fuels school-to-prison pipelines. Affirmative action ended in 2023 intensifying equity debates. Sex education and LGBTQ content vary by state; surveillance raises privacy issues. Libraries are accessible but parental involvement is low. Teacher burnout persists amid calls for greater societal respect and support.

## Education in Romania

Romania mandates 15 years of education starting at ages 5–6, blending French and Soviet influences. Kindergarten is partly compulsory, followed by elementary and six types of secondary education, including National Colleges with international curricula and vocational apprenticeships. Curricula emphasize sciences, humanities, and bilingualism, with national exams for secondary placement and university entry. English is the dominant foreign language. Higher education aligns with Bologna standards; 2016 enrollment included over 530,000 students mostly in medicine and technical fields. Universities enjoy autonomy; reforms post-1990 modernized the system and raised literacy near 99%, but minority illiteracy persists. Underfunding leads to unofficial parental support. Religious education is optional and debated; brain drain through emigration persists.

## Education in Finland

Finland mandates grades 1 to 9 before academic or vocational tracks, with vocational paths enabling tertiary education. Early childhood education focuses on play, social skills, and independence, with free universal daycare/preschool from 8 months to age 6. Basic education features small class sizes, autonomous unionized teachers, curricula promoting multilingualism, arts, minimal homework, outdoor activities, and reading for pleasure. Assessment uses verbal and numerical methods with support for struggling students. Schools are municipality-funded; private schools tuition-free. Upper secondary education is compulsory, offering academic and vocational tracks with matriculation exams for university access. Teachers are highly qualified. Tertiary education includes research universities and universities of applied sciences with merit-based admissions and Bologna degrees. Since 2017, tuition fees apply to non-EEA students. Adult education emphasizes lifelong learning with phenomenon-based curricula fostering 21st-century skills. Finnish educational exports and AI initiatives are notable.

## Education in Australia

Australia educates about 4.1 million students; two-thirds attend government schools, the rest Catholic or independent funded publicly and by fees. Schooling is compulsory ages 5–6 to 15–17, varying by state. The Australian Curriculum standardizes content to Year 9; senior secondary certifications vary. Specialized schools and IB programs exist. NAPLAN tests assess skills nationwide; international assessments provide context. Bilingual education peaked mid-20th century but declined due to English-only policies; recent Indigenous language revivals exist. Religious education is optional, secular, and non-proselytizing. School violence has risen significantly since 2008. Higher education comprises 42 universities and TAFE institutions with over 1.18 million students in 2021. The Group of Eight leads research. Australia ranks second on the UN Education Index with mid-to-high PISA scores; several universities rank highly globally. International students, mainly from Asia, are a growing economic factor.

## Education in South Africa

South African education divides into Basic (Grade R to 12) and Higher Education, funded primarily by government taxes and fees with exemptions for the poorest. Basic education includes GET and FET phases; learner-teacher ratios average ~30:1. Post-apartheid reforms unified departments, removed discriminatory curricula, and introduced continuous assessment. All 12 official languages are used early; English learning starts in Grade 1 except in Afrikaans schools. Education rights extended to undocumented children in 2019. ICT adoption since the late 1990s includes programs like MELFA and Dr. Maths but faces challenges: limited internet access (22% in 2017), inadequate teacher skills, overcrowding, and infrastructure gaps. Government plans aim for broadband ≥100 Mbps in all schools by 2030 and enhanced teacher training. COVID-19 exposed digital divides worsening disparities. Academic outcomes are weak due to uncommitted teachers, outdated curricula, dropout rates, and socioeconomic factors. School violence is high with intervention programs ongoing. Higher education requires Matriculation Endorsement; over one million students attend universities and colleges with institutional autonomy. Post-1994 mergers reduced institutions from 36 to 22 promoting transformation, yet segregation legacies persist. Graduate unemployment remains a concern tied to field preferences.

## Education in Portugal

Portugal’s system evolved from clerical and imperial roots, expanding literacy through the 20th century and adopting Bologna Process reforms. Basic education spans three cycles (grades 1–9) with national evaluations, followed by secondary education streams: General, Technological, and Artistic, leading to diplomas or professional/apprenticeship tracks. The school year runs mid-September to mid-June with national exams in June–July. Higher education distinguishes universities (theoretical/research) and polytechnics (practical/professional), with selective admission via national exams. Bologna reforms structured degrees into Licenciatura, Mestrado, and Doutoramento. Teacher education requires a 4-year Licenciado for early grades and a master's for basic and secondary levels; higher education faculty meet academic but not formal pedagogy standards, supported by professional development centers. Distance education is offered exclusively by Universidade Aberta. Private schools, mostly urban, vary widely in quality; public schools generally serve most students and are perceived as higher quality. Some private universities faced scandals prompting stricter inspections. School violence increased since the 1990s; the state runs the Escola Segura safety program. International schools cluster in Lisbon, Porto, Algarve, and Madeira offering international curricula and IB programs.

Critiques highlight frequent policy shifts, high dropout rates, significant functional adult illiteracy (~48%), and concerns about some private and polytechnic institutions functioning as diploma mills with low standards and unclear admissions. The 1990s-2000s saw rapid expansion of private higher education and polytechnics with lower standards causing inefficient resource use and poor outcomes. In 2008, over 85,000 teachers protested education policies, including a controversial teacher evaluation system. The "Novas Oportunidades" program certified adult learners based on life experience rather than formal exams, viewed by critics as an attempt to improve statistics without real workforce qualification impact.

## Integrated Overview

This synthesis reveals diverse historical legacies and system designs across Southeast Asia, the United States, Romania, Finland, Australia, South Africa, and Portugal, encompassing language policies, funding models, curricula, assessments, ICT adoption, teacher education, and tertiary frameworks. Recurring challenges include equitable access, ethnic and linguistic inclusivity, resource disparities, brain drain, school violence, graduate unemployment, digital divides, policy instability, and enduring inequalities rooted in historical segregation or social stratification. Reforms aim to enhance quality, inclusivity, internationalization, and adaptability to 21st-century demands, with variable success and persistent areas for improvement.
