# **Merged Summary: Comparative Overview of Education Systems in the Philippines, Malaysia, the United States, Romania, Finland, Australia, South Africa, and Portugal**

## **Global Trends in Equity, Quality, and Innovation**

Finland stands as a global benchmark for equitable, student-centered education, characterized by universal access, minimal standardized testing, and strong teacher autonomy. Since 2016–2017, **phenomenon-based learning** has been mandatory, integrating subjects through real-world, interdisciplinary challenges. The system emphasizes well-being, play-based early learning, and holistic development, with free early childhood education from age 8 months to 5 years. Students enter a nine-year comprehensive school (*peruskoulu*) and later choose between academic (*lukio*) and vocational tracks—both of which offer access to tertiary education. Finland leads in digital innovation with a free, widely accessible online AI course that has attracted over 220,000 sign-ups. Despite strong performance in PISA and high student outcomes, proficiency in Swedish remains limited among many citizens. The centralized, non-selective model ensures equity, with public universities historically offering tuition-free access to EU/EEA students—though non-EEA students now face fees of €1,500–18,000.

In contrast, the United States, despite hosting some of the world’s top universities and high reading literacy (99%), suffers from deep systemic inequities. Public education serves 85% of students, but funding is highly decentralized and property-based, leading to significant disparities in rural and low-income areas. Racial achievement gaps persist, teacher burnout is severe (nearly 44% work over 52 hours/week), and over 90% of high school students admit to cheating. Over 40% of students enroll in remedial courses, and student debt exceeds $1.5 trillion. The *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* decision ended race-based admissions, raising equity concerns. While the U.S. excels in research and global university rankings, its education system struggles with access, affordability, and student well-being.

Romania expanded compulsory education to 14 years by 2020, introducing a mandatory middle kindergarten and preparatory year (ages 6–7). Public education dominates, with less than 2% of students in private schools. The national **Evaluarea Națională** (age 14–15) determines secondary placement, and the **National Baccalaureate Exam (bac)** requires a minimum average of 6.00 for university admission. Higher education includes 97 institutions, with strong performance in medicine and technology. However, rural students face serious barriers: up to 25% of children aged 11–14 do not attend due to long commutes (up to 10 km) and poor infrastructure. Religious education is optional, and informal "class funds" are sometimes collected from parents, raising equity and transparency concerns.

Malaysia’s multilingual, ethnically structured system promotes cultural preservation through National Schools (SK), Chinese (SJK(C)), and Tamil (SJK(T)) vernacular education. Since 2003, promotion in primary school no longer depends on performance, and the UASA (Year 4–6) assessment evaluates Malay, English, Science, and Math. The **Dual Language Program (DLP)**, now in 2,420 schools, teaches Science and Math in English to boost global competitiveness. However, concerns persist over Bahasa Melayu proficiency and resistance to Vision Schools, which aim to foster inter-ethnic interaction but face fears of language dilution. Secondary education lasts five years (Form 1–5), with Form 5 culminating in the SPM (GCSE equivalent). Public universities maintain a 90% Bumiputera quota, limiting access for Chinese (5.43%) and Indian (3.72%) students—highlighting persistent ethnic equity issues. Despite reforms like KSSM and KSSR, language policy and gender imbalances remain deeply contested.

The Philippines has transitioned to a modern K–12 system (2008–2012), extending basic education to 12 years with specialized tracks: Academic, TVL, Sports, and Arts and Design. Kindergarten became compulsory in 2011, and the old K–4 system was phased out by 2017. Enrollment has grown (from 24 million to 28 million), but 6% of children remain out of school, with completion rates at 95.9% (primary), 81.7% (lower secondary), and 72.6% (upper secondary). Foundational skills remain weak: only 10% meet reading and 17% meet math standards. Systemic challenges include teacher shortages (licensure pass rates of 28–36%), underfunding, poor infrastructure (148,000 classrooms missing as of 2025), and bureaucratic inefficiencies. Higher education is dominated by private institutions (88% of over 1,975), which are often unaffordable. Policy efforts like RA 11713 (teacher scholarships) and RA 11997 (teacher allowances) aim to improve support but face corruption-related implementation barriers. Culturally responsive programs such as **Alternative Learning System (ALS)** and **Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE)** enhance inclusivity for marginalized groups.

Australia operates a decentralized system with compulsory schooling from age 5 to 17, depending on the state. The **Australian Curriculum**, developed by ACARA, ensures standardization across government, Catholic, and private schools. In 2022, 15-year-olds ranked 9th in reading and science and 10th in math in PISA, with less than 60% achieving proficiency. The national retention rate in Year 10–12 was 79.9% in 2024. Australia excels in tertiary education, hosting over 812,000 international students and ranking highly in research and university rankings. However, Indigenous students face persistent disadvantages, school violence remains a concern, and disparities in digital access and quality instruction persist. Bilingual initiatives, such as "both-ways" programs in the Northern Territory, support Indigenous language revitalization.

South Africa’s post-apartheid education system, formalized by the *South African Schools Act (1996)*, aimed to eliminate racial segregation and ensure equitable access. Despite reforms, challenges remain: a high learner-to-teacher ratio (30:1), unqualified teachers, outdated curricula, and widespread school violence—especially sexual assault and gang activity—are critical issues. The *Curriculum 2005* (Outcomes-Based Education) was abandoned due to poor implementation. All 12 official languages are used in early education, with English introduced in Grade 1 and adopted as the primary language from Grade 4. Government programs like *South Africa Connect* and the "No Fee" initiative support disadvantaged schools in the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal, and Limpopo. Digital access remains limited (~22% in 2017), and infrastructure gaps worsened during the pandemic. Higher education is accessible via matriculation, with over a million students enrolled, though graduate unemployment and underemployment—especially in STEM and healthcare—remain significant.

Portugal’s education system includes nine years of basic education (three cycles) followed by three years of compulsory secondary education. Students must pass national exams to obtain the *Certificate of Basic Education* and progress. Secondary education offers general, scientific, or technical/professional tracks, culminating in the *Certificado de Habilitações do Ensino Secundário*. Since the 1980s, access has improved, with notable gains in PISA performance since 2010. The system is aligned with the **Bologna Process**, standardizing higher education into three-tier degrees: *Bacharelato*, *Licenciatura*, *Mestrado*, and *Doutoramento*. However, confusion persists in fields like engineering and law, where older titles remain inaccessible. Admission to public universities requires a *Diploma de Ensino Secundário* and performance on the ENES exams, with enrollment capped by a *numerus clausus*. Teacher education requires a 4-year *Licenciado* program for primary teachers and longer programs for secondary educators, with a 2007 law mandating a *Mestre* degree for secondary teachers. Public institutions are generally equitable but face overcrowding and poor infrastructure. Private schools and polytechnics are often elite or criticized for low academic standards and diploma mills. Systemic issues include high dropout rates, functional illiteracy, flawed teacher evaluations (a catalyst for a 2008 protest), and the *Novas Oportunidades* program, which grants adult education equivalencies based on life experience—raising concerns about workforce readiness.

## **Cross-Cutting Themes**

- **Equity and Access:** All systems face disparities, especially in rural or disadvantaged communities. South Africa and Portugal struggle with teacher shortages, infrastructure, and digital access. The Philippines and Malaysia show persistent gaps in rural and marginalized populations.
- **Curriculum Reform:** Finland and Portugal have implemented transformative shifts—phenomenon-based learning and the Bologna Process—while South Africa’s *Outcomes-Based Education* failed due to poor implementation. Malaysia’s DLP and Australia’s national curriculum reflect efforts to modernize and globalize.
- **Global Assessments:** Finland consistently ranks at the top in PISA; Australia and Portugal show moderate to improving performance; South Africa and the Philippines lag in key subjects.
- **Innovation and Digitalization:** Finland leads in AI and digital education; South Africa and Portugal face significant digital divides.
- **Social and Cultural Factors:** Language policy, Indigenous education, religious instruction, and ethnic equity play pivotal roles in shaping outcomes. Finland’s inclusive model contrasts with Malaysia’s ethnic-based divisions and South Africa’s legacy of segregation.

**Conclusion:**  
The comparative analysis reveals that equitable, student-centered systems—like Finland’s—achieve high outcomes without stress, emphasizing teacher autonomy, well-being, and inclusive policies. In contrast, systems with structural inequities, fragmented governance, or poor implementation (e.g., U.S., Philippines, South Africa, Portugal) face persistent challenges in access, quality, and student outcomes. Sustainable education reform requires strong policy implementation, equitable investment, inclusive curricula, and robust teacher support—factors that are most effectively realized in systems that prioritize equity, cultural responsiveness, and holistic development.
