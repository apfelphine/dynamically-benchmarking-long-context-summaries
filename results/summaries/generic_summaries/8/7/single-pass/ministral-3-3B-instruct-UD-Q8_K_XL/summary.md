### **Education in Finland**

#### **History**
Finland’s education system traces back to Lutheranism, with literacy being a core tenet of the state religion. The first university, the Royal Academy of Turku, was founded in 1640. Early literacy rates were modest, with 50% literacy by the late 18th century and 80–90% by the mid-19th century. The system evolved from Swedish to Finnish as education became more accessible. Under Swedish rule, education was divided into pedagogio (basic reading/writing), trivial school (grammar, Latin, rhetoric), gymnasium (pre-university), and university. The 19th century saw the introduction of *kansakoulu* ("people’s school") and *oppikoulu* ("learning school"), leading to a bifurcated system of academic and vocational tracks. By 1921, attendance became mandatory, and free school lunches were introduced in 1948. The system was reformed in the 1970s, shifting from a rigid structure to a more flexible model with grades 1–9 compulsory, followed by academic (*lukio*) and vocational (*ammattioppilaitos*) tracks.

#### **Early Childhood Education**
Finland emphasizes high-quality daycare and kindergarten to foster social skills, communication, and independence. Early childhood education is voluntary but widely adopted, with free universal daycare for ages 8 months to 5 years and a year of preschool at age 6. The system prioritizes play-based learning, respect for individuality, and holistic development. Parents receive books as part of the "maternity package," and early education is seen as critical for lifelong learning, with 90% of brain growth occurring in the first five years. The focus is on preparing children for formal learning by age 7, with a strong emphasis on reading and numeracy through play.

#### **Basic Comprehensive Education**
Finland’s compulsory education spans 9 years (grades 1–9), with a new law expanding it to ages 7–18, including upper secondary education (*lukio* or vocational tracks). The system is small-class, student-centered, and emphasizes creativity and critical thinking. Grading ranges from 4 to 10, with no high-stakes testing. Schools are funded by municipalities, with few private options. Teachers have autonomy in teaching methods but follow a structured curriculum. Small class sizes (typically ≤20 students) and a relaxed atmosphere are hallmarks, with minimal homework to encourage extracurricular activities. Free school lunches, health care, and materials are provided.

#### **Upper Secondary Education**
Upper secondary education begins at 15–16 years and lasts 3–4 years. Students choose between academic (*lukio*) and vocational tracks, with both leading to university admission or vocational careers. Admissions to academic tracks are based on GPA, and students can take the *matriculation examination* (Ylioppilastutkinto) for university entry. Vocational tracks prepare students for polytechnic institutes or direct employment. The system is merit-based, with no racial or socioeconomic quotas. Recent reforms include the introduction of a matriculation exam for vocational students, though this is not yet widely implemented.

#### **Higher Education**
Finland’s higher education system comprises traditional universities (*yliopisto*) and universities of applied sciences (*ammattikorkeakoulu*). Admissions are merit-based, with no application essays or human factors in selection. Universities focus on research, while polytechnics emphasize practical, work-oriented education. Tuition is free for EU/EEA students; non-EEA students pay €1,500–€18,000 annually. Doctoral degrees are awarded by universities, with master’s degrees available at both levels. The Bologna Process has influenced reforms, aiming to integrate vocational and academic qualifications.

#### **Teachers and Tertiary Education**
Teachers in Finland require a master’s degree and are highly respected. Tertiary education is divided into universities and polytechnics, with bachelor’s degrees taking 3–4 years and master’s degrees 2 years. Doctoral degrees are research-focused, with universities offering licentiate and doctoral programs. The system is meritocratic, with no underrepresented minority support or extracurricular weight in admissions.

#### **Future Prospects**
Finland’s education system is recognized globally for its excellence. Ongoing reforms aim to align vocational and academic tracks under the Bologna Process, with a focus on increasing higher education participation. Challenges include teacher shortages, underfunding, and the need to address educational inequality. The system’s emphasis on play-based learning, small class sizes, and student-centered approaches continues to be a model for global education reform.

### **Education in the United States**

#### **History**
The U.S. education system evolved from colonial-era public schools, founded by religious leaders, to a national network of compulsory education. The 19th century saw the rise of public high schools, influenced by Horace Mann’s reforms. The Civil War and Reconstruction led to the establishment of land-grant colleges and the integration of Black schools. The 20th century introduced compulsory education laws, standardized testing, and the Civil Rights Movement’s impact on desegregation. The No Child Left Behind Act (2001) emphasized accountability through standardized testing, while the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted in-person learning, leading to increased reliance on technology.

#### **Early Childhood Education**
Early childhood education in the U.S. includes preschools and kindergartens, with Head Start providing free early childhood education for low-income families. Preschools are non-compulsory but encouraged, with 69% of 4-year-olds enrolled. Curricula focus on music, art, science, and social activities. The Head Start program aims to prepare disadvantaged children for school, though limited enrollment is available.

#### **K–12 Education**
The U.S. education system is divided into primary (K–6), middle (7–8), and high school (9–12). Compulsory education varies by state, typically from ages 5–18. Public schools are funded by local, state, and federal governments, with private schools offering alternatives. The system is decentralized, with each state setting its own standards and assessments. Standardized testing, such as the SAT and ACT, is widely used for college admissions, though critics argue it reflects systemic inequities. The U.S. ranks below average in science and mathematics literacy but excels in reading literacy.

#### **Higher Education**
Higher education in the U.S. is optional and divided into colleges, universities, and vocational schools. Public universities are more affordable than private institutions, with tuition varying widely. The U.S. ranks 10th globally in higher education participation, though disparities exist between wealthy and low-income students. The system is highly competitive, with many students taking remedial courses due to gaps in foundational skills. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated educational inequality, with millions of students falling behind.

#### **Issues**
Key challenges include affordability, teacher shortages, and the impact of standardized testing. The U.S. faces a significant achievement gap between students from different socioeconomic backgrounds, racial groups, and geographic areas. The education-to-employment mismatch has led to underemployment and brain drain, particularly in healthcare and STEM fields. The system’s reliance on standardized testing has been criticized for overemphasizing test scores and underappreciating creativity and critical thinking.

### **Education in South Africa**

#### **Structure and Policies**
South Africa’s education system is overseen by the Department of Basic Education and the Department of Higher Education and Training. Basic education spans primary (Grade R–9) and secondary (Grade 10–12) levels, with matriculation certificates enabling access to tertiary education. The system is divided into two bands: General Education and Training (GET) and Further Education and Training (FET). Compulsory education is free, though schools may charge fees. Private schools operate independently, funded by tuition fees or government grants.

#### **History**
Early European schools in South Africa were established by Dutch and British missionaries. The apartheid regime segregated education, with separate schools for Black, Colored, and Indian students. The Bantu Education Act (1953) aimed to prepare Black students for menial jobs, while religious schools were nationalized. The 1970s saw protests over language policies, leading to the Soweto Uprising (1976). Post-apartheid reforms aimed to integrate schools and promote equality, though systemic challenges persist.

#### **Education Technology**
South Africa’s education system has gradually integrated ICTs, though barriers include limited internet access, teacher training, and resource constraints. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated digital learning, with many students lacking access to devices or reliable internet. The government has implemented initiatives like *South Africa Connect* to improve broadband access in schools. Despite progress, disparities remain, with rural schools lagging behind urban counterparts.

#### **Performance and Violence**
South Africa’s education system faces low academic performance, high dropout rates, and violence in schools. The 2020 National Senior Certificate exams saw 7 schools with a 0% pass rate, attributed to teacher absenteeism and inadequate resources. School violence, including gang fights and sexual assaults, is a growing concern, with the Education Department launching programs like *Hlayiseka* to address it.

### **Education in the Philippines**

#### **History**
The Philippines’ education system traces back to Spanish colonial schools, which introduced Christianity, Spanish, and Latin. The American period established a centralized public school system, with the K–12 curriculum implemented in 2012 to align with global standards. The Japanese occupation (1942–45) introduced Japanese ideologies, while post-war reforms aimed to modernize education. The 1987 Constitution made elementary education compulsory, and the Free Public Secondary Education Act (1988) expanded access.

#### **Educational Stages**
The Philippines follows a K–12 system, with kindergarten and 12 years of primary and secondary education. Primary education (Kindergarten–Grade 6) is compulsory, while secondary education (Grades 7–12) is divided into junior high (Grades 7–10) and senior high (Grades 11–12). The curriculum emphasizes mother tongue instruction in early grades, with English and Filipino as core subjects. Vocational schools offer specialized training, and international schools follow curricula from other countries.

#### **Issues**
Key challenges include affordability, teacher shortages, and poor learning outcomes. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted education, leading to learning loss and mental health issues among students. The Philippines ranks poorly in international assessments, with high dropout rates and disparities between urban and rural areas. The education-labor mismatch has contributed to underemployment and emigration of skilled graduates.

### **Education in Portugal**

#### **History**
Portugal’s education system dates back to the 13th century, with universities founded in Coimbra and Lisbon. The 19th century saw literacy rates drop to over 80%, but the Estado Novo regime (1932–74) expanded primary education. The Carnation Revolution (1974) led to reforms aligning education with European standards. The Bologna Process (2006) introduced a three-cycle system (bachelor’s, master’s, doctoral degrees).

#### **Education Stages**
Portugal’s education system includes preschool (Jardim de Infância), basic education (9 years), and secondary education (3 years). Compulsory education spans ages 6–15, with free public schools and subsidized private schools. The curriculum is structured into cycles, with exams at the end of each cycle. Secondary education branches into general, professional, and artistic programs, with access to higher education based on national exams.

#### **Issues**
Portugal faces challenges like underfunding, high dropout rates, and poor teacher quality. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted education, with many students falling behind. The Bologna Process has led to debates over curriculum changes and teacher training. The system remains underfunded compared to other European countries, with limited resources in rural areas.

### **Education in Malaysia**

#### **History**
Malaysia’s education system began with Islamic schools and British colonial schools. The Razak Report (1956) introduced a national curriculum with Malay and English as mediums of instruction. The 1967 National Language Act mandated Malay as the national language, leading to the conversion of English-medium schools to Malay-medium. The Dual Language Program (DLP) aims to balance language instruction in science and mathematics.

#### **Educational Stages**
Malaysia’s education system spans preschool, primary (1–6), secondary (1–5), and post-secondary education. Primary education is compulsory, with exams at the end of Year 6. Secondary education is divided into Form 1–5, with students taking the Sijil Pelajaran Malaysia (SPM) exam at the end of Form 5. The system includes Chinese Independent High Schools and international schools following various curricula.

#### **Issues**
Key challenges include language policies, racial quotas in universities, and underfunding. The Dual Language Program has faced criticism for its implementation criteria. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted education, with many students lacking access to devices or reliable internet. The system also struggles with teacher shortages and disparities between urban and rural areas.

### **Education in Australia**

#### **History**
Australia’s compulsory education system was introduced in the late 19th century, with each state and territory regulating its own curriculum. The Australian Curriculum, Assessment, and Reporting Authority (ACARA) has since developed a national curriculum. The system is funded by state and federal governments, with non-government schools receiving subsidies.

#### **Educational Stages**
Australia’s education system includes preschool, primary (K–6), secondary (7–12), and tertiary education. Compulsory education spans ages 5–17, with free public schools and subsidized private schools. The National Assessment Program – Literacy and Numeracy (NAPLAN) assesses students annually. Tertiary education includes universities and vocational education, with international students representing a significant portion of the student population.

#### **Issues**
Australia faces challenges like underfunding, teacher shortages, and disparities between Indigenous and non-Indigenous students. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted education, with many students falling behind. The system also struggles with the impact of standardized testing and the need to address educational inequality.

### **Education in Romania**

#### **History**
Romania’s education system dates back to the 19th century, with compulsory education introduced in 1864. The communist regime (1947–89) expanded education but introduced political propaganda and forced labor. Post-communist reforms aimed to align education with European standards, though systemic challenges persist.

#### **Educational Stages**
Romania’s education system includes kindergarten, primary (5 years), secondary (4 years), and tertiary education. Compulsory education spans ages 6–14, with exams at the end of each cycle. Secondary education branches into theoretical, vocational, and military schools. The Baccalaureate exam is required for university admission.

#### **Issues**
Romania faces challenges like underfunding, high dropout rates, and poor teacher quality. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted education, with many students falling behind. The system also struggles with disparities between urban and rural areas and the need to address educational inequality.
