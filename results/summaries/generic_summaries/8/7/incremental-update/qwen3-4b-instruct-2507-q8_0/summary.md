# **Final Comprehensive Summary of Finland’s and Comparative Education Systems**

Finland’s education system is globally renowned for its equity, student-centered approach, and strong early childhood foundations. Rooted in Lutheran traditions and emphasizing inclusivity, it features a non-selective, publicly funded structure from primary to tertiary levels. Primary education begins at age 7 with play-based, language-rich learning, minimal homework, and robust literacy instruction. Grading is formative and corrective, focused on improvement rather than punishment. Upper secondary education is compulsory and offers academic and vocational pathways, with the national matriculation exam serving as a key gateway to university. Tertiary education is merit-based, with transparent admissions and small class sizes, and includes both research-focused universities and practical universities of applied sciences. The system is reinforced by a national curriculum promoting phenomenon-based learning and lifelong learning, and has expanded globally through initiatives like *Suomi-koulut* and *Finnish-style schools*.

In contrast, the United States, South Africa, and the Philippines face deep structural challenges. The U.S. suffers from systemic inequities, credential inflation, and poor literacy foundations, exacerbated by decentralized governance, teacher shortages, and rising student debt. South Africa’s legacy of apartheid continues to affect access and quality, with persistent disparities in funding, infrastructure, and outcomes. The Philippines, despite reforms such as the K–12 system and the Matatag curriculum, struggles with underfunding, teacher quality, poor learning outcomes (including high learning poverty), and an education-labor mismatch—61% of expected schooling was lost during the pandemic.

Portugal’s system spans nine years of basic education (ages 6–15), divided into three cycles with increasing academic depth, including general and professional subjects from the 7th year onward. Students take national exams in Portuguese and Mathematics at the end of each cycle. Secondary education offers general, technological, or artistic programs, all requiring a *Diploma de Ensino Secundário* for higher education access. General and technological programs share core subjects such as Portuguese, Physical Education, Philosophy, and a foreign language, while professional and artistic programs provide vocational or creative qualifications. Access to higher education is determined by a national online process based on academic averages and subject-specific exam grades (0–20 scale). Special modalities include apprenticeships, recurrent education, and non-traditional pathways. The Bologna Process (since 2006) structures higher education into *Bacharelato*, *Licenciatura*, *Mestrado*, *Pós-Graduação*, *Doutoramento*, and *Agregação*. Admission to public institutions requires the *Diploma* and *ENES* exams, with a *numerus clausus* limiting enrollment; older applicants can enter via an extraordinary exam. Teacher education includes 4-year *Licenciatura* programs, with a 2007 law requiring a *Mestrado* in basic and secondary education. Higher education teachers lack formal qualifications but must meet institutional standards. The 1990s–2000s saw rapid growth in low-standard private polytechnical institutions, leading to declining quality. In 2008, over 85,000 teachers protested against evaluation reforms. The *Novas Oportunidades* initiative enabled adults with low formal education to obtain grade equivalences based on life experience, but it was criticized for lacking academic rigor.

Malaysia’s education system includes public and private schools following the National Curriculum, Chinese Independent High Schools (with a six-year structure and UEC exams), and international schools offering curricula such as Cambridge, Australian, Canadian, and IB. The Dual Language Program (DLP), launched in 2016, promotes English in STEM to enhance bilingualism and global competitiveness, with 2,420 schools by 2023. Preschool is optional, typically starting at age 3 and mainly offered by private institutions, with limited access in rural areas. Primary education (ages 7–12) is compulsory, lasting six years with no academic promotion requirements. The UPSR test was replaced in 2021 by the UASA test (assessing Malay, English, Science, and Math). Public schools are divided into Malay-medium *SK* and non-Malay-medium *SJK* (C/T), all teaching Malay and English. A mixed-medium policy for Science and Math in English was reversed in 2009. Islamic schools (SRA, SMKA, SABK, SAR) teach Islamic subjects and offer the STAM qualification, with SARs still operating in rural areas. Chinese independent high schools are funded by the Chinese community via UCSCAM, use Mandarin, and emphasize cultural preservation under colonial-era "divide and rule" policies. The government has not yet recognized UEC for public universities. School uniforms are largely Western-style, with a 2024 policy allowing flexibility. Key policy blueprints (2006–2010, 2013–2025) aimed to improve access, equity, and global competitiveness, but persistent issues include language tensions (English vs. Malay in science), racial quotas in universities (historically 55–90% for Bumiputeras), and gender imbalances (2:1 male-to-female ratio in polytechnics).

Australia features a state-managed system with compulsory education from age 5 to 17, depending on state, and a national curriculum introduced in 2010, reinforced by the Australian Curriculum (since 2014). Approximately 64% of students attend government schools, with Catholic and independent schools making up the remainder. Preschool is voluntary but supported by government, with most children attending 15 hours per week. Tertiary education is subsidized via HECS loans, with public universities dominating. Australia ranks highly in global education indices (2nd in 2018), though performance has declined in PISA (16th in reading, 29th in math, 17th in science). The system includes diverse non-government schools—Catholic, religious, Montessori, Steiner, special needs—and private schools (12% of total, serving 16% of students) with fees ranging from under $100 to over $500,000 annually. Governance is typically managed by elected councils or boards. Specialized schools (e.g., arts, STEM, selective) and international schools provide alternative pathways. Senior secondary qualifications vary by state (e.g., HSC, VCE), with alternative qualifications like the IB offering globally recognized pathways. Annual NAPLAN assessments evaluate literacy and numeracy, and Australia participates in international assessments (PISA, TIMSS, PIRLS). Key challenges include persistent disparities in outcomes for Indigenous students, ongoing school funding reforms (Gonski reports), and school violence. Australia leads in international student enrollment and has strong global university rankings (e.g., Group of Eight), with 1.18 million students enrolled in tertiary education. Bilingual education in Indigenous communities—particularly “both-ways” programs in Yirrkala and Areyonga—effectively preserves languages and improves student outcomes, reducing dropout rates. Religious education varies by state, with growing recognition of culturally responsive education.

Romania’s education system is compulsory for 15 years, from the final year of kindergarten to the twelfth grade, shaped by French influence and communist-era policies. Key developments include expansion to 14 years (2012), addition of a preparatory year, and inclusion of kindergarten and two final high school years (2020). Kindergarten, now divided into three age groups with compulsory middle and large groups (2023), is widely available but faces shortages—especially in rural areas. Public kindergartens are free; private institutions charge 400–1,600 lei/month. Elementary education (grades 1–8) follows a five-module academic year, with a 1–10 grading system (5 is the minimum passing grade). Students must maintain at least one mark per class and three marks per subject; those below 5 in two or more subjects may take a *corigență* exam, while three or more failures require repeating the year. A single teacher teaches most subjects, with specialized classes in arts and languages. At the end of 4th grade, students transition to *gymnasium*, with more specialized teaching, increased class hours (up to 32 weekly), and a broader curriculum including sciences, foreign languages, and civic education.

High school is compulsory for four years (since 2020), requiring students to pass the *Evaluarea Națională* (National Test) in Romanian and Mathematics (and a foreign language for minority schools), with a passing grade of 5. Admission is determined by a computerized system based on student preferences and scores, with top-tier schools requiring averages above 9.5–9.6. Five types of high schools offer university access: *National Colleges*, *Theoretical High Schools*, *Military Colleges*, *Economic/Technical Colleges*, *Liceu Tehnologic*, and *Professional-Dual Schools*. Academic profiles include science (Mate-info, Științe ale naturii), humanities (Filologie, Științe sociale), technical, environmental, vocational, and economic programs. The *Bacalaureate* exam (*bac*)—administered nationally and digitized since 2023—consists of six to eight subjects, including Romanian, a foreign language, and subject-specific exams. Students must achieve at least a 6.00 average and a 5.00 in each subject to pass; a perfect score earns special honors. Foreign languages are introduced early, with English dominant. Significant disparities persist between urban and rural schools, with urban institutions offering better facilities, extracurriculars, and resources, while rural schools face teacher shortages, small class sizes, and long travel distances. School days follow a 50-minute class system with seasonal adjustments. The *bac* is mandatory for university enrollment, though its weight varies by institution.

The system includes a strong cultural emphasis on academic olympiads, extracurriculars, and school traditions. National olympiads (*olimpiade*) offer benefits such as skipped school, better teacher treatment, and exam preparation; some grant exemptions from the National Test. Physical education teachers organize intramural competitions and mountain trips, and summer camps (*tabere*) remain a traditional feature. Field trips are rare and limited to museums or nature visits. After-school programs with lunch are increasingly available in urban areas, though public schools typically do not provide lunch. Uniforms are common in primary and middle schools (especially in urban areas) but optional in high schools, with no penalties for non-compliance. Long commutes to predominantly urban high schools contribute to high dropout rates among rural students.

Higher education in Romania comprises 97 institutions (56 public, 41 private), organized into three tiers based on research and education focus, following the Bologna Process with three cycles (bachelor’s, master’s, doctoral). ECTS credits enable academic transfer. In 2016, 531,586 students were enrolled (76.3% at the bachelor’s level). Romanian universities are recognized as top in Eastern Europe and attract international students (5.1% of enrollment in 2016). Universities operate with full autonomy and often have department-level self-governance. Admission varies by institution and field, involving exams, file competitions, essays, or interviews—state universities have stricter selection processes. Romania actively participates in EU and international programs (e.g., Erasmus, Tempus, CEEPUS, Fulbright), aligning with European standards and promoting academic mobility and research.

Romania has made significant progress in education, with near-universal literacy (0.83% illiterate in 2021) and several universities ranked globally (e.g., Alexandru Ioan Cuza, Babeș-Bolyai, University of Bucharest). International programs such as Erasmus Mundus and recognition of degrees through the Netherlands (with titles like *baccalaureus* or *meester*) and the European Engineer (Eur. Ing.) title via FEANI enhance global credibility. Universities played a pivotal role in post-1990 democratization, leading student-led movements such as the University Square protests. However, rural education faces severe challenges: up to 25% of children aged 11–14 do not attend school, and dropout rates after eighth grade are high. Religious education remains controversial, with a 2014 Constitutional Court ruling requiring parental opt-in. Studying abroad has grown, contributing to brain drain, and secondary education remains underfunded, with illegal “class funds” totaling around 70 million euros annually. Despite progress, persistent inequities—especially in rural access, high costs of uniforms, and long travel distances—remain major barriers to inclusive and equitable education.
