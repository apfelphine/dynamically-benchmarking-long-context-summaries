# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in Finland, the United States, South Africa, the Philippines, Portugal, Malaysia, Australia, and Romania – Key Features, Challenges, and Comparative Insights**

## **Finland: Equity, Holistic Development, and Global Influence**

Finland’s education system is globally recognized for its equity, student-centered approach, and holistic development. Rooted in Lutheran traditions and formalized with universal access to basic education in 1898 and mandatory schooling in 1921, it underwent a transformative shift in the 1970s with the introduction of a nine-year *peruskoulu* (ages 7–16), later expanded to include upper secondary and vocational education up to age 18. This structure eliminates ability-based tracking, emphasizing play, cooperation, interdisciplinary learning, and well-being—focusing on social skills, independence, and early childhood development. Key features include small class sizes, minimal homework, strong mental health support, and a national mandate for bilingualism (all students learn English and either Finnish or Swedish).

Higher education is clearly segmented: universities offer theoretical and research-based programs, while universities of applied sciences provide practical, work-oriented training. Bachelor’s programs last 3–4 years in universities and 3.5–4.5 years in applied sciences, with master’s programs requiring two years of work experience (part-time options available). Doctoral and licentiate degrees are offered only in universities, and a unique professional degree—the Licentiate of Medicine—does not require a thesis. Since 2017, non-EEA students face tuition fees (€1,500–€18,000 per year), while EEA students study for free. Access to higher education is high (60–70% of youth), though recent reductions in student places—particularly in applied sciences—raise concerns about rising academic unemployment. Institutional mergers (e.g., Aalto and University of Eastern Finland) and greater collaboration between academic and vocational institutions are expanding, driven by the Bologna Process and national policy goals. Adult education is supported through vocational programs, municipal/private centers, and the "Open University" model, including non-degree, community-focused learning. Finland faces workforce shortages in trades due to an aging population and shrinking youth base, prompting expanded student places in Eastern Finland. The National Curriculum Framework 2016 promotes *phenomenon-based learning*, fostering critical thinking, creativity, and collaboration. Finland’s model is gaining global influence, with *Suomi-koulut* offering Finnish curriculum schools abroad and private institutions in the U.S., Qatar, and elsewhere adopting Finnish principles—such as play-based learning and holistic development—often with direct input from Finnish experts. The *Education Finland Programme* supports these methods through teacher training, curriculum design, and digital tools. Notably, in 2018, a free AI course launched by the University of Helsinki and Reaktor achieved over 220,000 sign-ups.

## **United States: Decentralized System, Persistent Inequities, and Evolving Challenges**

The U.S. education system is decentralized, governed by states and local school boards, with funding derived from local (often property-based), state, and federal sources. Despite high per-student spending—among the highest globally—funding disparities persist, especially in rural and low-income areas, due to limited federal oversight. Key federal programs like Title I, IDEA, and Child Nutrition provide targeted support, but increased funding has shown little correlation with improved student outcomes. Systemic issues include credential inflation, inadequate spelling instruction, and unscientific teaching methods such as three-cue reading. Curricula often reflect Eurocentric or historically inaccurate narratives (e.g., elevating Moses as a "founding father" in Texas), undermining inclusivity and educational quality.

Persistent inequities remain deeply entrenched, with significant achievement gaps between Black/Hispanic and Asian/Caucasian students, driven by institutional racism, socioeconomic disparities, and cultural bias in standardized testing. These disparities are estimated to cost the U.S. GDP between 9–16%. Standardized testing is widely criticized for reinforcing inequities and disproportionately burdening disadvantaged students. Immigrant students face additional barriers, including misplacement due to birthday-based grading, unmet expectations, and insufficient English literacy programs, leading to long-term Limited English Proficiency and academic setbacks. A severe teacher shortage—driven by low pay, burnout, and long work hours (average 52 hours/week)—exacerbates the crisis, with many teachers working multiple jobs to supplement income. Teacher compensation, emotional support, and professional respect must be strengthened to improve retention and equity. In 2023, *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* effectively ended affirmative action, raising concerns about marginalized group disadvantage. Corporal punishment remains legal in 17 states, and concerns over school safety, facial recognition, and student cheating (up to 90% of high school students have cheated, often due to task feasibility) persist. Despite efforts in accountability and pedagogical reform, access, quality, and equity remain major challenges. U.S. students rank below average internationally—25th in math, 17th in science, and 14th in reading—with weak problem-solving skills among millennials. College completion is deeply unequal: 77% of students in the wealthiest quartile earn degrees by age 24, compared to just 9% in the poorest. Only 36% graduate within four years, and enrollment disruptions—such as over a million children missing kindergarten in 2021–2022—highlight systemic instability.

## **South Africa: Legacy of Segregation, Ongoing Inequities, and Reform Efforts**

South Africa’s education system is structured into basic education (Grades 1–12), divided into General Education and Training (GET) and Further Education and Training (FET) phases, and higher education, overseen by the Department of Basic Education (DBE) and Department of Higher Education and Training. Historically, education was racially segregated under colonial policies, with the 1953 Bantu Education Act institutionalizing inferior education for Black students. The 1976 Soweto Uprising, sparked by resistance to Afrikaans-language instruction, became a pivotal moment in the anti-apartheid struggle. Post-apartheid reforms in 1994 merged 36 universities into 22, improving equity and autonomy. Despite progress, deep-rooted disparities in access, quality, and funding persist.

The system faces high learner-to-teacher ratios (average 30:1), uneven provincial funding, and substantial school fees (R2,500–R15,200 annually), with fee exemptions available only for Grades 1–9. Between 1984 and 1990, Black schools received significantly less funding, resulting in lower pass rates and poorer outcomes. White schools adopted "Model C"—a semi-private system with reduced state funding—many of whose features persisted into the post-apartheid era. In 1996, the *South African Schools Act* established a unified national system, and the *Curriculum 2005* (OBE) was introduced in 1998 but failed by 2006 due to poor implementation and was discontinued. Since 2006, education is delivered in 12 official languages, with English introduced from Grade 1 (since 2009), except in Afrikaans-speaking schools. In 2019, the High Court affirmed that children without birth certificates have a right to basic education. While higher education adopted ICT early, digital access remains limited—only about 22% of schools had reliable internet in 2017, with few devices and low teacher ICT competency. Government initiatives such as *South Africa Connect*, *Connect-ED*, and e-libraries aim to improve access, though the pandemic exposed deep inequities, especially in rural and disadvantaged areas. Ongoing challenges include racial inequities, weak teacher preparation, outdated curricula, high dropout rates, and widespread school violence—including sexual assault and gang-related incidents—prompting programs like *Hlayiseka* to address student safety and well-being.

## **Philippines: Structural Challenges, K–12 Reform, and Access Gaps**

The Philippine education system has evolved through colonial and post-colonial periods, with foundational developments during the Spanish era (1590s–1898), including the 1863 Educational Decree promoting free public schools and the rise of literacy and intellectual figures like José Rizal. The First Republic (1898–1901) enshrined free and compulsory education in the Malolos Constitution, though progress was disrupted by the Philippine–American War. The American period (1899–1946) introduced a centralized system, U.S.-trained teachers (Thomasites), and curriculum reforms such as the 1925 Monroe Survey. The landmark 2008 K–12 reform expanded compulsory education to 13 years—adding kindergarten (from 2011), six years of elementary, four years of junior high (Grades 7–10), and two years of senior high (Grades 11–12), with specialized tracks (Academic, TVL, Sports, Arts and Design) to prepare students for college or careers. The 1987 Constitution mandated free public secondary education, and institutions like CHED and TESDA were established in 1994. The 2017 Universal Access to Quality Tertiary Education Act made state universities and colleges tuition-free.

Despite these reforms, deep structural challenges persist. As of 2020, 1.6 million children (6% of school-aged children) remain out of school, with significant disparities by region, gender, and income. Only 72.6% complete upper secondary education, and just 10% of primary students meet basic reading standards. Foundational skills are weak, especially among the urban poor and in conflict-affected areas. Higher education remains inaccessible: only 49% of students from the richest decile enroll, compared to 17% from the poorest. A critical shortage of classrooms—148,000 as of 2025—exacerbates overcrowding. Chronic underfunding, poor learning outcomes (e.g., low PISA scores and high learning poverty), and substandard teaching practices undermine quality. Teacher-learner ratios range from 1:25 to 1:29, with average salaries between ₱25,000 and ₱30,000, and inadequate licensure and professional development. The pandemic deepened learning loss and mental health issues. While policy measures like teacher scholarships and allowances exist, implementation remains weak. The 2023 rollout of the *Matatag curriculum*—a simplified, Filipino-centered K–12 program set to be phased in from 2024 to 2028—aims to strengthen core competencies and national identity, but concerns remain over the dominance of American and Christian narratives and limited access for Muslim students in the Moro Province.

## **Portugal: Post-Revolution Expansion, Bologna Integration, and Systemic Pressures**

Since the 1960s, Portugal has expanded its public education system, establishing universal primary education and significantly growing secondary and higher education following the 1974 Carnation Revolution. The system includes pre-primary education, nine years of mandatory basic education divided into three cycles, and compulsory three-year secondary education beginning in 2012/2013. Students start at age six, progressing through general subjects that evolve into professional and specialized content by the 9th year, with national exams in Portuguese and Mathematics at the end of each cycle. Secondary education offers general, technological, and artistic tracks, all culminating in a diploma required for university admission.

Higher education operates under two main subsystems: universities, which emphasize theoretical and research-based studies (e.g., law, medicine, psychology), and polytechnics, which provide practical, profession-specific training in engineering, health, and technology. Both systems follow the Bologna Process, standardizing degrees into **Licenciatura** (Bachelor), **Mestrado** (Master), **Doutoramento** (Doctorate), and **Agregação** (Professor Doctor), with **Bacharelato** and **Pós-Graduação** also recognized. Admission requires a **Diploma de Ensino Secundário** and performance in the **ENES exams**, with enrollment limited by a **numerus clausus** and a preference-based ranking system. Over-23-year-olds may enter via the more selective **Extraordinary Exam Process**. Teacher education includes 4-year **Licenciatura** programs for primary teachers and 4–6-year programs for secondary educators, with a 2007 law mandating a **Master’s degree** in basic and secondary education.

Despite progress, access to higher education remains limited relative to other EU countries, and participation in the Bologna Process has faced implementation challenges due to outdated pathways. Financial pressures from the sovereign debt crisis have led to reduced university funding, rising tuition, staff reductions, and overcrowding. Private schools—especially in urban areas—are often elite and criticized for prioritizing affordability over merit and quality, contributing to educational inequities. Some polytechnic and private institutions have been accused of operating as diploma mills due to low academic standards. Rapid expansion in the 1990s–2000s raised concerns about declining academic quality and inefficient resource use. In 2008, over 85,000 teachers protested against teacher evaluation reforms. The *Novas Oportunidades* policy allowed adults with low formal education to obtain grade equivalences based on life experience, but it was criticized for lacking academic rigor and failing to improve workforce quality.

## **Malaysia: Multilingual Framework, Bilingualism, and Implementation Challenges**

Malaysia’s education system reflects its multicultural identity, with a clear hierarchy from primary through tertiary levels. At the secondary stage, students complete the Sijil Pelajaran Malaysia (SPM), followed by either Form 6 (leading to the internationally recognized STPM) or matriculation programs. Matriculation is merit-based but maintains a 90% Bumiputera quota, reflecting ethnic equity policies. National-type secondary schools (SMJK) preserve Chinese-language instruction, while Chinese independent high schools—funded by the community through UCSCAM—offer Mandarin education and the UEC, which is internationally recognized but not fully accepted by public universities. Islamic schools (e.g., SRA, SMKA, SABK, SAR) teach Islamic subjects and offer the STAM qualification, with SARs still operating in rural areas. The Ministry of Education guides the system through National Education Blueprints (2006–2010 and 2013–2025), aiming to improve access, equity, quality, and national unity, including expanding compulsory schooling to 11 years and introducing the KSSM and KSSR curricula to promote critical and higher-order thinking.

At the tertiary level, Malaysia has 20 public universities offering diverse specializations, including research, technical and vocational education (TVET), and Islamic institutions, alongside private institutions that deliver international-quality programs through partnerships with foreign universities. Many private colleges offer "twinning" programs, enabling students to study abroad and earn dual qualifications, while foreign universities have established branch campuses to deliver affordable global degrees. The system is governed by the Malaysian Qualifications Framework (MQF), with increasing emphasis on meritocracy and industry engagement—such as reduced postgraduate requirements for lecturers. Challenges remain, including gender imbalance in higher education, residual racial quotas in public universities, and ongoing tensions in language policy, balancing Malay as the national language with the preservation of Chinese and Tamil instruction through elective courses and the Dual Language Program. Since 2024, school uniforms have become more flexible, signaling a shift toward inclusivity and modernization.

The system includes public and private schools following the National Curriculum, Chinese Independent High Schools (with a six-year structure and UEC examinations), and international schools offering curricula such as Cambridge, Australian, Canadian, and IB. The Dual Language Program (DLP), launched in 2016, promotes English instruction in STEM subjects to enhance bilingualism and global competitiveness. As of 2023, it is implemented in 2,420 schools and can expand on a case-by-case basis. However, it faces challenges such as insufficient Bahasa Melayu proficiency, strict implementation rules (e.g., requiring non-DLP classes), and resource shortages—especially in under-resourced schools.

Primary education begins at age 7 and lasts six years, with no academic promotion requirements. The UPSR exam was discontinued in 2021 and replaced by UASA (Form 4–6), which assesses Malay, English, Science, and Mathematics, with optional additional subjects. Secondary education (Forms 1–5) is currently not compulsory but is planned to become so from 2024. It uses Malay as the main medium, with English compulsory. Students from non-Malay-medium schools (SJKC/SJK(T)) must achieve a minimum D grade in Malay to enter Form 1; those failing attend a transition class. A “vision school” model aims to reduce racial polarization but faces community resistance. Co-curricular activities are mandatory, with regional variations. All schools follow a unified syllabus for non-language subjects, and language instruction in Chinese, Tamil, and indigenous languages is provided upon parental request. Preschool education is optional, typically starting at age 3, and is largely dominated by private for-profit institutions, especially in urban areas.

## **Australia: A Diverse, Globally Competitive, and Inclusive System**

Australia operates a comprehensive, diverse, and internationally recognized education system. Compulsory education begins at age 5–6 and ends at 15–17, depending on the region, with a national curriculum standardized through the Australian Curriculum (since 2014). Government schools (64–65.7% of students) are publicly funded and free, while Catholic schools (19.7%) are systematically funded under Canon Law and serve around 750,000 students. Private schools (14.6% of students) include Anglican, Baptist, Lutheran, Islamic, Jewish, Montessori, Steiner, and special needs institutions, with elite schools charging up to $500,000 over 13 years. Governance is through elected school councils or boards, ensuring independence from government control. Performance is benchmarked globally via PISA, TIMSS, and PIRLS, with Australia ranking 2nd in the UN Education Index (2018)—16th in reading, 29th in maths, and 17th in science. The system emphasizes inclusivity, equity, and academic standardization without formal racial quotas.

Tertiary education dominates enrolment, with 1.18 million students in 2021 across 42 institutions (37 public, 3 private, 2 international), led by Monash University as the largest. Top universities like the University of Melbourne are globally recognized, ranking highly in QS, Times Higher Education, and ARWU. International students form a major component—accounting for 26.7% of university students in 2019, projected to reach 940,000 by 2025—mainly from China, India, and Southeast Asia. These students are regulated under the *Education Services for Overseas Students Act 2000*. Religious education varies by state, with formal instruction in NSW and WA, optional classes in Victoria, and both general and special religious education in WA. Bilingual education, initiated in the 1950s in the Northern Territory, has evolved into "two-way" programs supporting Indigenous language maintenance and immigrant communities. Despite English-only mandates, programs at schools like Yirrkala and Areyonga remain effective and are now integrated into national initiatives such as Closing the Gap.

Persistent challenges include uneven school funding (with Gonski reports recommending improved resourcing), Indigenous student disadvantage, and rising school violence—48% of principals in Queensland and South Australia reported physical violence in 2024.

## **Romania: A Structured, Reforming System with Regional Disparities**

Romania’s education system is compulsory for 15 years, beginning with a mandatory middle kindergarten (ages 3–6) and extending through the twelfth grade. Reforms in 2020 extended compulsory education to 14 years, and in 2023, the preparatory year (ages 6–7) was integrated into primary education. Most schools are public and free, though families may need to purchase textbooks or uniforms. Access to early education and primary schooling varies by location, with private options being costly and largely accessible only to higher-income families. Rural areas face significant underfunding, limited access, and high dropout rates—particularly after the eighth grade, where 16–25% of children aged 7–14 do not attend school.

Primary education (ages 6–10) is taught by a single teacher covering most subjects, with optional classes in religion, foreign languages, and arts. At ages 10–11, students transition to gymnasium (ages 11–18), where education becomes specialized and intensive, with subject-specific teachers and a class principal. Students are assessed on a 1–10 scale, with a minimum passing grade of 5. Those scoring below 5 in two or more subjects must take a special exam; below 5 in three or more must repeat the year. At ages 14–15, students take the national *Evaluarea Națională* (National Test) in Romanian and Mathematics, requiring an average of at least 5 to pass. Admission to high schools is based on test scores and preferences, with top institutions requiring scores above 9.5–9.6. Five types of high schools offer university access, though subject-specific requirements may create de facto barriers.

Secondary education culminates in the *National Baccalaureate Exam* (Bacalaureat), a centralized, high-stakes assessment comprising six to eight exams—written and oral in Romanian, a foreign language, and subject-specific exams. Exams are scored on a 1–10 scale (with two decimals for written, integer for oral), and students must achieve an average of at least 6.00 and a minimum of 5.00 in each subject to pass. A perfect score of 10.00 earns special honors, monetary awards, and eligibility for cultural festivals. Failed exams can be retaken up to five times, with a September session available for those who fail. Foreign languages are introduced from primary school, with English now the dominant first foreign language.

Student experiences vary significantly by location: urban schools offer modern facilities, extracurricular activities, and support services, while rural schools often have single teachers, limited resources, and long walking distances. A limited rural transportation service (yellow minibus) was introduced in 2003. Public schools generally do not provide lunch, though after-school meal programs are increasingly available in urban areas. Clubs in sports, drama, and science are organized by teachers and are not reflected in academic diplomas. National olympiads are widely pursued, offering benefits such as skipping school, better teacher treatment, and exam preparation; some allow students to skip the National Test. Physical education teachers organize intramural sports and school trips, while summer camps (*tabere*) remain a traditional part of the curriculum.

Higher education in Romania consists of 97 institutions—56 public and 41 private—organized into three tiers: education, research, and art. The system follows the Bologna Process with three cycles: bachelor’s (3 years), master’s (2 years), and doctoral (3 years), though medicine and engineering may extend beyond these. Admission varies by institution and field, involving exams, file competitions, essays, or interviews, with state universities maintaining rigorous selection processes. Romanian diplomas are internationally recognized, with titles such as *mr.* and *drs.* equivalent to LLM or MA/MSc degrees. FEANI grants the title *European Engineer (Eur. Ing.)* to qualified Romanian engineers. Romania offers specialized international programs through Erasmus Mundus (e.g., Community Law, European Economic and Political Integration), promoting cross-border mobility and joint degrees.

Romanian universities are recognized as among the best in Eastern Europe, particularly in medicine and technology. International student enrollment stands at 5.1% (as of 2016), and Romania is actively engaged in European higher education initiatives such as Erasmus, Socrates, Tempus, CEEPUS, and Fulbright. In 2012, four Romanian universities ranked within the QS Top 700, reflecting growing academic quality and international visibility.

Historically, universities have played a pivotal role in Romania’s democratization since 1990, with student-led movements like the University Square protests advocating for political reform and media freedom—events that influenced neighboring countries such as Bulgaria and Ukraine. A contentious issue remains religious education: since 2014, parents must opt-in for religious classes, sparking ongoing debates over confessional versus historical teaching and minors’ rights to choose. Literacy is nearly universal (0.83% illiterate), though higher rates are observed among Romani and Turkish minorities. A post-communist trend of studying abroad has grown, with around 50,000 youth studying overseas—raising concerns about brain drain.

## **Comparative Insights**

While all eight countries face challenges related to equity, access, and quality, their root causes and systemic responses differ significantly. Finland stands out for its equitable, student-centered, and holistic model, supported by strong policy coherence, teacher professionalism, and national investment in well-being. The U.S. struggles with decentralized governance, underfunded rural and low-income schools, flawed assessment practices, and deep-seated racial and socioeconomic inequities—despite high spending. South Africa grapples with the long-term legacy of apartheid, including institutionalized segregation, poor implementation of reforms, and persistent resource gaps, compounded by high dropout rates, violence, and limited digital access. The Philippines faces massive out-of-school populations, weak foundational literacy, and underfunded infrastructure, despite structural reforms like K–12 and the Matatag curriculum. Portugal has made progress in public education expansion and Bologna integration but faces financial strain, teacher shortages, and inequities in private vs. public institutions. Malaysia maintains a multilingual system promoting national identity and global competitiveness, yet implementation challenges—especially in bilingualism and equity across ethnic groups—remain. Australia demonstrates a globally competitive, inclusive, and well-resourced system, with strong performance in international benchmarks, though it faces persistent Indigenous disadvantage and regional funding disparities. Romania shows a structured, reforming system with regional disparities and high-stakes assessments, reflecting post-communist educational transformation and growing academic recognition.

Early literacy is critical in all systems: in the U.S., reading failure correlates strongly with criminal behavior; in South Africa, high dropout and violence reflect broader educational and social failures; in the Philippines, foundational skills are weak and deeply entrenched. Teacher shortages, inadequate curricula, and inequitable starting points for students remain persistent challenges. However, Finland’s model offers a blueprint for holistic, inclusive, and equitable education, while the U.S., South Africa, Philippines, Malaysia, Australia, and Romania face complex historical and structural barriers that require systemic reform, greater investment in teacher support, culturally responsive curricula, and inclusive policies to achieve lasting educational equity.
