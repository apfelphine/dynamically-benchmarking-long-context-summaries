# Global Education Systems Overview

## Finland
Finland’s education system prioritizes egalitarianism, inclusivity, and holistic development, with phenomenon-based learning strategies. Formal schooling begins at age seven and continues through 18, featuring small classrooms, free meals and materials, highly trained teachers, and robust student services. Responding to workforce needs, Finland recently introduced tuition fees for non-EEA students and expanded vocational and adult education.

## United States
The U.S. education system operates under local, state, and federal governance with funding disparities often tied to local property taxes, resulting in achievement gaps based on socioeconomic status and race. Key challenges include standardized testing, teacher shortages, high college costs, and student loan burdens. Recent policy shifts include the end of affirmative action and debates over curriculum and school safety, underscoring ongoing struggles for equity and systemic reform.

## South Africa
South Africa's education is government-funded and shaped by post-apartheid reforms aimed at expanding access and equity. While "No Fee" schools and infrastructure improvements have benefited some, significant disparities persist between urban and rural schools, exacerbated by teacher shortages, slow ICT adoption, and school safety concerns. Digital access and safety projects continue, but progress is uneven.

## Philippines
The Philippines utilizes a K–12 system with compulsory kindergarten and specialized senior high tracks, beginning instruction in local languages before shifting to Filipino and English. Challenges include underfunding, low teacher pay, corruption, learning poverty, and unequal access for marginalized communities. Recent reforms and curriculum updates strive to improve equity and educational outcomes, highlighting education’s role in national development.

## Portugal
Portugal shifted from church to state control in the 20th century, expanding compulsory schooling for ages 6 to 18 and creating general, professional, and artistic tracks. State education is free, with a growing private sector and higher education standardized for European comparability. Persistent issues include dropout rates, policy consistency, and quality assurance, particularly in private institutions.

## Malaysia
Malaysia’s education system features a broad spectrum of institutions—national, vernacular, Islamic, independent, and international schools—with compulsory primary education and expanding secondary requirements. Curriculum priorities include promoting unity through Malay language instruction, though reforms like the Dual Language Program and "vision schools" have produced mixed outcomes. Infrastructure disparities, policy debates on language and culture, quota systems favoring Bumiputeras, and recent moves to prioritize low-income applicants remain central challenges.

## Australia
Australia mandates education between ages 5/6 and 15/17 under joint federal-state oversight, with public, private, and elite Catholic schools. The national curriculum accommodates academic, arts, sports, STEM, Indigenous, and special needs tracks. Quality is monitored via assessments like NAPLAN and global benchmarks, but recent results reveal declining trends. Notable challenges include funding cuts, inequalities—especially for Indigenous students—and ongoing debates about bilingual and religious education; reforms such as the Gonski reports aim to bolster resources and equity.

## Romania
Romania mandates 15 years of schooling, starting in early childhood, with public institutions predominating. High school entry and university access depend on national exams, and the curriculum blends compulsory and elective subjects, with widespread foreign language study. Disparities in rural and urban resources drive up dropout rates, while the higher education sector, aligned with European standards, faces challenges like brain drain and ongoing debates over religious education and infrastructure.
