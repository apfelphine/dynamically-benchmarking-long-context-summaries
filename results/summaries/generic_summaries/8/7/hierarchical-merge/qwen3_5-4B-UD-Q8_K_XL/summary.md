# Master Summary: Global Education Systems

## Finnish and American Models
The Finnish system prioritizes equality, autonomy, and lifelong learning through nine years of compulsory schooling, small classes, and teacher-led assessments without high-stakes testing. Educators hold master's degrees, and the distinction between research-focused universities and practical institutions ensures free tuition for domestic and EEA students. In contrast, the U.S. system is highly stratified, driven by regional accreditation, standardized testing, and deepening inequalities caused by shrinking state funding. While U.S. graduates often earn higher wages, the nation ranks low in OECD math and science metrics, faces severe student debt exceeding $1.5 trillion, and struggles with literacy gaps linked to criminal behavior.

Currently, Finland addresses rising unemployment and funding cuts by shifting toward "phenomenon-based learning" and exporting its model globally. Conversely, the U.S. confronts critical disparities in degree attainment based on socioeconomic status and significant financial strain within its vast, deregulated post-secondary sector.

## Developing and Transforming Nations: Philippines, South Africa, and Malaysia
The Philippines operates under a 13-year K–12 framework designed to expand compulsory schooling and introduce specialized Senior High School tracks. However, the system grapples with severe resource constraints, including low budgets, insufficient classrooms, and compromised teacher quality, resulting in over 90% of students being classified as learning-poor. Similarly, South Africa's landscape is shaped by the legacy of the apartheid-era Bantu Education Act, with current reforms struggling to bridge the divide between basic and higher education amidst inadequate infrastructure and insufficient teacher digital skills.

Malaysia transitioned from a colonial era of Islamic *pondok* schools and English-language institutions to a unified national framework mandating Malay as the primary medium of instruction while preserving vernacular schools. The curriculum emphasizes bilingualism through the Dual Language Program to teach STEM subjects in English, aiming to enhance global competitiveness alongside a focus on Bahasa Melayu and English. While all three nations utilize national assessments to structure student progression, they face distinct challenges ranging from high dropout rates to infrastructure deficits in developing regions.

## Comparative Frameworks: Australia and Romania
Australia operates as a state-responsibility system with a unified national curriculum and a diverse tertiary sector that serves as a major economic engine for international students. Despite this integration, the country faces challenges regarding PISA rankings and disparities for Indigenous students. Australia is currently implementing the Gonski plan to increase federal funding and address school violence, alongside steady growth in student enrollment. In contrast, Romania maintains a 14-year compulsory public education system characterized by high enrollment but significant urban-rural disparities in infrastructure and teacher availability.

Higher education structures further differentiate these nations. Australia's tertiary sector is highly regulated and globally integrated, while Romania follows the Bologna Plan with a three-cycle degree structure (Bachelor, Master, PhD) using the ECTS credit system. Romania features autonomous universities despite ongoing funding challenges and often necessitates informal funding mechanisms to support its system.

## Assessment and Future Directions
The nations differ markedly in their assessment methods and admission policies. Malaysia relies on the SPM exam within the Malaysian Qualifications Framework, while Australia utilizes the Australian Qualifications Framework with a mix of qualitative and quantitative assessments. Romania enforces a rigorous numerical grading system (1–10) where failing subjects can mandate repeating the entire year, culminating in a centralized Baccalaureate Exam. Malaysia focuses on expanding access for minority groups like the Orang Asli and incorporating industry professionals into teaching roles, whereas Australia continues to refine its regulatory framework.

All three nations are actively working to improve accessibility and address systemic inequities. Malaysia is revising curricula to foster critical thinking and making English compulsory, while simultaneously working to reduce facility deficiencies. Australia is addressing school violence and steady growth in student enrollment, while Romania continues to bridge the gap between urban and rural institutions, aiming to improve resource distribution and retention rates across its 14-year compulsory system.
