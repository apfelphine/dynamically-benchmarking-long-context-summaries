## John L. Pollock: Academic and Philosophical Contributions
John L. Pollock (born 1940) earned a triple major in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota. His doctoral work at UC Berkeley led to his main philosophical contribution: the development of **defeasible reasoning**. His book, *Knowledge and Justification* (1974), moved beyond searching for strict criteria for knowledge, grounding epistemic norms in defeasible, or ceteris paribus, conditions that allow for exceptions.

Pollock became known as "Mr. Defeasible Reasoning." His later work involved the **OSCAR** software project, an AI prototype demonstrating his ideas on defeasible reasoning alongside concepts of intentions and problem-solving. He also deeply investigated the link between defeasible reasoning and probability in his work, *Nomic Probability and the Foundations of Induction* (1990).

## Deepfakes: Technology and Implications
Deepfake technology, which evolved from 19th-century photo manipulation, now involves creating realistic synthetic media using AI. Academic research is divided between **computer vision** (creating and detecting fakes) and **humanities/social sciences** (studying ethical, aesthetic, and informational impacts). Detection methods increasingly rely on deep-learning techniques to identify software-induced artifacts.

Deepfakes present broad risks: they can be used for entertainment, satire, or impersonation, but they also pose severe threats. Malicious applications include **fraud and scams** (estimated at billions globally), political misinformation campaigns, and non-consensual pornography. Countermeasures range from advanced detection algorithms (analyzing eye reflections or spatio-temporal inconsistencies) to legal frameworks like the EU AI Act and national legislation.

## Regulation of AI in the United States
U.S. federal regulation of AI has evolved from early, permissive guidance to more stringent measures. Initial efforts focused on risk assessment, culminating in the **AI Bill of Rights** (2022) outlining five key protections. More recently, the Biden administration issued an Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence (2023), which mandates security testing and calls for watermarking AI-generated content.

State and local interventions are also occurring; for example, the Tennessee ELVIS Act specifically targets audio deepfakes. These efforts are balanced against concerns over innovation speed, with some legislative proposals aiming to accelerate AI development while others focus on mitigating risks like job displacement and algorithmic bias.

## Ethics of Artificial Intelligence
The ethics of AI spans several domains: **Machine Ethics** (designing morally acting agents) and **Robot Ethics** (how humans design and treat robots). Debates over "robot rights" question whether moral consideration should rely on observable capacities or precautionary principles.

Concerns regarding AI include **algorithmic biases** (seen in facial and voice recognition systems against darker-skinned or minority individuals), **societal impact** (such as the concentration of power among Big Tech giants and environmental strain from data centers), and **existential risks** (from uncontrollable superintelligence or weaponization). Ethical guidelines often emphasize transparency, fairness, and non-maleficence.

## Concepts of Technological Change
Discourse around rapid technological advancement includes the **Singularity**, a hypothetical event where AI surpasses human intelligence. Key figures like I.J. Good and Vernor Vinge popularized the concept of an "intelligence explosion." While predictions vary widely (e.g., 2045 by Ray Kurzweil), critics question its plausibility due to technological and systemic limitations.

Related to this is **Transhumanism**, which advocates using technology (nanotechnology, genetic engineering, AI) to overcome human limitations. This movement has various currents, including **Singularitarianism** (advocating for the singularity) and **Abolitionism** (seeking to eradicate suffering). Furthermore, **Accelerationism** views technological progress—particularly capitalism's self-improvement—as a force to be hastened, with factions ranging from right-wing to left-wing accelerationism.

## General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
The GDPR (2016) established a comprehensive regulatory regime for personal data processing within the EU. It mandates principles like **lawful, fair, and transparent processing**, alongside data minimization and storage limitation. Key rights granted to data subjects include the **right to access** their data, the **right to data portability**, and a limited **right of erasure**.

Compliance requires stringent measures, including **data protection by design and by default**, and the appointment of a Data Protection Officer (DPO) for large-scale operations. Penalties for infringement can be severe (up to 4% of global turnover), and the regulation has driven global standards via the "Brussels effect," influencing laws in countries like California and China.
