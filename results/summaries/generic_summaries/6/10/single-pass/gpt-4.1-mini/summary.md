# Kenneth E. Iverson  
Ken Iverson (1920–2004) was a Canadian mathematician and computer scientist, best known for developing the APL programming language and its successor, J. Educated at Queen's University and Harvard, he worked at Harvard, IBM, I.P. Sharp Associates, and Jsoftware Inc. At Harvard, Iverson pioneered notation suited for data processing, leading to APL's creation. At IBM, he co-developed APL\360, formalizing the language and advancing its implementation and teaching. Later, at I.P. Sharp Associates and Jsoftware, he extended APL concepts and created J, emphasizing a simpler, shareware-available language using ASCII characters and natural-language terminology. Iverson’s work earned him the Turing Award (1979) and IBM Fellow recognition (1970).  

# George Boole  
Born in 1815 in England, George Boole was largely self-taught and became the first professor of mathematics at Queen’s College, Cork. He pioneered symbolic logic and created Boolean algebra, systematizing Aristotelian logic and extending it into a mathematical framework foundational to modern computing. Boole’s work addressed probability, differential equations, and logic, influencing 19th- and 20th-century mathematics and computer science. He died in 1864, leaving a legacy commemorated by numerous honors, and his algebraic logic underpins digital circuit design and information theory.  

# Jacek Karpiński  
Polish engineer Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a pioneer in early computing, building transistor-based analyzers (AKAT-1), a perceptron neural network, and advanced minicomputers such as KAR-65 and the innovative K-202. Despite technical excellence, political and bureaucratic obstacles in communist Poland hindered mass production and recognition of his work. Karpiński also served in WWII resistance, suffered injuries, and later worked internationally. Post-retirement, he advised on computing developments. Controversies include his cooperation with Polish intelligence and debates over the scope of his contributions.  

# Ramon Llull  
Catalan philosopher Ramon Llull (1232/3–1315/6) developed a combinatorial universal logic system, the Art, designed to prove theological truths and convert non-Christians. His Art used letters and diagrams in algorithmic ways, evolving through Quaternary and Ternary phases, incorporating a threefold ontology (agent, patient, act). Llull promoted linguistic education for missionaries and authored dialogs, tree diagrams, proverbs, and novels to popularize his method. Though opposed by medieval theologians, his ideas influenced Renaissance thinkers and early computation theory. Venerated as beatified in 1847, his works remain significant in logic and cultural history.  

# Federico Faggin  
Italian physicist and engineer Federico Faggin (born 1941) pioneered silicon-gate technology, enabling the first commercial MOS integrated circuits and leading Intel’s development of the 4004, the world’s first single-chip microprocessor. After early work at Olivetti and Fairchild, he managed Intel’s R&D and designed subsequent influential microprocessors (8008, 4040, 8080) and co-founded Zilog, designing the Z80 CPU widely used in early personal computers. Later, Faggin co-founded Synaptics and Foveon, innovating neural networks, touch interfaces, and imaging sensors. He also proposed a quantum theory of consciousness, linking it to quantum physics principles. Faggin holds numerous awards recognizing his microelectronics and computer industry contributions.  

# George Antheil  
American avant-garde composer George Antheil (1900–1959) was known for radical works like Ballet Mécanique, featuring mechanistic rhythms and innovative instrumentation involving player pianos and airplane propellers. Raised near industrial noise, Antheil embraced futurism and modernism in Europe and was connected to figures like Stravinsky and Ezra Pound. Later, he composed film scores in Hollywood. During WWII, he co-invented a frequency-hopping spread spectrum communication system with actress Hedy Lamarr, a precursor to modern wireless technologies. Antheil’s legacy blends experimental music, technology, and early communication innovations.  

# Grace Hopper  
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was a pioneering American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral who developed the first compiler and played a major role in creating the COBOL programming language, making programming accessible via English-like syntax. Beginning her career at Harvard on the Mark I computer, Hopper advanced automatic programming and programming language standardization, especially for business applications. Known for popularizing the term "debugging," her long Navy service included multiple retirements and recalls, culminating in her promotion to rear admiral. Post-retirement, she was a consultant and speaker, earning numerous honors including the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016.  

# Geoffrey Hinton  
Born in 1947, Geoffrey Hinton is a leading figure in artificial intelligence research, especially neural networks and deep learning. After a diverse education in psychology and AI, he contributed seminal theories like backpropagation, Boltzmann machines, and capsule networks. Hinton co-founded DNNresearch, later acquired by Google, where he worked until publicly resigning in 2023 to speak on AI risks. His research has driven advances in machine learning, perception, and representation with over 200 publications. Hinton won the 2018 Turing Award with Bengio and LeCun for deep learning breakthroughs, and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for foundational AI discoveries. He has expressed concerns over AI’s potential existential risks.  

# Gottlob Frege  
German logician and philosopher Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) is considered a founder of modern logic and analytic philosophy. His Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced axiomatic predicate logic with quantified variables, solving problems in Aristotelian logic and laying groundwork for later works in mathematics and philosophy. Frege aimed to derive arithmetic from logic (logicism) but his system was challenged by Russell’s paradox. Philosophically, Frege distinguished sense and reference in language, influencing philosophy of language and logic. Despite his mathematical genius, late in life he held extreme right-wing and antisemitic views.  

# Ismail al-Jazari  
Medieval Muslim engineer and inventor Ismail al-Jazari (1136–1206) authored The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices, describing automata, water clocks, pumps, and early mechanical innovations such as the camshaft, crankshaft with connecting rod, escapement mechanisms, and complex water-raising machines. His devices combined artistry with engineering, featuring humanoid automata and programmable musical robots. Al-Jazari contributed foundational mechanical design methods and has been credited as a pioneer of robotics and industrial engineering. His inventions influenced later European engineering and combine practical application with aesthetic sophistication.  

# Al-Khwarizmi  
9th-century Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi is considered the father of algebra. His works introduced systematic methods for solving linear and quadratic equations, with “algebra” deriving from his book Al-Jabr. He helped disseminate Hindu-Arabic numerals and algorithms to the Islamic world and Europe, profoundly impacting mathematics. He also contributed to astronomy, geography, and trigonometry, refining Ptolemy’s data and generating sine and cosine tables. His legacy includes foundational concepts in mathematics, computational procedures, and the etymology of the word algorithm.  

# Kurt Gödel  
Austrian-American logician Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) revolutionized mathematical logic through his incompleteness theorems, proving that axiomatic systems powerful enough to describe arithmetic cannot be both complete and consistent. His work ended the quest for a complete foundation of mathematics and deeply influenced logic, philosophy, and computer science. Gödel also contributed to set theory, demonstrated the relative consistency of the axiom of choice, and explored solutions in general relativity. Despite periods of mental illness, he maintained a lifelong close friendship with Albert Einstein.  

# Per Brinch Hansen  
Danish computer scientist Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a pioneer in operating systems, concurrent programming, and parallel and distributed computing. He developed the RC 4000 multiprogramming system introducing the kernel and separation of mechanism and policy, advanced the monitor concept for concurrency, and created the first concurrent programming language, Concurrent Pascal. His work laid foundations for modern OS design and concurrency theory, including remote procedure calls. As an educator and author of foundational textbooks, he significantly influenced computing research and practices.  

# Lynn Conway  
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was an electrical engineer and computer scientist who revolutionized VLSI chip design through innovative methodologies jointly with Carver Mead. After beginning her career at IBM and pioneering superscalar architecture, she transitioned gender in 1968, after which IBM fired her but later apologized. At Xerox PARC, she developed the multiproject wafer (MPW) system, and co-authored the seminal textbook "Introduction to VLSI Systems," which catalyzed the VLSI design revolution. She later contributed to DARPA’s Strategic Computing Initiative, advocated for transgender rights, and earned numerous honors for her technical and social contributions.  

# Herbert A. Simon  
American polymath Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) made foundational contributions to economics, psychology, artificial intelligence, and decision theory. His theory of bounded rationality challenged classical economics by recognizing cognitive limitations in decision making. Simon pioneered AI with programs like Logic Theory Machine and General Problem Solver, and studied human problem solving, organization theory, and emotional cognition. He authored seminal books including "Administrative Behavior" and received the Nobel Prize in Economics (1978) and the Turing Award (1975).  

# Donald Knuth  
Donald Knuth (born 1938) is a prominent computer scientist known for his multivolume work *The Art of Computer Programming*, which systematized algorithm analysis. Educated in physics and mathematics, he pioneered algorithmics, compiler construction, and digital typesetting (TeX). Knuth developed literate programming and contributed to recreational mathematics. His writing style and humor are notable; he offers monetary rewards for errors found in his books. Knuth has received numerous awards including the Turing Award and National Medal of Science, and remains an influential figure in computer science education and research.  

# Edsger W. Dijkstra  
Dutch computer scientist Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a foundational figure in programming theory and operating systems. He introduced structured programming, famously critiquing the "goto" statement, and developed algorithms such as Dijkstra’s shortest path. His work on semaphores, monitors, and THE operating system shaped modern software design. Known for his rigorous and terse writing and lectures, Dijkstra prioritized clarity and correctness. He worked at the Mathematisch Centrum, Eindhoven, Burroughs Corporation, and University of Texas at Austin.  

# Claude Shannon  
Claude Shannon (1916–2001), known as the "father of information theory," developed the mathematical framework for communication systems, introducing concepts such as information entropy and the Shannon limit. His 1937 master’s thesis applied Boolean algebra to switching circuits, foundational for digital circuit design. Shannon also contributed to cryptography, signal processing, and early AI with projects like the mechanical mouse Theseus. His work underpins the digital revolution, influencing computing, telecommunications, and cryptography. Shannon received many honors, and his legacy is commemorated worldwide.
