## Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004)  
Canadian mathematician and computer scientist who developed the APL programming language at Harvard. At IBM, he promoted APL in education and research, authored foundational texts, and later developed the J programming language with innovations like the "fork" operator. Founded Jsoftware and emphasized expressive notation and operator generalization. Awarded the Turing Award and is honored with a moth species named after him.

## George Boole (1815–1864)  
British logician who founded Boolean algebra, systematizing Aristotelian logic through algebraic methods. His work underpins modern digital computing and the Boolean data type. Influenced by Shannon’s application in circuit design.

## Jacek Karpiński (1927–?)  
Polish engineer and resistance member who developed pioneering computing devices (AAH, AKAT-1, KAR-65) and the K-202 minicomputer. His work was undermined by political resistance, and he served as a secret informant for Polish intelligence. Legacy remains controversial.

## Ramon Llull (1232–1316)  
Catalan philosopher and inventor whose combinatorial *Art* system influenced later thinkers like Leibniz. His *Ars Magna* is recognized as a precursor to computation theory. His ideas on logic and electoral systems have modern resonance in AI and voting theory.

## Federico Faggin (1925–2015)  
Italian physicist and computer engineer who pioneered silicon-gate MOS technology and designed the first commercial microprocessor (Intel 4004). Key figure in microprocessor development and co-founder of Zilog and Synaptics. Also proposed a quantum theory of consciousness.

## George Antheil (1888–1949)  
American composer known for *Ballet Mécanique* and co-inventor of frequency-hopping radio with Hedy Lamarr, a foundational concept in modern wireless communication.

## Grace Hopper (1906–1992)  
Pioneering computer scientist who developed COBOL and popularized "debugging." A trailblazer for women in computing, with lasting legacy in education, military tech, and global recognition through awards, institutions, and the Grace Hopper Celebration.

## Geoffrey Hinton (1947–)  
Foundational AI researcher who co-invented backpropagation and deep learning. Co-developed t-SNE and capsule networks. Now a vocal critic of AI risks, advocating for regulation, universal basic income, and ethical development. Shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield.

## Gottlob Frege (1848–1925)  
German logician who founded modern logic and introduced *sense and reference*. His work is central to analytic philosophy and the philosophy of language. Notably, his personal writings reveal antisemitic views, which contrast with his intellectual legacy.

## Ismail al-Jazari (c. 1136–1206)  
Islamic engineer whose mechanical inventions—including the crank-slider mechanism and water-raising machines—prefigure modern engineering. His automata and clocks demonstrate early principles of programmable systems and human-machine interaction.

## Al-Khwarizmi (c. 820–850)  
Persian mathematician who introduced algebra and the Hindu-Arabic numeral system. His work laid the foundation for modern mathematics and computing. The term "algorithm" derives from his name.

## Kurt Gödel (1906–1978)  
Logician who proved the incompleteness theorems, demonstrating inherent limits in formal systems. His work on the constructible universe and time-travel solutions in general relativity remains influential. Also proposed an ontological proof of God.

## Per Brinch Hansen (1930–?)  
Danish computer scientist who co-developed the monitor concept with Dijkstra and Hoare. Founded USC’s computer science department and developed Concurrent Pascal and the Solo operating system. His work on concurrency and distributed systems is foundational. Received the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award.

## Lynn Conway (1938–2024)  
Pioneering computer engineer who co-authored *Introduction to VLSI Systems* and founded MOSIS, enabling access to semiconductor fabrication. A transgender rights activist, she coined the "Conway effect" and advocated for inclusion in science and technology.

## Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001)  
Interdisciplinary scientist known for *bounded rationality* and *satisficing*. Co-developed the EPAM and CHREST models of cognition. Authored *Human Problem Solving* and *The Sciences of the Artificial*. Won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics and the Turing Award. Also a pianist and mountain climber.

## Donald Knuth (1938–)  
Computer scientist and mathematician best known for *The Art of Computer Programming* and the development of the TeX typesetting system. Authored influential works including *Concrete Mathematics* and *Surreal Numbers*, and wrote *Mathematical Writing*. Pioneered literate programming and the CWEB system. Known for his rigorous approach to algorithms, discrete mathematics, and computer languages. A member of several academies, he has received prestigious awards including the Turing Award, National Medal of Science, and Kyoto Prize. Holds a Chinese name, Gao Dena, adopted in 1977 to connect with Chinese programmers. Has held academic positions at Stanford, Oxford, and MIT. Advocates for clear, story-based communication in computer science and opposes trivial software patents. Introduced a satirical "iTeX" project at TUG 2010.

## Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002)  
Pioneering computer scientist known for structured programming, algorithm design, and formal methods. His influential 1968 letter "Go To Statement Considered Harmful" promoted structured programming, which became standard in modern computing. Developed Dijkstra's algorithm for shortest pathfinding, the Banker's algorithm for resource allocation, the semaphore for process coordination, and introduced self-stabilization in distributed systems. Known for his wit, sharp critiques, and fictional "Mathematics, Inc." as a satirical commentary on mathematical research. Received the Turing Award in 1972 and numerous honors, including the Dijkstra Prize in Distributed Computing. His selected writings, including essays and technical reports, remain foundational in computer science education and research. A modest, thoughtful man, he valued intellectual rigor, self-reliance, and principled thinking. Died in 2002 in Nuenen, Netherlands.

## Claude Shannon (1916–2001)  
American mathematician and electrical engineer whose work laid the foundation for modern digital computing and information theory. Early influenced by Thomas Edison and Boolean algebra, he earned degrees from the University of Michigan and MIT. His 1937 master's thesis on relay and switching circuits introduced Boolean logic to engineering, revolutionizing digital circuit design. During World War II, he contributed to fire-control systems and cryptography, co-authoring key papers on data smoothing and developing the mathematical theory of cryptography, including the proof that the one-time pad is unbreakable. In 1948, he published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," introducing information entropy and establishing information theory as a field. His work on sampling theory and noise-resistant coding also had lasting impacts on telecommunications and data transmission. Widely regarded as the father of information theory and a foundational figure in computer science and communications.  
**Expanded contributions:** Co-developed pulse-code modulation (PCM), designed early AI systems such as "Theseus," a learning machine demonstrating trial-and-error behavior that inspired AI research. Estimated the complexity of chess (the "Shannon number") and proposed algorithms for computer play. Co-organized the 1956 Dartmouth Workshop, a foundational event in AI history, and co-edited *Automata Studies*. Invented devices such as the Rubik’s Cube solver, flame-throwing trumpets, and wearable computers. Recognized as a polymath, his legacy endures in telecommunications, AI, and computing, with tributes including a cryptocurrency unit (shannon), the AI model family "Claude," and statues. Celebrated as one of the most influential scientists of the 20th century, on par with Einstein and Newton.  
**Additional details from Chunk 43:** Developed a minimax algorithm with an evaluation function for chess, formulated "Shannon's maxim" ("The enemy knows the system"), and contributed to combinatorics and the matched filter principle. A successful investor, he pioneered early stock market analysis and portfolio strategies, including "Shannon's demon." In 2016, the Shannon centenary celebrated his legacy with global events, including a conference at Bell Labs, a web exhibit, commemorative stamps, and a documentary. He received numerous awards, including the National Medal of Science and the Kyoto Prize, and is honored through the Claude E. Shannon Award. His key works include his 1937 master's thesis and the seminal 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication."
