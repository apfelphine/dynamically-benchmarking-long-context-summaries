# Foundations of Computing, Logic, and Science

The theoretical roots of modern computation and logic are deeply intertwined. Early concepts were established by Ramon Llull and the work of Al-Khwarizmi, who popularized Hindu–Arabic numerals and gave rise to the term "algorithm." Formal logic was advanced by Gottlob Frege and George Boole, whose binary principles were later applied to electrical circuits by Claude Shannon, who also developed Information Theory. Further theoretical limits were established by Kurt Gödel with his Incompleteness Theorems.

Pioneering figures in hardware and programming include Grace Hopper, who developed early compilers, and Federico Faggin, who invented Silicon Gate Technology (SGT), enabling the first single-chip microprocessor (4004). Donald Knuth advanced the field through "algorithmics," while Edsger W. Dijkstra advocated for structured programming.

# Artificial Intelligence and Cross-Disciplinary Innovations

Foundational work in Artificial Intelligence (AI) includes Herbert A. Simon, who introduced "bounded rationality" and contributed to the General Problem Solver (GPS). Later, Geoffrey Hinton became a leading pioneer known for backpropagation and has since voiced concerns regarding existential risks from advanced AI. John von Neumann's influence and Simon's work linked AI with fields like psychology and economics.

Innovations often spanned disciplines. Geoffrey Hinton and Hedy Lamarr collaborated on frequency-hopping radio technology. Kenneth E. Iverson significantly contributed to automatic data processing, formalizing the IBM System/360 and developing the APL and J languages. Additionally, early computer scientists like Jacek Karpiński made notable advancements, such as the world's first transistor-based differential equations analyzer.
