# Claude Shannon  
Claude Shannon, born in 1916 in Michigan, was a pioneering mathematician and electrical engineer known as the “father of information theory.” During his education at the University of Michigan and MIT, he applied Boolean algebra to switching circuits, revolutionizing digital circuit design and laying the groundwork for all modern digital computers. His 1948 seminal paper, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, established information theory by introducing concepts such as information entropy. He worked extensively during WWII on cryptography and fire-control systems, proving the one-time pad's unbreakability and linking cryptography with communication theory. Shannon also contributed to artificial intelligence by creating a learning machine “Theseus” and was part of the founding Dartmouth workshop in 1956. After teaching at MIT, he retired in 1978 and passed away in 2001. Beyond academia, he was an inventor and hobbyist, known for juggling, unicycling, and building playful machines. He left a profound legacy honored by numerous awards, statues, and recognitions, and his work shaped the digital revolution and modern communication.  

# Edsger W. Dijkstra  
Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002), a Dutch computer scientist, was instrumental in establishing programming as a rigorous scientific discipline. Initially trained in physics and mathematics, he became the first professional programmer in the Netherlands. Dijkstra worked at the Mathematisch Centrum, developing the shortest path algorithm (Dijkstra’s algorithm) and one of the earliest ALGOL 60 compilers, contributing significantly to structured programming and operating system design (notably the THE operating system). He was known for his clear, precise writing style and strong opinions about software engineering, rejecting the use of GOTO statements and emphasizing program correctness. He avoided computers for his own writing and preferred handwritten manuscripts circulated as “EWDs.” Later he worked at the University of Texas at Austin and Burroughs Corporation. Dijkstra was revered for his intellectual rigor, pedagogy, and influence on programming fundamentals, earning multiple prestigious awards including the Turing Award in 1972.  

# Donald Knuth  
Donald Knuth is an American computer scientist born in 1938, renowned for his comprehensive multi-volume work *The Art of Computer Programming*, which laid the foundations for algorithm analysis and computer programming principles. Educated at Case Institute of Technology and Caltech, Knuth contributed to compiler design and early programming languages, rejecting a high-paying career in favor of deep academic work. He developed the TeX typesetting system to produce high-quality technical documents and popularized the concept of literate programming. Knuth’s contributions span theory, practice, and education, with a meticulous approach to writing and teaching. He has received numerous awards including the Turing Award and National Medal of Science, and is also known for his humor and interest in religion and music.  

# Herbert A. Simon  
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was a polymath whose work spanned economics, psychology, artificial intelligence, and decision theory. Born in Milwaukee, he earned a PhD in political science and made foundational contributions to organizational decision-making and bounded rationality, arguing that humans make satisficing rather than optimal decisions due to cognitive limitations. Simon co-developed early AI programs like the Logic Theory Machine and the General Problem Solver, and made significant contributions to cognitive psychology and computational models of expertise. His interdisciplinary work earned him the Turing Award (1975), Nobel Prize in Economics (1978), and National Medal of Science (1986). He held faculty positions at Carnegie Mellon University and influenced fields ranging from economics to AI and education.  

# Lynn Conway  
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and transgender rights activist. She contributed fundamentally to computer architecture and VLSI design, co-leading the development of superscalar dynamic instruction scheduling at IBM and later revolutionizing chip design methodology with Carver Mead at Xerox PARC. Their Mead–Conway VLSI design introduced scalable design rules and rapid prototyping infrastructure (MOSIS), transforming computer chip industry and education worldwide. After completing her gender transition, Conway restarted her career, facing discrimination but ultimately becoming a prominent figure at Xerox PARC, DARPA, and University of Michigan. She advocated for transgender rights and inclusion, influencing both computer science and social justice.  

# Per Brinch Hansen  
Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a Danish-American computer scientist vital to operating systems and concurrent programming. He developed the RC 4000 multiprogramming system introducing the kernel and separation of mechanism and policy. He pioneered monitors for concurrency and created the first concurrent programming language, Concurrent Pascal. He authored foundational textbooks on operating systems and concurrent programming and influenced the development of distributed computing. His work on structured multiprogramming and remote procedure calls laid groundwork for modern operating systems. Hansen held faculty positions in Denmark and the US and was recognized with the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award.  

# Kurt Gödel  
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978), born in Austria-Hungary, was a preeminent logician and mathematician. He proved the incompleteness theorems, showing that any sufficiently powerful formal axiomatic system cannot be both complete and consistent, reshaping mathematical logic and foundations. Gödel also made advances in set theory, proving the consistency of the axiom of choice and continuum hypothesis relative to ZF set theory, introduced the constructible universe, and contributed to relativity with solutions featuring closed timelike curves. He emigrated to the US fleeing Nazi annexation, became an IAS professor, and formed a close friendship with Einstein. Gödel suffered mental health issues later in life and died in 1978.  

# Al-Khwarizmi  
Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī (c.780–c.850) was a Persian mathematician, astronomer, and geographer. Often called the “father of algebra,” his seminal work *Al-Jabr* systematically solved linear and quadratic equations, coining the term algebra. He introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals and algorithms to the Western world, profoundly impacting mathematics. Al-Khwarizmi also produced influential astronomical tables and improved geographic knowledge by correcting Ptolemy’s data. His practical approach combined earlier Greek, Indian, and Babylonian knowledge, significantly shaping medieval science and the scientific Renaissance in Europe.  

# Ismail al-Jazari  
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–c.1206) was a Muslim polymath engineer and inventor from Upper Mesopotamia, renowned for his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*. He advanced mechanical engineering by inventing early crankshafts, camshafts, escapement mechanisms, and complex water-raising devices powered by hydropower. Known for sophisticated automata including humanoid figures with practical functions (e.g., hand-washing automaton) and musical robot bands, al-Jazari’s work influenced both Islamic and later European technological development. His water clocks, including the elephant and castle clocks, were milestones in timekeeping technology.  

# Gottlob Frege  
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German logician, mathematician, and philosopher who founded modern predicate logic and analytic philosophy. He introduced quantified variables and formalized logic beyond Aristotelian syllogism, enabling rigorous foundations of mathematics. His major works include *Begriffsschrift* and *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik*. Frege distinguished sense and reference in language, influencing philosophy of language deeply. Though his system was undermined by Russell’s paradox, his ideas laid the groundwork for 20th-century logic, mathematics, and analytic philosophy. He held academic positions in Jena and was noted for an introverted personality and controversial later antisemitic views.  

# Geoffrey Hinton  
Geoffrey Hinton (b. 1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist known as a pioneer of deep learning and neural networks. Educated at Cambridge and Edinburgh, he contributed foundational work in neural network architectures, backpropagation, Boltzmann machines, and capsule networks. His research at University of Toronto and Google led to breakthroughs in machine learning, speech recognition, and AI. Hinton co-founded DNNresearch Inc., which was acquired by Google in 2013. He received the 2018 Turing Award with Yann LeCun and Yoshua Bengio and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for fundamental contributions to AI. Recently, he has expressed concerns about AI risks and advocated for AI safety.  

# Grace Hopper  
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was an American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral, a pioneer of computer programming. Educated at Vassar and Yale, she contributed to early computers during WWII (Mark I). Hopper invented one of the first compilers, promoted machine-independent programming languages, and was instrumental in developing COBOL, a high-level business language. She championed standards in programming languages and was a colorful speaker known for popularizing the term “debugging.” Hopper served in the Navy for over 40 years, retiring as a rear admiral, and was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom posthumously.  

# George Antheil  
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer and inventor known for his mechanistic musical style and the co-invention of a frequency-hopping spread spectrum system with Hedy Lamarr for secure radio communications. He studied piano and composition in the US and Europe, becoming noted for disruptive, percussive works such as *Ballet Mécanique*. Antheil worked in Hollywood film scoring later in life. His frequency-hopping patent was an early form of communication encryption technique influential for modern wireless technology.  

# Federico Faggin  
Federico Faggin (b. 1941) is an Italian-American physicist and inventor, best known for designing the first commercial microprocessor, the Intel 4004, and pioneering silicon-gate MOS technology. Educated in physics in Italy, he led developments at SGS-Fairchild and later at Intel, creating transistor-level innovations and design methodologies enabling modern microprocessors. After founding Zilog and designing the Z80 CPU, he contributed to innovation in touchpads and image sensors at Synaptics and Foveon. Faggin also founded a foundation supporting research on consciousness and proposed quantum-based theories thereof. He holds many patents and received numerous honors including the National Medal of Technology.  

# Ramon Llull  
Ramon Llull (c.1232–1316) was a Majorcan philosopher, logician, and writer notable for developing the *Art*, a combinatorial logical system intended to prove theological and philosophical truths and aid in missionary work. Born into Catalan nobility, he experienced religious visions prompting him to abandon worldly life for study and conversion of Muslims using his logical method. Llull wrote extensively, composing dialogues, treatises, and literary works, aiming to unify knowledge and promote interreligious dialogue. Though rejected in his time by academic theologians, his work influenced later thinkers including Leibniz and contributed to early computational ideas. He was beatified in 1847.  

# Jacek Karpiński  
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and inventor, notable for building early transistor-based computers and analog differential equation analyzers. Despite hardships during WWII, including severe injury and resistance involvement, he accelerated his education and contributed to pioneering projects such as the transistorized AKAT-1 and the KAR-65 digital computer. He developed one of the first perceptrons and designed the innovative K-202 minicomputer, featuring advanced architecture and performance but limited production due to political and institutional opposition. Karpiński worked in both Poland and Switzerland, and his legacy is marked by technical excellence and controversies related to political circumstances.  

# George Boole  
George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician, founder of Boolean algebra which underpins modern digital logic and computer science. Largely self-taught, he published pioneering work on symbolic logic and its algebraic formalization, linking logic with mathematics. His *Laws of Thought* set the framework for mathematical logic, using variables and operations like AND, OR, and NOT, foundational to electronics and computer circuits. Although his work was underrecognized during his lifetime, later scholars and technologists, notably Claude Shannon, applied Boolean logic to digital circuit design, impacting the development of the Information Age. Boole had religious and philosophical interests, with his wife Mary Everest Boole promoting his legacy.  

# Kenneth E. Iverson  
Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004) was a Canadian computer scientist and mathematician, best known for creating the APL programming language and notation. He developed a concise and expressive notation system for array processing and data manipulation, which greatly influenced programming and mathematical notation. Iverson worked at Harvard, IBM, and I.P. Sharp Associates, contributing to formal system descriptions, language design, and education. Later he developed the J programming language, extending APL’s concepts. Iverson received the Turing Award in 1979 for his contributions to programming language design and work on formal descriptions of computing systems.
