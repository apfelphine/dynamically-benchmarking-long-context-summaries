**Foundational Figures in Computing and Theory:** Computing's foundations rest on figures such as George Boole (Boolean algebra), Claude Shannon (applying Boolean logic to electronics), and ancient contributors like Al-Khwārizmī and Ismail al-Jazari. Mathematical logic was advanced by Kurt Gödel and Gottlob Frege, alongside Ramon Llull's "Llull's Art." Modern theorists include Herbert A. Simon, Donald Knuth, and Edsger W. Dijkstra. Notably, Jacek Karpiński developed a Polish perceptron in 1964 and the KAR-65 in 1968.

**Pioneers in Hardware, Software, and Design:** Hardware and software innovation involved Grace Hopper (pioneering compilers and COBOL) and Lynn Conway (VLSI design). Per Brinch Hansen championed OS simplicity, while Federico Faggin was crucial in developing MOS metal-gate and Silicon-Gate Technology (SGT) at Fairchild, leading to devices like the Intel 4004 and Zilog Z80. Hedy Lamarr and George Antheil also co-developed frequency-hopping technology in 1942.

**AI, Computation, and Notation Systems (Kenneth E. Iverson):** Kenneth E. Iverson was instrumental in computing; he helped establish the first graduate program in "automatic data processing" at Harvard and created novel mathematical notation for data processing. This notation evolved into APL, a widely used language, earning him the 1979 Turing Award. Iverson worked on APL at IBM from 1960 to 1980, and later developed "Dictionary APL" at I. P. Sharp Associates (1980–1987). Post-retirement, his work led to Jsoftware (1990–2004), an APL dialect featuring natural language terms and 7-bit ASCII. In 1990, Iverson founded Iverson Software Inc., which became Jsoftware Inc., providing the J programming language under GPL3. Throughout his career, he received numerous honors, including the IBM Fellow designation (1970) and membership in the National Academy of Engineering (1979).
