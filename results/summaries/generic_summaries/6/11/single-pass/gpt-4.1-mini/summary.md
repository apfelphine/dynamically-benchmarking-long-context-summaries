# George Boole
George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician who laid the foundations of symbolic logic and Boolean algebra. Self-taught in mathematics and languages, he began teaching at a young age and established his own schools before publishing influential works on symbolic logic and algebra. Boole was appointed the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork, Ireland, where he continued his research and teaching until his death from pneumonia.

Boole's major contributions include the development of Boolean algebra, which formalized logic in mathematical terms, and influential treatises on differential equations and the calculus of finite differences. His work earned him multiple honors including fellowship at the Royal Society and several awards. His legacy endures through the widespread application of Boolean logic in computer science and digital circuit design, and he is commemorated by institutions and places bearing his name.

# Ramon Llull
Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1315) was a medieval Majorcan philosopher, logician, and missionary who developed the "Art," a combinatorial system of logic aimed at proving truths about God and facilitating interfaith dialogue. Inspired by visions, he dedicated his life to converting Muslims and schismatic Christians through education and logical debate, traveling widely in Europe and North Africa.

Llull's Art used diagrams and algebraic methods to explore theological and philosophical questions. Despite opposition and limited academic acceptance in his time, his works influenced Renaissance metaphysics and modern cognitive systems. He was beatified in 1847 and is recognized as a precursor to computational logic and electoral theory.

# Kenneth E. Iverson
Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004) was a Canadian computer scientist and innovator of programming notation that evolved into the programming language APL and later J. After education disrupted by war and self-study, he earned degrees from Queen’s University and Harvard, then began pioneering work on notation for automatic data processing and computer programming.

At IBM, Iverson formalized APL, co-developed its implementation, and used it in teaching and industrial applications. Later, at I.P. Sharp Associates and his own company, he extended the language's capabilities, influencing computer science education and software design. Iverson’s work earned him numerous awards including the Turing Award in 1979.

# George Antheil
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer known for his radical, machine-like musical style. Raised near industrial environments, he pursued music passionately despite a lack of formal success early on and achieved notoriety in Europe among modernists.

His most famous work, *Ballet Mécanique*, was a groundbreaking composition originally intended to accompany a film, notable for its use of player pianos and dissonant rhythms. In later years, Antheil worked in Hollywood composing film scores and invented frequency-hopping spread spectrum radio technology with actress Hedy Lamarr during WWII, a key foundation for modern wireless communication. He died of a heart attack in New York.

# Jacek Karpiński
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and pioneer notable for developing early transistor-based differential analyzers and the K-202 minicomputer with advanced features such as memory segmentation and high operation speed during the 1960s and 70s. His career was shaped by wartime resistance activities and serious injuries, followed by postwar engineering breakthroughs in Poland.

Despite international recognition, his later projects faced political obstacles and limited production. Karpiński also worked on early neural network devices and scanners. He spent late years in business and advisory roles, with controversies over his cooperation with state intelligence agencies. He is remembered as an innovative but politically challenged figure in Eastern European computing history.

# Geoffrey Hinton
Geoffrey Hinton (b. 1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist renowned for foundational work in artificial neural networks and deep learning. Educated in experimental psychology, he contributed key advances including backpropagation, Boltzmann machines, and capsule networks, shaping modern AI and machine learning. He held academic posts at University College London and University of Toronto, co-founded DNNresearch acquired by Google, and led major research programs.

Hinton received numerous awards including the 2018 Turing Award and 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics. Recently, he has expressed concerns about AI’s rapid progress and existential risks, resigning from Google in 2023 to speak freely about AI dangers. He is also notable for mentoring leading AI researchers and advancing both theoretical and practical AI methods.

# Federico Faggin
Federico Faggin (b. 1941) is an Italian-American physicist and semiconductor engineer credited with pioneering silicon-gate MOS technology and designing the world's first commercial microprocessor, the Intel 4004. Educated in physics at the University of Padua, his early career included work at Olivetti and SGS-Fairchild before moving to Silicon Valley.

At Fairchild and Intel, Faggin developed crucial innovations enabling integrated circuits and microprocessors. He led design of key early CPUs (4004, 8008, 4040, 8080) and later co-founded Zilog to create the Z80 microprocessor. He also co-founded companies like Synaptics and Foveon, contributing to optical character recognition and touchpad technology. Faggin has been awarded major honors for his transformative role in microelectronics and has proposed a quantum theory of consciousness.

# Ismail al-Jazari
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–c.1206) was a medieval inventor and engineer of Upper Mesopotamia, known for his influential *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*. Serving as chief engineer at the Artuklu Palace, he designed advanced automata, water clocks, pumps, and mechanical controls employing mechanisms such as camshafts, crankshafts, and segmental gears, many pioneering for their time.

Al-Jazari created water-raising machines, clockwork automata including drink-serving waitresses and musical bands, and developed innovative hydraulic and mechanical systems integrated with artistic miniatures. His work blended practical engineering with artistry, significantly contributing to the development of robotics and mechanics in the Islamic Golden Age, influencing later engineering traditions.

# Grace Hopper
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was a pioneering American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral who contributed significantly to programming languages and software development. She earned advanced degrees in mathematics, taught at Vassar, and during WWII joined the Navy Reserve programming the Mark I computer.

Hopper developed the first compiler and was instrumental in creating COBOL, a business-oriented programming language that popularized English-like syntax. She advocated for software standards and programming education and remained active in the Navy until age 79. Known for popularizing the term "debugging," she influenced both military and commercial computing and received numerous honors including the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

# Kurt Gödel
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) was an Austrian-American mathematician and logician famed for his incompleteness theorems, which demonstrated the inherent limitations of formal axiomatic systems. Educated in Vienna, Gödel advanced mathematical logic, proving fundamental results that transformed foundational mathematics and philosophy.

After fleeing Nazi Austria, he worked at Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study, forming a close friendship with Einstein. Gödel contributed to set theory, mathematical logic, and cosmology (Gödel metric). He suffered from mental health issues in later life and died of self-starvation in 1978. His work profoundly influenced logic, mathematics, and the philosophy of mathematics.

# Gottlob Frege
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German logician, mathematician, and philosopher, considered the father of modern logic and analytic philosophy. He developed the first formal system of predicate logic with quantified variables, aiming to base arithmetic on logical foundations (logicism).

His major works include *Begriffsschrift*, *Grundlagen der Arithmetik*, and *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik*, though his system was shown inconsistent by Russell's paradox. Frege introduced key semantics concepts such as sense and reference, influencing philosophy of language. Despite personal controversies over antisemitic views, his innovative work laid groundwork for much of twentieth-century logic and analytic philosophy.

# Lynn Conway
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was an American computer scientist, electrical engineer, and transgender rights activist. After early work at IBM on advanced computer architecture and pioneering superscalar design, Conway transitioned in the late 1960s, facing discrimination but later receiving public apology and awards.

She played a pivotal role at Xerox PARC in developing the Mead-Conway VLSI design methodology, which revolutionized chip design and education, enabling scalable, modular integrated circuits. Conway also contributed to rapid prototyping infrastructure (MOSIS) and held academic and DARPA positions. Later, she became an advocate for transgender rights and is recognized for both technical innovation and social impact.

# Al-Khwarizmi
Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (c.780–850) was a Persian mathematician, astronomer, and geographer, often called the "father of algebra." Working in Baghdad’s House of Wisdom, he authored *Al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wal-Muqabala*, establishing systematic solutions to linear and quadratic equations that gave rise to algebra as a discipline.

He introduced Hindu-Arabic numeral algorithms spreading their use in the West, contributed to trigonometry, and reworked Ptolemy's Geography with improved coordinates. His works laid foundational principles for mathematics, astronomy, and geography in medieval Islam and Europe. The terms "algorithm" and "algebra" are derived from his name and writings.

# Donald Knuth
Donald Knuth (b. 1938) is an American computer scientist, mathematician, and professor emeritus at Stanford, renowned for authoring *The Art of Computer Programming*, a foundational multi-volume work on algorithms. Early on, he excelled at problem solving and programming, creating compilers and teaching at Caltech and Stanford.

Knuth invented TeX, a widely used digital typesetting system, and pioneered literate programming. His work has shaped computer science education and theory. A Lutheran and accomplished organist, Knuth has received numerous awards including the Turing Award and National Medal of Science. He is noted for meticulous scholarship and a lifelong dedication to programming and mathematics.

# Per Brinch Hansen
Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a Danish-American computer scientist renowned for foundational work in operating systems, concurrent programming, and parallel computing. He led development of the RC 4000 multiprogramming system introducing the kernel and separation of mechanism and policy, authored seminal texts including *Operating System Principles* and *The Architecture of Concurrent Programs*, and pioneered the monitor construct and concurrent Pascal language.

His subsequent work extended to distributed computing and parallel algorithms, advancing multiprocessor and multicomputer systems. Brinch Hansen’s contributions shaped modern understanding of systems design, programming languages, and computational paradigms, earning him the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award.

# Claude Shannon
Claude Shannon (1916–2001) was an American mathematician and electrical engineer, hailed as the father of information theory. His 1937 master's thesis applied Boolean algebra to electrical circuits, laying foundations for digital circuit design.

During WWII, Shannon contributed to cryptography and control systems. His landmark 1948 paper, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, introduced information entropy, revolutionizing communications and triggering the digital revolution. He also pioneered signal processing, sampling theorem, and early AI devices such as a maze-solving mechanical mouse.

Shannon’s work forms the backbone of modern computing and telecommunications. He was also known for playful inventions and investment acumen. Numerous awards and tributes commemorate his influence.

# Herbert A. Simon
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was an American polymath known for groundbreaking contributions to economics, psychology, artificial intelligence, and decision theory. Educated in social sciences and mathematics, he developed theories of bounded rationality and satisficing in administrative behavior, integrating economics and cognitive psychology.

Simon was a pioneer in AI, co-creating early problem-solving programs and emphasizing procedural rationality and organizational behavior. He held faculty positions at Carnegie Mellon and contributed extensively to social sciences, earning the 1978 Nobel Prize in Economics and the 1975 Turing Award. His interdisciplinary work influenced economics, cognitive science, AI, and management.

# Edsger W. Dijkstra
Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist and pioneer in algorithms, programming languages, and software engineering. He formulated key algorithms (e.g., shortest path), advocated structured programming (famously critiquing GOTO), and developed early operating systems including THE multiprogramming system.

Dijkstra contributed to concurrent programming concepts such as semaphores, and produced extensive, elegantly written technical reports known as the EWDs. Known for his sharp intellect and rigorous style, he emphasized program correctness and clarity. He received the 1972 Turing Award and influenced computer science foundationally.
