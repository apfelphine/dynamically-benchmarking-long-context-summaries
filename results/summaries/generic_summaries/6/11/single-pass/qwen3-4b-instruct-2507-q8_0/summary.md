**George Boole** (1815–1864) was a British mathematician and logician, known as the founder of Boolean algebra, which forms the basis of modern digital computing. He began his education in Lincoln, self-taught in mathematics and languages, and later taught at various schools. He became a prominent local figure, involved in civic campaigns and the Lincoln Topographical Society. In 1849, he was appointed the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork (UCC), where he married Mary Everest. His key works include *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847), introducing symbolic logic, and *The Laws of Thought* (1854), which systematized logic and influenced the development of computer science. He was awarded the Royal Society's first gold prize in mathematics (1844), the Keith Medal (1855), and elected FRS in 1857. He died in 1864 from pleural effusion and is remembered for his foundational role in algebraic logic, with his legacy honored in institutions, a lunar crater, and programming languages.

**Ramon Llull** (1232–1315) was a Catalan philosopher, theologian, and inventor whose work in the 13th century laid the foundation for combinatorial logic. Inspired by Islamic and Christian thought, he developed a system of universal logic called "Llull's Art," which used combinatorial principles to derive truths about God and creation. His method involved a series of interlocking diagrams and principles, forming a logical framework for theological and philosophical inquiry. Though initially met with resistance in academic circles, his ideas influenced later thinkers like Leibniz and were later adopted in Renaissance and modern thought. His works, including *The Book of the Gentile and the Three Wise Men*, were widely circulated and promoted through dialogues and educational materials. He is venerated as a saintly figure, beatified in 1847, and is recognized as a precursor to modern computer science and algorithmic thinking.

**Kenneth E. Iverson** (1920–2004) was a Canadian computer scientist and pioneer in programming language design. Born in Alberta, he studied mathematics at Queen's University and Harvard, where he worked under Howard Aiken. He developed a novel notation system, which became the foundation for the programming language APL. His work at IBM led to the creation of APL, a language that emphasized concise, expressive syntax for data processing. He later developed J, a dialect of APL that used natural language-like syntax and became widely used in data analysis. Iverson's contributions to programming language design, particularly in data processing and teaching, earned him the Turing Award (1979), the IBM Fellow award, and numerous honors. He retired in 2004 and is remembered for his innovative approach to programming and his advocacy for accessible, intuitive computational tools.

**George Antheil** (1888–1959) was an American composer and inventor known for his avant-garde music and pioneering work in technology. Born in Trenton, New Jersey, he studied piano and composition and developed a unique style characterized by percussive, mechanical sounds. His most famous work, *Ballet Mécanique*, was a revolutionary fusion of music and machinery, using player pianos and mechanical devices to create a rhythmic, industrial soundscape. This work was a precursor to modern electronic music and inspired later composers. In addition to music, Antheil made significant contributions to technology, including co-inventing the frequency-hopping radio system with Hedy Lamarr during WWII, a foundational concept in modern wireless communication. He also wrote for film and was known for his flamboyant personality and activism. He died in 1959 and is remembered as a visionary who bridged the worlds of music and technology.

**Jacek Karpiński** (1927–2010) was a Polish computer scientist and engineer whose work in the mid-20th century laid the groundwork for modern computing. Born in Turin, Italy, he was the son of aeronautical engineer and physician. During World War II, he joined the Polish underground and participated in resistance activities, including operating a secret resistance outpost. After the war, he pursued engineering studies and became a leading figure in computer science. He designed the AAH (Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics), a machine that improved weather forecasting accuracy, and the AKAT-1, the world’s first differential equations analyzer based on transistors. He later developed the KAR-65 and K-202 computers, which were innovative for their time. Despite facing political and bureaucratic challenges, Karpiński's work significantly advanced computer science in Poland. He was recognized with numerous awards, including UNESCO’s international award for young engineers, and is celebrated for his contributions to computer engineering and his role in the development of early digital systems.

**Geoffrey Hinton** (1947–) is a British-Canadian artificial intelligence (AI) researcher and one of the most influential figures in the development of deep learning. Born in England, he studied at Cambridge and Edinburgh, earning a PhD in artificial intelligence. He co-invented the backpropagation algorithm, a key technique in training neural networks, and developed the Boltzmann machine, which enabled neural networks to learn from data. He played a central role in the development of deep learning, which powers modern AI systems. Hinton has published over 200 peer-reviewed papers and is a co-author of the seminal work on neural networks. In 2018, he won the ACM A.M. Turing Award for his contributions to deep learning. He is also known for his recent scientific skepticism about the risks of AI, particularly regarding the possibility of artificial general intelligence (AGI) surpassing human intelligence. He has advocated for AI safety, regulation, and the need for universal basic income to mitigate job displacement. Hinton is a Fellow of the Royal Society and has received numerous awards, including the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2024, shared with John Hopfield, for foundational discoveries in neural networks.

**Federico Faggin** (1927–2013) was an Italian computer engineer and inventor whose work revolutionized the design of microprocessors and digital circuits. Born in Italy, he worked at Olivetti and then joined Fairchild Semiconductor, where he developed the silicon-gate technology (SGT), a foundational innovation that enabled the creation of modern integrated circuits. His work at Intel led to the design of the world’s first single-chip microprocessor, the Intel 4004, which marked the beginning of the microprocessor era. Faggin also contributed to the development of dynamic RAM, the first microcontroller, and the touchpad. Later in life, he became interested in consciousness and proposed a theory of quantum-based consciousness, suggesting that pure quantum states could be the basis of subjective experience. He received numerous awards, including the Marconi International Fellowship, the Golden Plate Award, and the Kyoto Prize, and is remembered as a pioneer in the development of modern computing hardware.

**Ismail al-Jazari** (1136–1206) was a 12th-century Muslim engineer and inventor from Upper Mesopotamia, known as the "father of robotics." He served as chief engineer at the Artuqid palace in Mardin and authored *The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, a comprehensive treatise on mechanical devices. Al-Jazari designed a wide range of automata, including water clocks, fountains, and musical robots. His inventions incorporated innovative mechanisms such as the crankshaft, camshaft, and escapement, and included the first known use of a true suction pump and a double-acting piston. His work demonstrated early principles of automation, control systems, and fluid dynamics. Al-Jazari's designs were both practical and artistic, often illustrated with detailed miniatures. His legacy has influenced modern engineering and robotics, and his work is recognized as a foundational contribution to the history of mechanical engineering and automation.

**Grace Hopper** (1906–1992) was an American computer scientist and naval officer, widely recognized as a pioneer in the development of programming languages. Born in New York, she studied mathematics and physics at Vassar and Yale, and later became a professor at Vassar. During World War II, she served in the U.S. Navy and worked on the Harvard Mark I computer, where she developed the first compiler. She is credited with inventing the first compiler, a program that translates high-level programming languages into machine code, which laid the foundation for modern programming. Her work led to the creation of COBOL, one of the first high-level programming languages designed for business applications. Hopper became a leader in the field, advocating for standardized programming languages and the use of English-like syntax. She held the rank of commodore in the U.S. Navy and received numerous awards, including the National Medal of Science and the Presidential Medal of Freedom. She is remembered for her contributions to programming language design, her leadership in computer science, and her advocacy for the accessibility of computing technology.

**Kurt Gödel** (1906–1978) was an Austrian-born logician and mathematician whose work fundamentally changed the foundations of mathematics. Born in Brünn, Austria-Hungary, he studied at the University of Vienna and later worked at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. His most famous contributions include the incompleteness theorems, which proved that in any formal system powerful enough to describe arithmetic, there are true statements that cannot be proven within the system. This result challenged the idea that mathematics could be fully formalized and completed. Gödel also made significant contributions to set theory, including the consistency of the axiom of choice and the generalized continuum hypothesis. He was a leading figure in the development of mathematical logic and is considered one of the most important logicians in history. Gödel's work has had a profound impact on philosophy, computer science, and the foundations of mathematics. He is known for his rigorous, abstract style and his deep philosophical views on mathematics and religion.

**Gottlob Frege** (1848–1925) was a German philosopher and logician who is considered the founder of modern logic and analytic philosophy. Born in Wismar, Germany, he studied at the University of Jena and later at Göttingen. His groundbreaking work, *Begriffsschrift* (1879), introduced a formal system of logic that separated logic from arithmetic and established the foundations of symbolic logic. He developed a rigorous treatment of functions, variables, and the distinction between sense and reference, which became central to modern philosophy of language. Frege's work on the foundations of arithmetic and his development of the concept of number as a logical construct influenced the development of set theory and the philosophy of mathematics. He is best known for his distinction between "sense" and "reference," which has had a lasting impact on linguistic theory. Frege's work laid the foundation for the development of modern logic and influenced later thinkers such as Russell, Wittgenstein, and Turing. He is regarded as one of the most important figures in the history of logic and philosophy.

**Lynn Conway** (1938–2024) was an American computer scientist and pioneer in the field of VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) design. Born in New York, she studied at MIT and Columbia University, and later joined IBM, where she worked on advanced supercomputers. After being fired for her gender identity, she transitioned to a new career in computer design and co-developed the Mead–Conway VLSI design methodology, which revolutionized chip design by introducing scalable, modular design rules. Her work led to the creation of MOSIS, a system that enabled universities and small companies to access state-of-the-art digital technology. Conway is credited with establishing the "foundry model" of chip design and was instrumental in the growth of the electronic design automation industry. She is also known for her advocacy of gender equality in science and technology and for her work in transgender rights activism. She received numerous awards, including the IEEE EAB Major Educational Innovation Award and the IEEE/RSE James Clerk Maxwell Medal, and is recognized as a trailblazer in both computer science and social justice.

**Al-Khwarizmi** (c. 780–850) was a Persian mathematician, astronomer, and geographer whose work laid the foundation for modern algebra and the use of the Hindu-Arabic numeral system. Born in Khwarazm, he worked in Baghdad at the House of Wisdom and authored several influential texts. His most famous work, *Al-Jabr* (c. 820), introduced the concept of algebra as a distinct field of mathematics, and the term "algebra" is derived from the title of this book. He also wrote on arithmetic, where he introduced the use of decimal numbers and algorithms, which were later translated into Latin and became the basis of modern computing. Al-Khwarizmi's work on trigonometry, astronomy, and geography significantly advanced scientific knowledge in the medieval Islamic world. His contributions are foundational to the development of modern mathematics, and he is remembered as a key figure in the transmission of scientific knowledge from the Islamic world to Europe.

**Claude Shannon** (1916–2001) was an American mathematician and electrical engineer, widely regarded as the father of information theory and digital circuit design. Born in Michigan, he studied at the University of Michigan and MIT, where he developed the foundational concepts of information theory. His 1948 paper, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, introduced the concept of information entropy and established the mathematical foundations of communication systems. He is best known for his work on switching circuits, which demonstrated that Boolean algebra could be used to design digital circuits, forming the basis of modern digital computers. He also made significant contributions to cryptography, artificial intelligence, and computer chess. Shannon's work on the one-time pad and the sampling theorem has had a lasting impact on modern telecommunications and data security. He is credited with co-inventing the concept of the digital computer and is recognized as a key figure in the digital revolution. His legacy is honored through the unit "shannon" in cryptocurrency and numerous awards, including the National Medal of Science and the Kyoto Prize.

**Herbert A. Simon** (1916–2001) was an American psychologist, economist, and computer scientist known for his work on bounded rationality and organizational decision-making. Born in Milwaukee, he studied at the University of Chicago and became a leading figure in the field of organizational behavior. His book *Administrative Behavior* (1947) introduced the concept of bounded rationality, arguing that human decision-making is limited by cognitive constraints and incomplete information. This concept has had a profound impact on economics, psychology, and management science. Simon co-developed the Logic Theory Machine and the General Problem Solver, early programs in artificial intelligence. He also contributed to the development of human-computer interaction and the theory of problem-solving. He won the Nobel Prize in Economics (1978) for his research into decision-making in organizations and was awarded the ACM Turing Award (1975) for his contributions to AI and cognitive science. Simon is remembered as a pioneering figure in the intersection of psychology, economics, and computer science.

**Edsger W. Dijkstra** (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist and one of the most influential figures in the development of structured programming and algorithm design. Born in Rotterdam, he worked at the Mathematical Centre in Amsterdam and later at Eindhoven University of Technology and the University of Texas at Austin. He is best known for his 1968 letter "Go To Statement Considered Harmful," which advocated for structured programming and had a major impact on modern programming practices. He developed Dijkstra's algorithm for finding the shortest path in a network, widely taught in computer science curricula. Other key contributions include the Banker's algorithm for resource allocation, the semaphore construct for process synchronization, and the concept of self-stabilization in distributed systems. Dijkstra emphasized clarity, elegance, and simplicity in programming and was known for his rigorous, mathematical approach. He won the Turing Award (1972) and is remembered as a visionary who shaped the foundations of modern software engineering and computer science.
