# Grace Hopper  
Grace Hopper was a pioneering computer scientist and Navy rear admiral, known for her work on early computers and programming languages. Born in New York City in 1906, she earned advanced degrees in mathematics from Vassar and Yale. During WWII, she joined the Navy WAVES and worked on the Harvard Mark I computer. Later, Hopper contributed to the development of UNIVAC and pioneered the creation of compilers, advocating for programming languages that used English-like syntax. This work led to the development of COBOL, a widely used business programming language. She promoted computer standards and networked systems within the Department of Defense. Hopper retired as the oldest active-duty officer and received numerous awards, including posthumous Presidential Medal of Freedom. Known for popularizing the term “debugging,” she remained active as a consultant until her death in 1992.

# Federico Faggin  
Federico Faggin is an Italian physicist and inventor notable for developing the first commercial MOS silicon gate technology and leading the design of the Intel 4004, the world’s first single-chip microprocessor. After early work at Olivetti and SGS-Fairchild, he joined Fairchild Semiconductor and later Intel, where his innovations in process technology and design methodology enabled the first microprocessor’s realization. He co-founded Zilog, creating the Z80 microprocessor and early microcontrollers. Faggin also advanced AI research at Synaptics and improved image sensors at Foveon. Since 2011, he has supported consciousness research through his foundation and proposed a quantum theory of consciousness. He has received numerous international awards, including the National Medal of Technology and Innovation.

# Gottlob Frege  
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German logician and philosopher who founded modern predicate logic and analytic philosophy. He introduced a formal logical language, distinguishing sense and reference in language and logic, and sought to derive arithmetic from logic (logicism). His "Basic Law V" was shown inconsistent by Russell's paradox, but much of his program influenced later logic and mathematics. Frege’s philosophical work shaped philosophy of language and mathematics. Despite his groundbreaking work, late in life he held antisemitic views. He was reserved in personality and taught mainly in Jena, where he produced his key logical and philosophical writings.

# Jacek Karpiński  
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and WWII resistance fighter known for creating pioneering computers and neural network-based devices behind the Iron Curtain. Despite severe wartime injuries, he developed advanced machines like the AAH harmonic analyzer (1957), AKAT-1 transistor differential analyzer (1959), and the percepton (1964), and later designed the KAR-65 and the revolutionary K-202 minicomputer with modular architecture and advanced features unmatched by contemporaries. Political obstacles and industrial rivalry curtailed widespread adoption. After moving to Switzerland, he returned to Poland and advised on computing until his death. Controversies include covert intelligence cooperation and debates over the extent of his technical contributions.

# Al-Khwarizmi  
Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī (c.780–c.850) was a Persian mathematician, astronomer, and scholar in the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, whose works established algebra as a discipline. His treatise Al-Jabr introduced systematic solutions to linear and quadratic equations, leading to the term "algebra." He popularized Hindu–Arabic numerals in the Western world via his arithmetic texts, coining "algorithm." He produced influential works in astronomy, geography, and mechanical devices, including accurate sine and cosine tables and revised Ptolemaic geography. His writings on calculation methods profoundly impacted medieval mathematics and science in both the Islamic world and Europe.

# Kenneth E. Iverson  
Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004) was a Canadian computer scientist noted for developing the APL programming language, which introduced powerful array and matrix notations. Initially self-taught, he earned degrees from Queen’s University and Harvard. At IBM, he helped formally specify the IBM System/360 and co-developed APL, greatly influencing interactive computing. Later, Iverson worked at I. P. Sharp Associates and founded J Software Inc., creating the J language as a successor to APL with ASCII-based syntax. He contributed significantly to computer notation, education, and implementation, earning prestigious awards including the Turing Award in 1979.

# Per Brinch Hansen  
Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a Danish computer scientist and pioneer in concurrent and parallel programming. He developed the RC 4000 multiprogramming system introducing modern OS kernel concepts, authored the first textbook on operating systems, and created the first monitor notation and language, Concurrent Pascal. His work extended to distributed computing with remote procedure calls and programming languages like Edison and Joyce. He was a professor at USC, University of Copenhagen, and Syracuse University. Brinch Hansen’s emphasis on simplicity and practical testing shaped operating systems and concurrency theory. He received the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award in 2002.

# George Boole  
George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician who founded Boolean algebra, the mathematical basis of digital logic and computing. Largely self-taught, he became the first professor of mathematics at Queen’s College Cork. Boole’s work systematically treated logic algebraically, extending Aristotelian logic, and laid foundations for probability theory and symbolic logic. His algebraic approach directly influenced electronic circuit design through Claude Shannon’s work. Despite a brief academic career cut short by pneumonia, Boole’s legacy persists as foundational in computer science, mathematics, and logic, celebrated worldwide including bicentenary events and numerous eponymous honors.

# Herbert A. Simon  
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was an American polymath whose work spanned economics, psychology, artificial intelligence, and organizational theory. Educated at the University of Chicago, he pioneered decision-making research, introducing concepts like bounded rationality and satisficing. He contributed extensively to artificial intelligence, co-developing early AI programs and influencing cognitive psychology. Simon’s interdisciplinary research led to the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978. He authored influential works on administrative behavior and cognitive models, impacting social sciences, economics, AI, and education. Simon’s extensive scholarly output and awards mark him as a seminal figure in 20th-century science.

# Ramon Llull  
Ramon Llull (c.1232–1316) was a Majorcan philosopher, theologian, and medieval Christian mystic known for creating the Ars, a universal combinatorial logic system aiming to prove Christian doctrines. Inspired by visions, he devoted his life to missionary work, linguistic education, and conversion efforts. Llull’s Art combined letters, diagrams, and combinatorics in iterative proofs, influencing later logic, theology, and early computation concepts. He authored dialogues, proverbs, novels, and mechanical theological treatises. Though controversial and resisted in his time, he left a significant legacy as a precursor to computation and logical systems and was beatified in 1847.

# Edsger W. Dijkstra  
Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist and one of the pioneers of structured programming and algorithm theory. Early in his career he developed foundational algorithms such as Dijkstra’s shortest path and introduced concepts such as semaphores and software layering (THE operating system). He worked at the Mathematisch Centrum, Eindhoven, Burroughs Corporation, and the University of Texas at Austin. Known for his rigorous, elegant style and strong opinions, Dijkstra influenced programming methodology profoundly, advocating for clarity and correctness. His “Go To Statement Considered Harmful” letter helped shift programming paradigms. He received the 1972 Turing Award among many honors.

# George Antheil  
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer and inventor known for his radical modernist music and innovations blending mechanical and electronic sounds. After formative years in the US and Europe working with figures like Igor Stravinsky, he gained notoriety for works like Ballet Mécanique, featuring synchronized player pianos and airplane propellers. Later, he composed for Hollywood films and pursued diverse interests. During WWII, alongside actress Hedy Lamarr, Antheil co-invented a frequency-hopping spread spectrum system for secure communication—an early precursor to modern wireless encryption. He was noted for his provocative performances and broad artistic pursuits.

# Claude Shannon  
Claude Elwood Shannon (1916–2001) was an American mathematician and electrical engineer credited as the father of information theory. He applied Boolean algebra to electronic circuits, laying the foundation for digital circuit design. Shannon formalized the concept of information entropy and developed fundamental theorems on data transmission and compression. His 1948 paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," revolutionized telecommunications and computer science. During WWII, he contributed to cryptography and fire-control systems. Shannon also pioneered early AI experiments, including maze-solving machines. A prolific inventor and polymath, his work profoundly shaped the Digital Age and modern communications.

# Geoffrey Hinton  
Geoffrey Hinton (b.1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist known as a founding figure in deep learning and neural networks. Educated at Cambridge and Edinburgh, he has been instrumental in advancing machine learning, notably co-inventing the backpropagation algorithm and Boltzmann machines. Hinton co-founded DNNresearch (acquired by Google) and contributed to major AI breakthroughs such as capsule networks and contrastive learning. In 2018, he jointly received the Turing Award for deep learning breakthroughs. Hinton continues to research and express concerns about AI risks, advocating for responsible development. He won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for foundational AI discoveries.

# Donald Knuth  
Donald E. Knuth (b.1938) is an American computer scientist and mathematician, renowned for his multi-volume work *The Art of Computer Programming*, a foundational series in algorithm analysis. A pioneer of algorithmics, Knuth introduced the concept of literate programming and developed TeX, a widely used digital typesetting system. Educated at Case Institute and Caltech, he has held teaching positions at Caltech and Stanford. His contributions shaped programming language design, digital typography, and computer science education. Knuth has received numerous honors including the Turing Award, Kyoto Prize, and National Medal of Science.

# Ismail al-Jazari  
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–c.1206) was a medieval Muslim polymath, inventor, and engineer from Upper Mesopotamia, serving as chief engineer at the Artuklu Palace. Author of *The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, he described numerous advanced automata, water-raising machines, musical devices, and sophisticated water clocks, including the crankshaft and camshaft mechanisms. Al-Jazari introduced early mechanical controls and programmable automata, influencing engineering and robotics history. His innovations include the elephant clock and water-powered devices incorporating escapements. He also produced miniature paintings illustrating his inventions, blending craftsmanship with practical engineering.

# Lynn Conway  
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was an American computer scientist and transgender activist whose innovations revolutionized VLSI chip design. After early work at IBM—including contributions to the Advanced Computing Systems project and invention of dynamic instruction scheduling—she transitioned publicly in 1968, faced job loss, and restarted her career. At Xerox PARC, she co-developed the Mead-Conway VLSI design methodology and pioneered multiproject wafer technology and the MOSIS prototyping system, enabling modern chip design practices. Conway held academic and DARPA leadership roles and advocated for transgender rights later in life. Her technical and social contributions earned numerous awards and enduring impact on semiconductor design.

# Kurt Gödel  
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) was an Austrian-American logician, mathematician, and philosopher known for his incompleteness theorems, which profoundly affected the foundations of mathematics. Educated in Vienna, he proved that any sufficiently powerful consistent formal system cannot prove all truths about arithmetic and cannot prove its own consistency. Gödel also contributed to set theory, relativity (Gödel metric), and philosophical logic. After emigrating to the US in 1940, he was a lifelong member of the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton and a close friend of Einstein. His work is foundational in mathematical logic, computability, and philosophy of mathematics, though he struggled with mental health and paranoia later in life.
