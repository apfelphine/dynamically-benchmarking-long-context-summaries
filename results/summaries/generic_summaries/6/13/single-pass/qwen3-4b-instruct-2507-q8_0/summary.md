**George Antheil**  
A controversial "futurist" pianist and composer, Antheil gained fame in the 1920s with works like *Ballet Mécanique*, a radical piece of mechanical music using player pianos and airplane sounds. He studied under Constantine von Sternberg and Ernest Bloch, and was influenced by Dadaism and modernist art. Though his music was provocative and often met with hostility, it inspired later composers. He also invented a frequency-hopping radio system with Hedy Lamarr during WWII, a precursor to modern spread-spectrum technology. Later in life, he moved to Hollywood, where he composed scores for films and wrote on music and technology. His memoir, *The Bad Boy of Music*, became a bestseller.

**Geoffrey Hinton**  
A pioneering computer scientist, Hinton is known for his foundational work in neural networks, including the backpropagation algorithm and Boltzmann machines. He co-founded DNNresearch Inc. and worked at Google, where he later resigned to speak out about AI risks. He has expressed deep concerns about AI surpassing human intelligence, its potential for misuse, and its impact on jobs and society. A socialist, he advocates for government regulation of AI and has promoted ethical AI development. Hinton won the Turing Award and numerous honors, including the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2024 (shared with John Hopfield).

**Ramon Llull**  
A 13th-century Catalan polymath, Llull developed a system of universal logic called *Llull's Art*, which used combinatorial principles to explore philosophical and theological questions. His works, including dialogues and treatises, aimed to convert Muslims and heretics through logical reasoning. Though not widely accepted in his time, his ideas influenced later thinkers and are seen as precursors to modern logic and computer science. He is venerated as a saint and his legacy includes contributions to philosophy, theology, and cognitive science.

**Ismail al-Jazari**  
A 12th–13th century engineer, al-Jazari designed numerous mechanical devices, including water clocks, pumps, and automata. His inventions, such as the water-raising *saqiya chain pump* and the *peacock fountain*, showcased early applications of hydraulics and automation. He is credited with pioneering concepts like the crankshaft, camshaft, and escapement mechanisms. His work laid the foundation for modern engineering and robotics, and he is recognized as a key figure in the history of mechanical engineering.

**George Boole**  
A 19th-century mathematician, Boole developed Boolean algebra, which forms the foundation of modern digital computing. His work on logic and probability influenced the development of computer science. He proposed a symbolic system for logic and used it to analyze reasoning and truth. His ideas were later adopted by Claude Shannon, who used them to design digital circuits. Boole is considered a foundational figure in the development of computer science and information theory.

**Kurt Gödel**  
A 20th-century logician, Gödel proved the incompleteness theorems, showing that in any formal system powerful enough to describe arithmetic, there are true statements that cannot be proven within the system. His work revolutionized mathematics and logic, establishing limits on formal systems. He also contributed to the foundations of set theory and computer science. A devout theist, he proposed a formal proof of God's existence. He died in 1978 and is remembered as one of the most influential figures in modern logic.

**Kenneth Iverson**  
A computer scientist and inventor, Iverson developed the APL programming language, which introduced a unique, concise notation for data manipulation. He created the APL language to improve programming efficiency and clarity. His work on APL influenced the development of modern programming languages. He also invented the *fork* operator, which enabled more expressive and powerful programming. Iverson retired in 2004 and is remembered for his contributions to programming and computer science.

**Lynn Conway**  
A pioneering computer engineer, Conway co-developed the Mead–Conway VLSI design methodology, which revolutionized chip design by introducing scalable design rules and a prototype-based infrastructure. She founded the MOSIS system, enabling small-scale chip fabrication. A transgender woman, she faced discrimination and adversity in her career but became a trailblazer in both engineering and LGBTQ+ rights. She is recognized for her contributions to computer science and for advocating for gender equity in STEM.

**Jacek Karpiński**  
A Polish engineer and computer scientist, Karpiński designed the K-202 minicomputer, one of the first to use microchips and memory segmentation. Though production was limited due to political and industrial challenges, his work advanced early computing. He also developed the AAH and AKAT-1 machines. Karpiński is known for his technical innovations and for his role in the development of computer systems in communist Poland.

**Donald Knuth**  
A computer scientist and mathematician, Knuth is best known for *The Art of Computer Programming*, a monumental series that became a foundational text in computer science. He developed TeX and Metafont for typesetting, and introduced the concept of *literate programming*. He also wrote *Surreal Numbers*, a mathematical novelette. Knuth is widely regarded as one of the most influential figures in computer science and has received numerous awards, including the Turing Award and the National Medal of Science.

**Federico Faggin**  
An Italian engineer, Faggin invented the silicon-gate technology that enabled the development of modern microprocessors. He designed the Intel 4004, the world's first single-chip microprocessor. His work at Intel and later at Zilog led to the creation of the Z80 microprocessor. Faggin also developed the Communication CoSystem and contributed to the field of artificial neural networks. He is known for his contributions to computer architecture and microprocessor design.

**Claude Shannon**  
A pioneer in information theory, Shannon developed the mathematical foundations of communication, introducing concepts like entropy and the sampling theorem. His work on switching circuits and Boolean algebra laid the groundwork for digital computing. He co-invented the concept of the one-time pad in cryptography and developed early models for artificial intelligence, including the "Theseus" learning machine. Shannon is considered the father of information theory and is credited with shaping the digital age.

**Grace Hopper**  
A computer scientist and pioneer in programming, Hopper developed the first compiler and created COBOL, a programming language designed for business applications. She was instrumental in establishing standards for programming languages and advocated for user-friendly programming. A trailblazer for women in computing, she received numerous awards and is remembered for her leadership and advocacy in the field.

**Edsger W. Dijkstra**  
A Dutch computer scientist, Dijkstra is known for his work in algorithms, including Dijkstra's algorithm for shortest paths and the concept of bounded rationality. He promoted structured programming and wrote extensively on software engineering. He was a strong critic of unstructured programming and advocated for clear, formal, and rigorous design. Dijkstra is widely regarded as one of the most influential figures in computer science and received the Turing Award.

**Gottlob Frege**  
A 19th-century German philosopher and logician, Frege developed the foundations of modern logic, introducing the distinction between sense and reference. His work on predicate logic and the foundations of arithmetic influenced later developments in mathematics and philosophy. He is considered the father of modern logic and a key figure in the development of analytic philosophy.

**Herbert A. Simon**  
A cognitive scientist and economist, Simon developed the theory of bounded rationality, arguing that humans make decisions based on limited information and cognitive capacity. He pioneered work in artificial intelligence, including the Logic Theory Machine and the General Problem Solver. His work on human decision-making and organizational behavior has had a lasting impact on economics, psychology, and computer science.

**Al-Khwarizmi**  
A 9th-century Persian mathematician, al-Khwarizmi is credited with founding algebra and introducing the Hindu-Arabic numeral system to the West. His work on arithmetic and algebra laid the foundation for modern mathematics. The term "algorithm" derives from his name, and his contributions to astronomy and geography were also significant. He is considered one of the most important figures in the history of mathematics.

**Per Brinch Hansen**  
A Danish computer scientist, Brinch Hansen made foundational contributions to operating systems and concurrent programming. He developed the RC 4000 multiprogramming system and introduced the monitor concept. His work on Concurrent Pascal and distributed computing influenced the design of modern operating systems and programming languages. He is recognized as a pioneer in computer architecture and systems programming.
