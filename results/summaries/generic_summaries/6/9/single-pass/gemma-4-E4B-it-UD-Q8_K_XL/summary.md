## Jacek Karpiński
Jacek Karpiński, born in Turin, Italy, was the son of an aeronautic engineer and a physician, both pioneers in winter mountaineering. His life was dramatically altered by World War II; he joined the Polish underground paramilitary scouts and was severely injured in 1943. During the Warsaw Uprising, he was shot and left paralyzed.

Post-war, Karpiński studied engineering, and later focused on advanced computing. His key contributions include the AAH (Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics) and, most significantly, AKAT-1 in 1959, which was the world's first differential equations analyzer based on transistors. Following a UNESCO scholarship in the U.S., he developed the Polish perceptron in 1964. His career involved the development of the KAR-65 computer, which faced censorship, and the K-202 minicomputer, which, despite its technical excellence, never reached mass production due to political obstacles. He died in 2010, having also served as a secret informant for Polish intelligence.

## Kenneth E. Iverson
Ken Iverson, born in Canada, earned advanced degrees from Queen's and Harvard Universities. He developed notation systems for data processing, realizing conventional mathematics was insufficient for his needs. During his time at Harvard, he began developing his notation, which was later field-tested at McKinsey & Company.

At IBM, Iverson and Adin Falkoff formalized the description of the IBM System/360. This work led to the creation of APL (A Programming Language), which became widely adopted. Later, at I. P. Sharp Associates, he further refined APL, culminating in the "dictionary APL." After retiring from IPSA, he focused on creating the J language, which introduced "forks," allowing for a more natural, human-language-like programming style.

## Federico Faggin
Federico Faggin, educated in Italy, began his career at Olivetti, co-designing an early digital computer and contributing to the Olivetti Programma 101. In 1967, he joined SGS-Fairchild, where he pioneered the transformative MOS metal-gate process technology.

At Fairchild, Faggin led the development of Silicon-Gate Technology (SGT), which formed the basis for modern microelectronics. He later joined Intel in 1970 as the project leader for the MCS-4 family, which included the Intel 4004—the world's first single-chip microprocessor. His SGT design methodology was applied to subsequent microprocessors like the 8080. Faggin later founded Zilog, where he conceived the Z80 CPU, and co-founded Synaptics, leading to innovations like the touchpad and early touchscreens. He currently researches consciousness, proposing it is a purely quantum phenomenon.

## George Boole
George Boole was a self-taught mathematician who began by teaching in Lincoln, England. He studied algebra using symbolic methods and began publishing research papers. His early work led to his appointment as the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork, in 1849.

Boole is best known for publishing *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* in 1847, which established what is now known as Boolean algebra. In this work, he introduced concepts like "and" for multiplication and "or" for addition, founding the "algebra of logic." His later work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought*, systematized his views and founded the "algebra of logic" tradition. He died in 1864.

## Grace Hopper
Grace Brewster Murray Hopper was an accomplished mathematician who studied at Vassar and Yale. During World War II, she served in the U.S. Navy Reserve, working on the Mark I computer at Harvard.

In 1949, she joined Eckert–Mauchly Computer Corporation, working on the UNIVAC I. Insisting that programs should use English words, she persisted in developing compiler technology, which led to the creation of the A compiler and later FLOW-MATIC. By 1959, she was a technical consultant on the CODASYL conference that defined COBOL, the ubiquitous business language. Later, she advocated for network standards in the 1970s before retiring in 1986.

## Ramon Llull
Ramon Llull, born in Majorca, was a scholar who experienced religious visions in 1263. These visions inspired him with three goals: dedicating his life to God, converting Muslims to Christianity, and writing the best book against unbelief. He studied both Christian and Muslim theology and philosophy.

His central work, *Llull's Art*, was a combinatorial system of universal logic based on general principles activated in a process. The Art was divided into a Quaternary Phase and a Ternary Phase. He developed concepts like the "correlatives" to support his system, which was intended as a tool for conversion. Despite academic opposition and papal prohibitions, his work influenced later thinkers, including Gottfried Leibniz.

## Gottlob Frege
Gottlob Frege, born in Germany, initially focused on geometry and physics. His work shifted toward logic when he devised the *Begriffsschrift* in 1879, which modeled pure thought after arithmetic.

Frege invented axiomatic predicate logic, largely due to his use of quantified variables, which allowed him to represent complex logical statements far beyond the scope of Aristotelian syllogistic. His major goal was to prove that arithmetic grew out of logic (logicism). His magnum opus, *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik*, attempted to derive all laws of arithmetic from logical axioms. The system faced a crisis when Bertrand Russell demonstrated a contradiction (Russell's Paradox), forcing Frege to issue an Appendix. Philosophically, he introduced the distinction between "sense" (mode of presentation) and "reference" (denotation) in 1892.

## George Antheil
George Antheil was a German-immigrant composer from Trenton, New Jersey. He studied composition in Europe, absorbing influences from Dadaism and modernists like Stravinsky.

His most famous work is *Ballet Mécanique*, which was scored for numerous player pianos and designed to evoke the rhythm of machinery. He achieved notoriety in Paris for his loud, percussive music style. During WWII, he collaborated with Hedy Lamarr to develop frequency-hopping radio technology, which was patented in 1942, a precursor to modern spread-spectrum communication.

## Al-Khwarizmi
Al-Khwārizmī, a Persian scholar active in Baghdad between 813 and 833, made foundational contributions across mathematics, geography, and astronomy.

His systematic approach to solving linear and quadratic equations led to the development of algebra; the term "algebra" derives from his book, *Al-Jabr*. Furthermore, his work on calculating with Hindu-Arabic numerals was key to spreading this system across the Middle East and Europe, giving rise to the term "algorithm." His geography, *Kitāb Ṣūrat al-Arḍ*, significantly improved upon Ptolemy's coordinates.

## Geoffrey Hinton
Geoffrey Hinton, educated at Cambridge and Edinburgh, initially studied under the symbolic AI approach. He later became a leading figure in neural network research.

He was instrumental in co-developing the backpropagation algorithm with David E. Rumelhart and Ronald J. Williams, which demonstrated how multi-layer networks could learn useful representations. He co-invented Boltzmann machines with David Ackley and Terry Sejnowski. Hinton is known for his recent philosophical skepticism regarding current AI limitations, advocating for self-organizing systems like GLOM, and publicly voicing concerns about the existential and societal risks of rapidly advancing AI.

## Per Brinch Hansen
Per Brinch Hansen was a computer scientist whose career spanned operating systems, concurrent computing, and distributed systems. His work at Regnecentralen led to writing a parser for a COBOL compiler and developing a file system.

He defined the computer architecture and instruction set for the RC 4000, using ALGOL 60. His seminal 1973 book, *Operating System Principles*, was the first comprehensive text on the subject. Later, at USC, he authored *The Architecture of Concurrent Programs* (1977), introducing the monitor concept and the language Concurrent Pascal, which was the first language specifically designed for concurrency.

## Ismail al-Jazari
Ismail al-Jazari was an artisan and chief engineer in Upper Mesopotamia in the 12th century. His practical work is documented in his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*.

He is recognized for inventing numerous complex mechanical devices, including sophisticated automata, water-raising machines, and elaborate water clocks. Key mechanical innovations attributed to him include the camshaft, an early crankshaft and crank-slider mechanism, and the conical valve and segmental gear. His water-raising machines are considered direct ancestors of modern engineering principles.

## Herbert A. Simon
Herbert A. Simon, a political scientist, became renowned for his theories on decision-making. His book, *Administrative Behavior* (1947), challenged the "homo economicus" model by arguing that human decisions are constrained by limited knowledge and cognitive capacity.

He defined rational decision-making as selecting the alternative that yields the most preferred set of consequences, divided into steps of identifying alternatives, determining consequences, and comparing them. Simon was a pioneer in Artificial Intelligence, co-creating the Logic Theory Machine and the General Problem Solver (GPS) with Allen Newell. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978 for his work on bounded rationality.

## Kurt Gödel
Kurt Gödel, a brilliant mathematician from Austria-Hungary, studied in Vienna, becoming deeply interested in mathematical logic after encountering Bertrand Russell's work.

His singular achievement was proving his incompleteness theorems in 1930. These theorems demonstrated that for any sufficiently powerful, computable axiomatic system (like arithmetic), there will always be true statements that cannot be proven within that system, thereby ending the quest for a complete axiomatization of arithmetic. He also contributed to set theory by showing the consistency of the Axiom of Choice and the Continuum Hypothesis within the constructible universe.

## Edsger W. Dijkstra
Edsger W. Dijkstra was a programmer who became a philosophical voice in computer science, advocating for a discipline of programming. He was instrumental in the development of the THE operating system, which featured software-based paged virtual memory.

Dijkstra famously authored *A Discipline of Programming* (1976), advocating for structured programming over unstructured jumps (`GOTO` statements), a stance formalized in his famous 1968 letter. He also developed the monitor concept alongside C.A.R. Hoare, and his work on concurrent programming led to the creation of Concurrent Pascal. He was awarded the Turing Award in 1972.

## Lynn Conway
Lynn Conway was a computer scientist whose career spanned the development of computer architecture. While working at IBM, she was part of the team designing the Advanced Computing Systems (ACS) supercomputer, credited with inventing multiple-issue out-of-order dynamic instruction scheduling.

At Xerox PARC, she co-authored *Introduction to VLSI Systems* with Carver Mead, leading the "LSI Systems" group. She founded the multiproject wafers (MPW) technology. Her invention of dimensionless, scalable design rules and the institutionalization of the MOSIS system were crucial to the VLSI chip design revolution. She was also a vocal advocate for transgender rights.

## Claude Shannon
Claude Shannon, born in Michigan, is widely regarded as the founder of information theory. In his master's thesis (1937), he revolutionized switching circuit theory by demonstrating that Boolean algebra's essential operators could be implemented using electrical switches, fundamentally underpinning all digital computer design.

His 1948 paper, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, introduced information entropy as a measure of uncertainty reduction, thus inventing information theory. During WWII, his cryptography work led to proving the unbreakable nature of the one-time pad. He also created the learning machine "Theseus," an early AI device, and co-organized the Dartmouth workshop that founded the field of Artificial Intelligence.

## Donald Knuth
Donald Knuth is a computer scientist and writer known for his monumental work, *The Art of Computer Programming* (TAOCP), which spans numerous volumes detailing algorithms and mathematics.

He was a key figure in the development of digital typesetting, creating TeX and Metafont. Knuth introduced the methodology of "literate programming," viewing programs as works of literature. He is highly regarded for his rigorous approach to computer science, his contribution to combinatorics, and his dedication to clear exposition, having also written *Concrete Mathematics: A Foundation for Computer Science*.
