# Lynn Conway
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist who made major contributions to VLSI chip design, architecture, and computer science education. After beginning her education at MIT and Columbia, she worked at IBM on the early Advanced Computing Systems project, contributing to the first superscalar designs. Facing discrimination after revealing her plan to transition as a transgender woman in 1968, she was fired by IBM but later publicly recognized and honored. Post-transition, Conway advanced VLSI design at Xerox PARC, collaborating on the influential Mead–Conway methodology and pioneering the MOSIS system for rapid chip prototyping. She also taught at MIT, influenced DARPA computing initiatives, and held professorship at the University of Michigan. Conway advocated for transgender rights, criticized biases that obscure marginalized innovators (coining the "Conway effect"), and helped update IEEE codes for LGBT inclusion. She received numerous honors including membership in the National Academy of Engineering and IBM’s Lifetime Achievement Award.

# Donald Knuth
Donald Knuth is a foundational figure in computer science, known especially for his multivolume work *The Art of Computer Programming*. Born in 1938 in Milwaukee, he studied physics before transitioning to mathematics and computing at Case Institute and Caltech, developing compilers early in his career. He pioneered the rigorous analysis of algorithms, popularized the notion of "analysis of algorithms" as a distinct field, and contributed to digital typography via his creation of TeX and Metafont. Knuth also introduced literate programming, viewing code as literature to be explained for human readers. An accomplished organist and author in both computer science and religion, he has been honored with the Turing Award, National Medal of Science, Kyoto Prize, and many others. Known for meticulousness, he famously rewarded readers who found errors in his books.

# Kurt Gödel
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) was a mathematician and logician renowned for his incompleteness theorems, showing fundamental limits to axiomatic systems in mathematics. Born in Austria-Hungary and later becoming a U.S. citizen, he worked primarily at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. Gödel’s work revolutionized logic, proving that any sufficiently powerful consistent system cannot be complete, and that its consistency cannot be proven within itself. He also contributed to set theory and general relativity, discovering solutions permitting time travel (Gödel metric). With a close friendship with Einstein, he made philosophical contributions, including a formal ontological argument for God’s existence. His later life was marked by mental health struggles culminating in death by self-starvation.

# Claude Shannon
Claude Shannon (1916–2001) was an electrical engineer and mathematician who founded information theory, profoundly impacting digital communication. His 1937 master’s thesis applied Boolean algebra to switching circuits, laying the groundwork for digital circuit design. His 1948 landmark paper, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, introduced concepts like entropy to quantify information. During WWII, he worked on cryptography and fire-control systems. Shannon also made early AI contributions, building a mechanical mouse learning device (Theseus) and pioneering computer chess programming. Known as a polymath, he invented numerous mechanical gadgets and excelled in juggling and unicycling. Recognized with many awards, he is regarded as the father of the digital revolution.

# Ismail al-Jazari
Al-Jazari (1136–1206) was a medieval Muslim polymath and engineer from Upper Mesopotamia noted for his innovations in mechanical engineering and automata. Serving as chief engineer at the Artuklu Palace, he authored *The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, a practical manual describing inventive machines he built himself. Al-Jazari introduced key mechanical components such as the camshaft, crankshaft with connecting rods, escapement mechanisms, and early valves used in water-raising machines and elaborate clocks. His automata included humanoid devices like a drink-serving waitress and musical robot bands. His water clocks integrated hydropower and early feedback controls and influenced later engineering and robotics traditions.

# Edsger W. Dijkstra
Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist and pioneer of structured programming and concurrent computing. Starting as the Netherlands’ first programmer, he developed foundational algorithms (notably Dijkstra’s shortest path algorithm) and contributed to programming language theory. Dijkstra was influential in advancing the discipline of programming towards mathematical rigor, famously criticizing the GOTO statement. He designed the THE operating system, helped develop ALGOL compilers, and was an outspoken and sometimes controversial figure. He authored over 1,300 essays (EWD reports), many circulated privately. He held academic positions in the Netherlands, the Burroughs Corporation, and the University of Texas at Austin. His work shaped modern software engineering and theoretical computer science.

# Geoffrey Hinton
Geoffrey Hinton (born 1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist seminal in the development of artificial neural networks and deep learning. After earning a PhD in AI from Edinburgh, he held positions in the UK, US, and Canada, becoming a professor at the University of Toronto and a CIFAR Fellow. Hinton popularized backpropagation, co-invented Boltzmann machines, and contributed to many neural network architectures including capsule networks. He co-founded DNNresearch, acquired by Google. His students include many leading AI researchers. Despite his foundational role, Hinton has recently raised concerns about AI risks and resigned from Google to speak freely. In 2024, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for machine learning breakthroughs.

# Herbert A. Simon
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was an influential polymath in economics, psychology, and computer science. Trained in political science and economics at University of Chicago, he pioneered theories of decision-making, organizational behavior, and bounded rationality, challenging idealized economic models. Simon was a founding figure in artificial intelligence, co-creating early AI programs such as the Logic Theory Machine and General Problem Solver. He contributed to cognitive psychology with models of human learning and expertise. His work earned many honors, including the Turing Award, the Nobel Prize in Economics, and the National Medal of Science. Simon’s interdisciplinary research transformed social sciences and computational approaches to human cognition.

# George Antheil
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer known for his experimental, mechanistic musical style epitomized in *Ballet Mécanique*. Raised by German immigrant parents, he studied piano and composition, gaining notoriety in Europe for his percussive, machine-inspired works. Antheil collaborated with Hedy Lamarr to invent a frequency-hopping spread spectrum system intended to secure radio-controlled torpedoes during WWII, a precursor to modern wireless communications technologies. Later in life, he worked extensively in film scoring in Hollywood. Despite mixed critical reception in his career, his innovations influenced both music and technology.

# Per Brinch Hansen
Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a Danish computer scientist known for foundational work in operating systems, concurrent programming, and parallel computing. After studying electrical engineering and working at Regnecentralen, he led the design of the RC 4000 multiprogramming system, introducing the kernel concept and separation of mechanism and policy. He coined and implemented monitors for concurrency, created Concurrent Pascal, and authored the first textbook on operating systems and concurrent programming. Hansen held faculty positions at Caltech, USC, and Syracuse, advancing distributed computing and parallel programming paradigms. He was recognized with IEEE Fellow and Computer Pioneer awards for his engineering rigor and explanatory clarity.

# Ramon Llull
Ramon Llull (1232/3–1316) was a Majorcan philosopher, logician, and Christian missionary, famous for developing *Llull’s Art*, a combinatorial logical system aimed at proving religious truths and promoting conversion. After a series of visions in 1263, Llull abandoned his courtly life to study languages and theology deeply. His Art employed diagrams and combinatorial methods to analyze divine attributes, producing what may be regarded as an early algorithmic logic. He pioneered efforts in missionary education and influenced Renaissance thinkers such as Nicholas of Cusa and Leibniz. Beatified in 1847, his work foreshadowed early computation theory and electoral systems.

# Al-Khwarizmi
Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī (c. 780–850) was a Persian mathematician and astronomer whose works laid the foundations of algebra and helped disseminate Hindu-Arabic numerals in the West. Serving in Baghdad’s House of Wisdom, he authored *Al-Jabr*, introducing systematic solutions to linear and quadratic equations, and popularizing the method of “restoration and balancing.” His name gave rise to the terms “algebra” and “algorithm.” He compiled astronomical tables (Zīj as-Sindhind), improved geographic coordinates, and contributed to trigonometry and calendrical computations. His Latin-translated works were pivotal for European science development.

# George Boole
George Boole (1815–1864) was a self-taught English mathematician who founded Boolean algebra, the algebraic system underpinning digital logic and computing. Born to a shoemaker, Boole overcame limited formal education to become a professor at Queen’s College, Cork. His work unified logic and algebra, enabling the application of mathematical methods to logic and probability. Boole’s algebra formalized logical operations, which Claude Shannon later applied to digital circuits. His legacy is central to computer science, reflected in data types, circuit design, and programming languages. He was a devout thinker blending logic with religious philosophy.

# Gottlob Frege
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German mathematician, logician, and philosopher who founded modern predicate logic and analytic philosophy of language. Frege introduced quantified variables, function-argument analysis, and the distinction between sense and reference, enabling precise logical representation. His work aimed to ground arithmetic in logic (logicism) but was undermined by Russell’s paradox. Though unrecognized in his lifetime, his ideas influenced Russell, Wittgenstein, and subsequent logic. Frege also contributed to the philosophy of mathematics, semantics, and the foundations of geometry. Despite his brilliance, he held problematic antisemitic views in his later years.

# Kenneth E. Iverson
Kenneth Iverson (1920–2004) was a Canadian mathematician and computer scientist, inventor of the APL programming language noted for its array-processing capabilities and concise notation. Starting at Harvard teaching automatic data processing, Iverson developed a notation blending matrix algebra and higher-order functions to describe computations clearly. Joining IBM, he formalized and implemented APL, aiding in description and teaching of complex systems. Later, he developed the J programming language, a Unicode-based successor to APL with powerful programming constructs. Iverson was awarded the Turing Award in 1979 and is celebrated for making mathematical notation a tool of thought in computing.

# Grace Hopper
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was a pioneering American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral known for developing the first compiler and promoting the idea of programming languages close to English. Educated in mathematics and physics, she worked on Harvard’s Mark I computer during WWII and later contributed to the development of UNIVAC and COBOL, one of the first high-level programming languages. Hopper advocated programming standardization and computer education, became a prominent speaker, and popularized the term “debugging.” Honored with many awards, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom, she inspired generations of women in computing and was nicknamed “Grandma COBOL.”

# Jacek Karpiński
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and pioneer in early computer design. Despite wartime injuries, he studied electrical engineering and developed Poland’s first transistor-based differential analyzer (AKAT-1) and built early computers including the KAR-65 and the advanced minicomputer K-202, which featured several innovations like memory segmentation and paging. His work was hampered by political and economic obstacles in communist Poland. Karpiński also built an early perceptron for pattern recognition. After facing opposition and censorship, he spent later years in agriculture and abroad. Posthumously honored, his legacy remains as a bold innovator in Eastern European computing history.

# Federico Faggin
Federico Faggin (born 1941) is an Italian-American physicist and engineer celebrated for inventing the first commercial microprocessor and pioneering silicon-gate MOS technology. Starting at Olivetti and then SGS-Fairchild, he developed silicon gate technology crucial to modern integrated circuits. At Intel, he led the design of the Intel 4004, the world’s first single-chip microprocessor, and later created the 8080 and Zilog Z80 CPUs, foundational for early personal computers. Faggin co-founded Synaptics, advancing neural networks and touchpad technology, and promoted consciousness studies through his foundation. He received numerous honors, including the Kyoto Prize and National Medal of Technology.
