**Kenneth E. Iverson**  
Kenneth E. Iverson, a Canadian mathematician and computer scientist (1920–2004), pioneered the development of the programming languages **APL** and **J**. Born in Alberta, he served in the Canadian military during WWII and earned degrees from Queen’s University and Harvard University. At Harvard, he developed a novel mathematical notation that evolved into APL. At IBM (1960–1980), he founded the Philadelphia Scientific Center, promoted APL in education and scientific fields, and authored foundational works on APL and programming languages. He advanced APL through key innovations such as the rank operator and "dictionary APL," which defined syntax via a parse table. In 1988, he invented the "fork" operator, enabling expressive function composition. After retiring in 1987, he co-developed the **J programming language** with Eugene McDonnell and Arthur Whitney, inspired by the need for a simple, accessible, and powerful APL dialect. J features natural language terms (e.g., noun, verb, sentence) and uses 7-bit ASCII characters instead of special symbols, enabling powerful tacit programming through forks. Iverson co-founded **Jsoftware Inc.** (originally Iverson Software Inc.) in 1990, which became the sole focus of the company. He authored numerous publications on J, education, and language design, including *Programming in J*, *Tangible Math*, and *Mathematical Roots of J*. He received major honors, including the **Turing Award (1979)**, **IBM Fellow (1970)**, and an **honorary doctorate (1998)**; a moth species, *Agdistis iversoni*, was named after him in 2015.

**Al-Khwarizmi**  
Al-Khwarizmi, a 9th-century Persian mathematician, astronomer, and geographer from Khwarazm (modern-day Uzbekistan/Turkmenistan), made foundational contributions to algebra, trigonometry, and the Hindu-Arabic numeral system. Working at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, he systematized the solution of linear and quadratic equations using *al-jabr* (restoration) and *al-muqābala* (balancing), introducing a structured, elementary approach that laid the foundation for modern algebra. His work, *Kitāb al-jabr wal-muqābala*, marked a shift from geometric to algebraic mathematics, treating numbers and magnitudes as algebraic objects and emphasizing equations as independent entities. Though his work was rhetorical (using words rather than symbols), it was more accessible and systematic than earlier works by Diophantus or Indian mathematicians like Brahmagupta. In arithmetic, he popularized Hindu–Arabic numerals and algorithms for decimal calculations using a dust board (*takht*), which were later transmitted to Europe through Latin translations. His name gave rise to "algorismus," evolving into the modern term "algorithm." His influence helped transform European computation, replacing abacus-based methods and establishing algebra and arithmetic as central branches of mathematics. He also authored *Zīj as-Sindhind*, which advanced Islamic astronomy with accurate sine and cosine tables and planetary motion calculations, and *Kitāb Ṣūrat al-Arḍ*, which improved upon Ptolemy’s geography with more accurate coordinates and a correct depiction of oceans, influencing later Muslim cartography. He wrote on the Hebrew calendar, sundials, and astrolabes, and received honors including lunar and asteroid names.

**Jacek Karpiński**  
Jacek Karpiński, a Polish engineer and resistance fighter born in 1927 in Turin, was a pioneering computer engineer whose work on the **KAR-65** and **K-202** minicomputers demonstrated significant technical innovation. The KAR-65, Poland’s fastest computer at the time (1968), was a landmark achievement, though its promotion was censored and Karpiński faced political and institutional resistance. The K-202, a highly advanced minicomputer using cutting-edge microchips and innovative features such as memory segmentation and paging, was ahead of its time and outperformed competitors like the PDP-11. However, it never reached mass production due to political opposition, bureaucratic interference, and economic constraints—including resistance from established institutions like Mera and the rise of the Comecon Riad project. Karpiński also served as a secret informant for the Polish intelligence service, a role that later drew scrutiny. Despite commercial failure, his projects remain historically significant. He later moved to Switzerland, returned to Poland, and advised on computer science, receiving honors posthumously. The full scale and impact of his achievements remain debated, with some viewing his failure as a missed opportunity for Poland’s technological advancement. He is also referenced in Roman Bratny's novel *Lot ku ziemi*, where he is portrayed as Marek Zych, and discussed in several books and scholarly works, including *Geniusz i Świnie* by P. Lipiński, *Automaty liczą* by B. Kluska, *Informatyka bez złudzeń* by A. Targowski, *Lot ku ziemi* by R. Bratny, and a scholarly article by A. Kochajkiewicz on state actions against him.

**Gottlob Frege** (1848–1925)  
Gottlob Frege, a German logician and philosopher born in Wismar, laid the foundation for modern logic through his seminal work *Begriffsschrift* (1879), which introduced modern predicate logic, including quantified variables, enabling precise formalization of mathematical and logical statements. His key works include *Begriffsschrift* (1879), which introduced a formal system of logic; *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884), establishing arithmetic on logical principles; and *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* (1893–1903), where he developed logical foundations for number theory. He also made major contributions to philosophy, notably in "On Sense and Reference" and "Function and Concept." His logical investigations laid the groundwork for modern logic and analytic philosophy. Influenced by his father's linguistic work and teachers like Ernst Abbe and Hermann Lotze, Frege studied at Jena and Göttingen. He championed *logicism*, the view that arithmetic is reducible to logic, most notably in *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (1884) and *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* (1893–1903), where Basic Law V established a key principle about predicate extensions. His system in *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* was undermined by Russell's paradox, which exposed a flaw in Basic Law V. Frege responded with a hasty appendix, proposing modifications that were later shown to be inadequate; however, alternatives such as weakening Basic Law V or replacing it with Hume’s principle (leading to Frege’s theorem) have preserved much of his logical program. His contributions to the philosophy of language—especially the distinction between sense and reference, function and argument, and compositionality—shaped modern analytic philosophy. Though initially overlooked, his ideas gained prominence through students like Carnap, Russell, and Wittgenstein. A reclusive and introverted thinker, Frege made lasting contributions to logic, mathematics, and philosophy, despite personal and historical complexities, including antisemitic views and hostility toward democracy and liberalism revealed in his personal writings (*Nachlass*), which contrast with his public image as a rationalist.

**Grace Hopper**  
Grace Hopper, a pioneering computer scientist and U.S. Navy officer, made foundational contributions to programming and computer language development. As a child, she demonstrated a strong curiosity, famously dismantling alarm clocks and advocating for change. She earned degrees in mathematics and physics from Vassar and Yale, and joined the U.S. Navy during World War II as part of the WAVES, where she worked on early computing projects at Harvard. In the 1950s, she developed the first compiler and championed the creation of **COBOL**, a business-oriented programming language using natural English, which became widely adopted. She served as a key figure in standardizing programming languages and advocated for distributed computing systems. Hopper was known for popularizing the term **"debugging"** after a moth was found in a computer, a moment that became iconic in computing history. Post-retirement, she served as a goodwill ambassador, lecturing widely and mentoring young professionals. She received numerous awards, including the **Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016**, and is remembered through numerous honors: a U.S. Navy destroyer, a satellite, a supercomputer, a college, and a variety of academic and public institutions named after her. Her legacy endures in both technology and education, with ongoing recognition of her impact on computing and women in STEM.  
**Expanded Legacy:** She founded the **Grace Hopper Celebration of Women in Computing**, a major annual conference promoting women in technology. Her influence extends to Microsoft, where employee groups and scholarships honor her, and a named professorship exists at Yale. A major undersea internet cable, completed in 2021, bears her name. Her contributions are also recognized in popular culture, including the comic book *Secret Coders* and the Python library *matplotlib*, and are frequently highlighted in obituary notices from major tech publications.
