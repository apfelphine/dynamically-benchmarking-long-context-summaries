### Ramon Llull: Life, Logic, and Enduring Legacy

Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1315), a Majorcan Franciscan friar and philosopher, dedicated his life to missionary work and theological study, aiming to convert Muslims through linguistic education. His seminal contribution was **Llull's Art** (*Ars*), a system of universal logic developed in two phases: the **Quaternary Phase** (1274–1290) utilizing 16 divine principles, and the **Ternary Phase** (1290–1308), which streamlined the system to four main figures and nine principles. Central to this framework was the **doctrine of correlatives**, positing a threefold structure (agent, patient, act) to support proofs for the Trinity and Incarnation. Llull expressed these complex ideas through diverse genres—dialogues, proverbs, and novels—often in Catalan for accessibility, while integrating Islamic theology (e.g., Al-Ghazali) and alchemical concepts. Although initially condemned by the Inquisition and academia, his influence persisted through early modern proponents like Nicholas of Cusa and Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples, shaping Renaissance metaphysics, Leibniz's logic, and theological humanism. Despite failing to achieve canonization, Llull was beatified in 1847, and modern scholarship continues to recognize his pivotal role in Catalan literature and cognitive systems.

### Modern Recognition and Scientific Precursors

Llull's legacy extends significantly into the 20th and 21st centuries, where he has been recognized in art and literature. He inspired surrealists like Salvador Dalí, influenced literary works by Jorge Luis Borges, Aldous Huxley, and Paul Auster, and served as a major character in children's literature. Beyond artistic influence, his lost manuscripts, discovered in 2001, reveal a logical system that acts as a precursor to computer science and is credited with early concepts behind the Borda count and Condorcet criterion. His extensive corpus has been preserved and made accessible through multiple edited translations, including selections by Mark D. Johnston and Anthony Bonner, covering key texts such as *Ars Demonstrativa*, *Ars Brevis*, and *The Book of the Gentile and the Three Wise Men*.
