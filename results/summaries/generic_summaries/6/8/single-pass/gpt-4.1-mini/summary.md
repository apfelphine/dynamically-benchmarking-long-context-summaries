# Federico Faggin
Federico Faggin, an Italian physicist and innovator, pioneered silicon-gate technology (SGT) and led the design of the world's first single-chip microprocessor, the Intel 4004, leveraging SGT advancements. After starting at Olivetti and SGS-Fairchild, where he developed key MOS integrated circuits, he joined Intel in 1970, contributing foundational designs for early microprocessors like the 4004, 8008, 4040, and 8080. Later, Faggin co-founded Zilog, designing the Z80 microprocessor widely used in early personal computers. He also contributed to communication systems, co-founded Synaptics (introducing the touchpad), and revitalized Foveon with advanced image sensor technology. Passionate about consciousness, he founded a research foundation and developed a quantum-information-based theory of consciousness.

# Jacek Karpiński
Jacek Karpiński was a Polish computer engineer and resistance fighter during WWII, known for pioneering early Polish computers such as the transistor-based AKAT-1 and the innovative KAR-65 minicomputer. Despite technical excellence, political and bureaucratic obstacles limited widespread production of his advanced minicomputer K-202, comparable to contemporaries like DEC's PDP-11. Karpiński's career was marked by collaboration with Polish intelligence, censorship, and struggles with Communist-era authorities. He later worked in Switzerland and advised in Poland, and was posthumously honored for his contributions to computing despite controversies around his legacy.

# Grace Hopper
Grace Hopper was a pioneering American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral who developed the first compiler and significantly contributed to the creation of COBOL, an English-like programming language widely used in business applications. Her work in automatic programming languages facilitated making computing accessible beyond mathematicians. Hopper served extensively in World War II and post-war Navy programming groups, advocated for distributed computing, and helped standardize programming languages. She retired as one of the oldest serving Naval officers and later worked as a consultant for Digital Equipment Corporation until her death. Hopper's legacy includes numerous awards, honors, and institutions named after her, reflecting her foundational role in computer science and women's advancement in technology.

# Kenneth E. Iverson
Canadian mathematician Kenneth E. Iverson developed a powerful mathematical notation later formalized as the APL programming language, pioneering array programming and formal system description at Harvard and IBM. He contributed to the design and implementation of APL\360 and extended his work at I. P. Sharp Associates and Jsoftware, evolving APL into the J language emphasizing simplicity and expressiveness. Iverson was recognized with the Turing Award and IEEE honors, profoundly influencing programming language design, notation, and education.

# Gottlob Frege
Gottlob Frege was a German mathematician and logician, foundational to modern logic and analytic philosophy. He invented axiomatic predicate logic and introduced the distinction between sense and reference in language. His works aimed to ground arithmetic in logic (logicism), elaborated in Begriffsschrift and Grundgesetze der Arithmetik, though challenged by Russell's paradox. Frege’s theories deeply influenced 20th-century logic, philosophy, and mathematics despite his controversial antisemitic views late in life.

# George Boole
English mathematician George Boole founded Boolean algebra, providing the mathematical foundation for digital logic and computer science. Born in 1815, self-taught and a pioneer in symbolic logic, Boole extended Aristotle's logic and introduced an algebraic approach to logic operations used in digital circuits. His work was further developed by successors and instrumental in modern computing, notably influencing Claude Shannon. Boole's contributions span logic, differential equations, and probability theory.

# Al-Khwarizmi
Persian polymath Al-Khwārizmī (c.780–850) revolutionized mathematics by founding algebra (his book Al-Jabr) and popularizing Hindu-Arabic numerals, inspiring the term "algorithm." His work systematized solving linear and quadratic equations and enhanced trigonometric, astronomical, and geographical knowledge. As head of Baghdad’s House of Wisdom, he influenced mathematical practice in the Islamic world and medieval Europe. His legacy includes advances in arithmetic, astronomy, geography, and calendrical computation.

# Ramon Llull
Ramon Llull (1232/3–1315) was a Majorcan philosopher, logician, and missionary who developed an early combinatorial logical system called the Art, aimed at proving Christian doctrines and converting non-Christians. Influenced by Islamic philosophy, he created complex diagrammatic methods and authored dialogues, proverbs, and narrative works. Although his system was controversial and opposed by some ecclesiastical authorities, Llull inspired later thinkers including Leibniz and is considered a forerunner of computational logic.

# Per Brinch Hansen
Danish computer scientist Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a pioneer in operating systems, concurrent and parallel programming. He led the development of the RC 4000 multiprogramming system (introducing the kernel concept), first comprehensive textbooks on operating systems and concurrent programming, and created the first monitor notation. He developed concurrent programming languages like Concurrent Pascal and Joyce, influencing distributed systems and parallel computing. His work bridged theory and practical systems, earning him the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award.

# George Antheil
American composer George Antheil (1900–1959) was an avant-garde modernist known for his mechanical, percussive compositions like Ballet Mécanique. After early European notoriety, he worked in Hollywood scoring films and co-invented frequency-hopping spread spectrum communication with Hedy Lamarr during WWII. Their patent anticipated modern wireless encryption technologies. Antheil’s influence extended from early mechanical music innovation to pioneering secure communications.

# Herbert A. Simon
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was a multidisciplinary American scientist who contributed fundamentally to decision-making theory, artificial intelligence, economics, psychology, and organizational theory. He introduced concepts such as bounded rationality and satisficing in administrative decision-making, developed early AI programs like the Logic Theory Machine, and advanced understanding of human cognition. Awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics and the Turing Award, Simon’s work bridged social science, computational modeling, and cognitive psychology.

# Geoffrey Hinton
Geoffrey Hinton (b. 1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist, a pioneer of neural networks and deep learning. He helped popularize backpropagation algorithms, co-invented Boltzmann machines, and contributed foundational work in machine learning, including capsule networks and t-SNE visualization. A Turing Award laureate and 2024 Nobel laureate in Physics, Hinton has recently expressed concern over AI risks and advocates for cautious regulation. His research catalyzed the AI revolution and continues to influence AI theory and applications.

# Edsger W. Dijkstra
Dutch computer scientist Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a foundational figure in programming and computing theory. Known for promoting structured programming, he famously critiqued the GOTO statement. He developed Dijkstra’s algorithm for shortest paths, contributed to semaphores, and created the layered "THE" operating system. His prolific essays (EWDs) combined mathematical rigor with clarity and wit. Dijkstra shaped modern programming principles and computer science education.

# Ismail al-Jazari
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–1206) was a medieval Muslim inventor and engineer known for advancing mechanical engineering, especially automata and water-raising devices. He invented early camshafts, crankshafts, escapements, and water clocks with automated figures. His Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices documents numerous machines involving hydraulics and programmable automata for entertainment, ritual washing, and practical uses. Al-Jazari’s work influenced later engineering and robotics developments.

# Claude Shannon
Claude Shannon (1916–2001) was an American mathematician and engineer, founder of information theory and digital circuit design. His 1937 master's thesis established the application of Boolean algebra to relay circuits, underpinning digital electronics. In 1948, he published the seminal "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," introducing concepts like information entropy. Shannon also contributed to coding, cryptography, artificial intelligence, and computer chess. His work laid the foundations of the digital age, influencing communications, computing, and AI.

# Kurt Gödel
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) was an Austrian-American logician and mathematician best known for his incompleteness theorems, which proved fundamental limits on formal axiomatic systems, showing that any sufficiently powerful system cannot be both complete and consistent. His work revolutionized logic, mathematics, and philosophy. Gödel also contributed to set theory, cosmology (Gödel metric), and ontological arguments for God’s existence. A close friend of Einstein, he faced mental health struggles late in life and died of self-starvation following his wife's hospitalization.

# Donald Knuth
Donald Knuth (b. 1938) is an American computer scientist and mathematician noted for authoring the multi-volume "The Art of Computer Programming," a foundational work on algorithm analysis and programming techniques. He pioneered algorithmic analysis, developed the TeX digital typesetting system, and introduced literate programming. Knuth contributed broadly to programming language design, combinatorics, and mathematics. An awarded Turing laureate, he is recognized for his high standards in computer science and is considered one of the field's greatest scholars.

# Lynn Conway
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and transgender activist who transformed VLSI chip design through collaboration with Carver Mead, inventing scalable design rules and the MOSIS prototyping service. After transitioning in 1968 and overcoming career obstacles, she led innovations in computer architecture and chip design at IBM, Xerox PARC, and DARPA, significantly influencing microelectronics and computer engineering education. Conway actively advocated for transgender rights and inclusion in tech. Her work catalyzed the modern semiconductor industry and democratized chip design.
