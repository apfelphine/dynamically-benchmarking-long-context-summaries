### George Antheil: Avant-Garde Composer, Inventor, and Eccentric Figure

George Antheil, a German-American composer born to immigrants in Trenton, NJ, was a pioneering figure in both music and technology. Despite dropping out of high school, he began piano studies at six under the influence of Franz Liszt, Ernest Bloch, and T.S. Eliot. Financially supported by Mary Louise Curtis Bok, he moved to Europe in 1922, initially meeting Igor Stravinsky in Berlin before settling in Paris in 1923 to immerse himself in modernist circles alongside James Joyce and T.S. Eliot.

Antheil gained notoriety for his aggressive, percussive style and a staged riot during a debut performance at the Ballets suédois. He is best known for the avant-garde piece *Ballet Mécanique*, which sought to create radical, machine-like music that often alienated audiences, contributing to a decline in his reputation during his lifetime. While critics found his technique too loud, he shifted toward more tonal, romantic styles after leaving Germany in the 1930s due to Nazi persecution. This shift led him to become a prolific film composer in Hollywood, despite facing industry hostility. Beyond music, Antheil was an author, critic, and columnist with eccentric interests in endocrinology and "glandular criminology."

His most significant contribution to science was the 1922 patent for frequency-hopping radio technology, a concept utilizing a player piano roll to encrypt signals for torpedoes. This innovation predated practical implementation in the US until 1962 and shares concepts with modern spread-spectrum systems like Bluetooth; however, it was initially rejected by the U.S. Navy. Antheil also collaborated with Hedy Lamarr to develop this technology. His musical legacy includes works such as *Ballet Mécanique* and *Third Sonata for Violin*, and he is remembered for leaving a distinguished circle of students and a complex personality reflected in literature. George Antheil died of a heart attack in Manhattan and is buried in Trenton, New Jersey.
