# Per Brinch Hansen
Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a Danish computer scientist known for pioneering work in operating systems, concurrent programming, and distributed computing. Educated in electrical engineering at the Technical University of Denmark, he developed the RC 4000 multiprogramming system introducing the OS kernel and the separation of mechanism and policy. He authored foundational texts like *Operating System Principles* (1973) and *The Architecture of Concurrent Programs* (1977). At multiple institutions, including Caltech, USC, and Syracuse University, he advanced concurrent languages such as Concurrent Pascal and Edison, and developed new programming paradigms for parallel computing. His work influenced microkernel architectures and monitor concepts in concurrent programming. He received the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award in 2002.

# Al-Khwarizmi
A Persian polymath of the 9th century, Al-Khwarizmi laid the foundations of algebra and significantly influenced arithmetic, astronomy, and geography. His seminal work, *Al-Jabr*, introduced systematic solutions to linear and quadratic equations, giving rise to the term "algebra." He popularized the Hindu–Arabic numeral system through his writings, which introduced the concept of algorithm to the Western world. His astronomical tables and geographic coordinates refined Ptolemy’s data, contributing to map-making and timekeeping. Al-Khwarizmi’s works profoundly impacted medieval European science and mathematics.

# Herbert A. Simon
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was an American polymath whose interdisciplinary research spanned economics, psychology, computer science, and artificial intelligence. He developed the theory of bounded rationality and satisficing in decision-making, challenging classical economic assumptions of perfect rationality. Simon co-created pioneering AI programs like the Logic Theory Machine and General Problem Solver, and contributed to cognitive psychology with models of human problem solving and expertise acquisition. His extensive writing includes *Administrative Behavior* and *The Sciences of the Artificial*. He received awards including the Turing Award (1975) and Nobel Prize in Economics (1978).

# Gottlob Frege
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German logician and philosopher who founded modern predicate logic and analytic philosophy. His *Begriffsschrift* (1879) introduced quantified variables and rigorous logical notation, laying the groundwork for axiomatic systems and formal proof. Frege championed logicism, showing mathematics could be derived from logic, as elaborated in *The Foundations of Arithmetic* and *Basic Laws of Arithmetic*. Although his system was undermined by Russell’s paradox, later work salvaged much of his framework. Frege also made seminal contributions to philosophy of language, including the distinction between sense and reference.

# Edsger W. Dijkstra
Dutch computer scientist Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a pioneer in algorithms and programming methodology. Working initially at the Mathematisch Centrum in Amsterdam, he developed Dijkstra’s algorithm for shortest paths and contributed to the design of ALGOL 60 compilers. He championed structured programming, famously critiquing the GOTO statement, and developed influential concepts such as semaphores and self-stabilization in distributed systems. Later positions included professorship at Eindhoven and the University of Texas. Known for his precise writing style and skepticism of software engineering’s ambitions, he received the Turing Award in 1972.

# Grace Hopper
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was a pioneering American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral. She developed the first compilers and was instrumental in the development of COBOL, one of the earliest high-level programming languages designed to be close to English. Hopper contributed to the programming of the Mark I computer during WWII, helped standardize programming languages, and advocated for user-friendly computing. Post-retirement, she served as a consultant for Digital Equipment Corporation. Famous anecdotes include popularizing the term "debugging." She received numerous honors and has enduring legacies in naval and computing history.

# Claude Shannon
Claude Shannon (1916–2001) is the father of information theory. His 1937 master’s thesis applied Boolean algebra to electrical circuits, establishing the foundation of digital circuit design. During WWII, he worked on cryptography and fire-control systems. His landmark 1948 paper, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*, introduced the concept of information entropy and founded information theory, revolutionizing telecommunications and computing. Shannon also contributed to artificial intelligence, designing early learning machines, and is remembered for inventions spanning juggling machines to a wearable computer. His work underpins the digital age.

# Federico Faggin
Federico Faggin (born 1941) is an Italian-American physicist and engineer renowned for pioneering the silicon-gate MOS technology and designing the world’s first commercial microprocessor, the Intel 4004. After developing MOS circuits at SGS-Fairchild, he led microprocessor projects at Intel and later co-founded Zilog, creating the Z80 microprocessor. Faggin also co-founded Synaptics, advancing touchpad and touchscreen technologies. His recent interests include the quantum theory of consciousness, and he established a foundation supporting consciousness research.

# Donald Knuth
Donald Knuth (born 1938) is an American computer scientist known as the "father of the analysis of algorithms." Author of *The Art of Computer Programming*, he profoundly shaped computer science literature and methodology. He invented TeX and METAFONT for high-quality digital typesetting and introduced literate programming. Knuth’s work spans algorithms, programming languages, and mathematical writing. A recipient of the Turing Award and National Medal of Science, he is also a Lutheran organist and has contributed to recreational mathematics.

# Jacek Karpiński
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and inventor notable for creating early transistor-based analog computers and minicomputers during the 1950s and 1970s. Despite innovative designs such as the transistorized differential analyzer AKAT-1 and the modular minicomputer K-202—which featured advanced technologies like segmented memory and asynchronous operation—his work faced political and institutional opposition in communist Poland, limiting widespread adoption. Karpiński also contributed to early artificial neural networks (perceptron) and scanning devices.

# Lynn Conway
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was an American computer scientist and transgender rights activist who revolutionized VLSI chip design. After early work on IBM’s Advanced Computing Systems and transitioning gender, she pioneered scalable, dimensionless design rules and founded the multiproject wafer (MPW) technology at Xerox PARC. Conway co-authored the influential *Introduction to VLSI Systems* textbook and helped establish the MOSIS system for rapid chip prototyping. Later, she contributed to DARPA’s Strategic Computing Initiative and academia. Conway advocated for inclusivity and transgender rights within technology fields.

# Kenneth E. Iverson
Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004), a Canadian mathematician and computer scientist, developed the APL programming language and notation, emphasizing array programming and concise symbolic representation. After education at Queen’s University and Harvard, he worked at IBM, contributing to the formal description of System/360 and its APL implementation. Later, Iverson extended APL at I. P. Sharp Associates and created the J programming language, designed for teaching and broad use. He received the Turing Award in 1979 for his influential work on programming languages.

# Kurt Gödel
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) was an Austrian-American logician and mathematician famous for his incompleteness theorems, proving that any sufficiently powerful formal system cannot be both complete and consistent. His foundational work transformed mathematical logic and had profound philosophical implications. Gödel also contributed to set theory, demonstrating the relative consistency of the axiom of choice and the continuum hypothesis. He spent most of his career at Princeton’s Institute for Advanced Study, forming a close friendship with Albert Einstein. Gödel struggled with mental health issues later in life and died from self-starvation.

# George Boole
George Boole (1815–1864) was a self-taught English mathematician and logician who founded Boolean algebra, laying the groundwork for modern digital logic and computer science. Despite limited formal education, he developed a symbolic logic system extending Aristotelian logic and worked on differential equations and probability. Boole served as the first professor of mathematics at Queen’s College, Cork. His work was later foundational to Claude Shannon’s application of Boolean logic in circuit design. Boole’s legacy endures in mathematical logic, computer science, and programming.

# Ismail al-Jazari
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–1206) was a medieval Muslim polymath and mechanical engineer known for designing innovative automata, water clocks, and water-raising devices. Serving at the Artuklu Palace, he authored *The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, describing numerous mechanical innovations including early camshafts, crankshafts, escapements, and valves. His automata included humanoid robots like drink-serving figures and musical ensembles. Al-Jazari's work advanced practical mechanical controls and inspired later engineering developments in clockmaking and robotics.

# Ramon Llull
Ramon Llull (1232/3–1315/6) was a Majorcan philosopher, logician, and missionary whose combinatorial system known as *The Art* attempted to provide universal logic to prove theological truths and convert non-Christians. He developed innovative symbolic and diagrammatic methods and authored dialogues, proverbs, and allegorical fiction to communicate his ideas. Llull’s works integrated Christian, Jewish, and Islamic intellectual traditions. Although controversial in his time, his ideas influenced later figures such as Nicholas of Cusa and Leibniz and have been recognized as precursors to computational logic.

# Geoffrey Hinton
Geoffrey Hinton (born 1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist considered a founding figure in deep learning and neural networks. He contributed foundational work on backpropagation, Boltzmann machines, distributed representations, and capsule networks. Hinton co-founded DNNresearch, acquired by Google, and has held positions at University of Toronto and Google Brain. He won the 2018 Turing Award and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for advances enabling machine learning via artificial neural networks. Recently, Hinton has voiced concerns about AI risks and advocated for regulation.

# George Antheil
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer known for his mechanistic, percussive musical style exemplified in *Ballet Mécanique*. He was a Dadaist and futurist figure active in Europe and the U.S. Antheil collaborated with Hedy Lamarr on an early frequency-hopping spread spectrum invention during World War II, which later influenced wireless communications. Later in life, he composed film scores in Hollywood, wrote memoirs, and engaged in varied artistic and scientific pursuits. His legacy combines innovative music with pioneering technology.
