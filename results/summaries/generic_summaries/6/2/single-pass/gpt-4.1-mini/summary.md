# Edsger W. Dijkstra
Dijkstra, born in Rotterdam, was a pioneering computer scientist who transitioned from theoretical physics to programming in the 1950s. He became the Netherlands’ first official programmer, contributed to ALGOL 60 compiler development, and created the influential THE operating system while at Eindhoven University of Technology. Later, at Burroughs Corporation and the University of Texas at Austin, he produced a prolific series of essays (EWDs) and advanced formal methods in computing. Known for his rigorous teaching style and skepticism of computers in personal work, he strongly advocated structured programming, famously criticizing the GOTO statement. Dijkstra won the Turing Award in 1972 and is known for key contributions like Dijkstra’s algorithm, semaphores, and early operating system design.

# Herbert A. Simon
Simon was an American polymath born in 1916, renowned for his foundational work in decision-making, artificial intelligence (AI), economics, psychology, and organizational theory. He developed the theory of bounded rationality, emphasizing human cognitive limits in rational choice and introducing satisficing as a decision strategy. Simon co-created early AI programs such as the Logic Theory Machine and General Problem Solver, and collaborated on theories of human problem solving and expertise. His research influenced microeconomics and behavioral economics and earned him the 1978 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics and the 1975 Turing Award for AI contributions.

# Claude Shannon
Known as the "father of information theory," Shannon revolutionized communication and digital technology. After graduating from MIT, he formalized Boolean algebra’s application to switching circuits in his 1937 master’s thesis, laying the foundation for digital circuit design and computing. His 1948 seminal paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," introduced the concept of information entropy, fundamentally shaping telecommunications and data compression. He also contributed to cryptography, AI (notably with the mechanical mouse Theseus), and invented spread spectrum frequency hopping during WWII with Hedy Lamarr. Shannon’s work underpins modern digital communication and computing, earning him multiple honors including the National Medal of Science.

# Per Brinch Hansen
Danish computer scientist Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a pioneer in operating systems, concurrent programming, and parallel computing. He led development of the RC 4000 multiprogramming system introducing the kernel concept and separation of mechanism and policy, and proposed the first monitor notation for concurrent programming. Author of the first operating systems textbook and innovator of languages like Concurrent Pascal, Edison, and Joyce, he contributed to the formalization and practical implementation of concurrency and distributed computing. His career spanned Regnecentralen, Carnegie Mellon, Caltech, USC, University of Copenhagen, and Syracuse University, where he established influential research and teaching programs.

# Donald Knuth
Donald Knuth, born in 1938, is a seminal figure in computer science best known for authoring *The Art of Computer Programming*, a comprehensive multi-volume work foundational to algorithm theory. Educated at Case Institute of Technology and Caltech, Knuth pioneered compiler construction and coined the term “analysis of algorithms.” He created the TeX typesetting system and popularized literate programming—integrating code and documentation. Knuth’s work spans mathematics, computer science, and digital typography. He has received numerous prestigious awards including the Turing Award (1974) and National Medal of Science.

# Al-Khwarizmi
Al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–850) was a Persian polymath whose works established the foundations for algebra and introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals to the Islamic world and Europe. His book *Al-Jabr* formalized systematic solutions to linear and quadratic equations, giving rise to the term “algebra.” He also contributed to astronomy, geography, and arithmetic algorithms. His Latinized name led to the term “algorithm,” reflecting his influence on computation. Appointed head of Baghdad’s House of Wisdom library, al-Khwarizmi’s texts shaped medieval scientific thought and the development of mathematics.

# Lynn Conway
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and transgender activist whose innovations revolutionized VLSI chip design and digital system architecture. After early contributions to IBM’s Advanced Computing Systems, Conway’s collaboration with Carver Mead at Xerox PARC led to the Mead–Conway VLSI design methodology and the creation of the MOSIS chip fabrication service, transforming semiconductor design and production. She held faculty positions at MIT and University of Michigan, influenced internet technologies, and later advocated for transgender rights, challenging bias in tech. IBM publicly apologized for firing her during her gender transition.

# Gottlob Frege
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925), a German logician and philosopher, founded modern predicate logic by introducing quantified variables and formalizing logical inference in his *Begriffsschrift* (1879). He sought to base arithmetic on logic, pioneering logicism, and introduced the sense-reference distinction in semantics. Despite initial obscurity, his work greatly influenced analytic philosophy, mathematics, and logic, underpinning developments by Russell, Gödel, and Tarski. Frege’s system encountered Russell’s paradox, challenging its foundations. Known for his intellectual rigor, he is considered a key figure in 20th-century philosophy and logic.

# Kurt Gödel
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978), an Austrian-American logician, is renowned for his incompleteness theorems proving that any sufficiently powerful axiomatic system cannot be both complete and consistent—fundamentally altering mathematical logic. A student of the Vienna Circle, Gödel also proved the completeness of first-order logic and showed the consistency of the axiom of choice and continuum hypothesis relative to set theory. He emigrated to the U.S., becoming an Institute for Advanced Study faculty member and close friend of Einstein. His work extended into philosophy and general relativity (Gödel metric). Gödel suffered from mental illness in later years and died in Princeton.

# Grace Hopper
Rear Admiral Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was a pioneering computer scientist and Navy officer instrumental in developing the early programming language COBOL and automatic compilers. A mathematics professor before WWII, she joined the Navy Reserve in 1943 and worked on programming the Harvard Mark I. Hopper advocated English-like programming languages, leading to FLOW-MATIC and influencing COBOL’s creation. She promoted software standardization and best practices in programming throughout her career. Known for popularizing the term “debugging,” Hopper retired as a rear admiral and was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016.

# Ismail al-Jazari
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–1206) was a medieval Muslim polymath and engineer best known for his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, which described innovative water-raising machines, automata, mechanical controls, and clocks. He pioneered engineering concepts such as the crankshaft, camshaft, escapement mechanisms, and crank-slider mechanisms centuries before the industrial revolution. His automata included humanoid figures and musical robots operated by water power. Al-Jazari’s work laid foundational ideas in robotics, hydraulics, and mechanical design in the Islamic Golden Age.

# Federico Faggin
Federico Faggin (born 1941) is an Italian-American physicist and engineer who led the development of the first commercial microprocessor, Intel’s 4004, using his pioneering silicon-gate MOS transistor technology and novel chip design methodologies. He co-founded Zilog and designed the influential Z80 microprocessor. Faggin also co-founded Synaptics, popularizing touchpads and touchscreens. His career spans Olivetti, Fairchild Semiconductor, Intel, Zilog, Synaptics, and Foveon, with extensive contributions to integrated circuit technology, microprocessor architecture, and the study of consciousness in later years.

# Geoffrey Hinton
Geoffrey Hinton (born 1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist notable for foundational contributions to artificial neural networks and deep learning. Educated at Cambridge and Edinburgh, he helped revive neural networks through backpropagation, developed Boltzmann machines, and co-founded DNNresearch acquired by Google. Hinton has advanced representation learning and capsule networks. He won the 2018 Turing Award and 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for machine learning breakthroughs. Recently, he resigned from Google citing AI risks and advocates for ethical AI development.

# Jacek Karpiński
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and pioneer in transistor-based computing. Despite wartime injuries and political challenges, he developed early analog analyzers (AKAT-1), one of the first perceptrons, and highly innovative minicomputers (KAR-65 and K-202) featuring memory segmentation and asynchronous operation ahead of contemporaries. His career was marred by political obstacles and controversies during communist Poland, limiting wider adoption of his innovations. He later worked in Switzerland and advised Polish economic reforms before passing away in 2010.

# George Antheil
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer and inventor known for his mechanized, percussive music embodying industrial modernism. His seminal work *Ballet Mécanique* (1920s) combined piano rolls, airplane propellers, and percussion in a radical performance. Later, he composed film scores in Hollywood and, with Hedy Lamarr, patented frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology during WWII, foundational for modern wireless communications. Antheil’s career spanned modernist music, film, and technological innovation.

# Kenneth E. Iverson
Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004) was a Canadian computer scientist and mathematician known for creating the APL programming language, distinguished by its concise array-oriented notation. Educated at Queen’s University and Harvard, he worked at Harvard, IBM (contributing to System/360 specification and APL implementation), and later I.P. Sharp Associates and Jsoftware. Iverson’s notation influenced programming language design, formal system descriptions, and education. He received the 1979 Turing Award and was pivotal in advancing programming language theory and notation.

# Ramon Llull
Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1315) was a Majorcan philosopher, logician, and missionary credited with creating a combinatorial system of logic called the Art, aimed at converting non-Christians through rational discourse. Inspired by divine visions, he developed a universal logic based on divine attributes and their combinations, expressed through letters and figures, laying groundwork precursing computational logic. Llull authored dialogues, proverbs, and encyclopedic trees, influenced by Islamic thought, and advocated linguistic education for missionaries. Beatified in 1847, his work impacted philosophy, theology, and early algorithmic thought.

# George Boole
George Boole (1815–1864) was a largely self-taught English mathematician and logician who founded Boolean algebra, providing the algebraic framework to formalize logic operations. His work combined mathematics with logic, influencing probability theory and laying foundations for digital circuit design. Boole's algebra underpins modern computing and information theory. He served as the first professor of mathematics at Queen’s College Cork, Ireland, married Mary Everest, and was recognized posthumously for his lasting impact on computer science and logic.
