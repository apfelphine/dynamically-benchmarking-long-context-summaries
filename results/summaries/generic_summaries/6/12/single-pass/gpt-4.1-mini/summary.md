# Ramon Llull
Ramon Llull (1232/33–1315/16), born in Palma to a wealthy family, experienced spiritual visions around 1263 that transformed him from a troubadour to a devout Christian missionary. He dedicated himself to converting Muslims and promoting linguistic education, founding a hermitage and pioneering Arabic studies in Europe. His life's work centered on the creation of the *Art*, a universal combinatorial logic system designed to prove theological truths, most notably about the Christian Trinity, utilizing letters, diagrams, and an innovative threefold "correlatives" framework. Llull authored dialogues, tree diagrams, proverbs, and narrative novels aimed at broad dissemination of his method. Though initially met with resistance, his ideas influenced later scholars and saw revival particularly in Spain. He was beatified in 1847.

# George Antheil
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer known for his machine-inspired, percussive piano music such as *Ballet Mécanique*. Raised in a German immigrant family, he studied under European modernists and gained notoriety in Berlin and Paris’s artistic circles. His most famous work was initially met with controversy and riot but influenced contemporaries including Satie and Stravinsky. Later, Antheil worked as a Hollywood film composer, scoring over 30 films, and explored diverse interests such as writing, radio critique, and relationship advice. During WWII, he co-invented a frequency-hopping radio technology with actress Hedy Lamarr to secure torpedo guidance systems, a precursor to modern spread-spectrum communications. Antheil died of a heart attack in New York.

# George Boole
George Boole (1815–1864), an English mathematician largely self-taught beyond primary schooling, became the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork. He laid the foundations of symbolic logic and algebra, developing Boolean algebra that models logical reasoning with algebraic structures—a cornerstone of modern digital computing. His works encompass studies in differential equations, probability, and logic, notably formalizing logic’s "universe of discourse" and addressing challenges in logical addition. He died from pneumonia after lecturing while wet. Boole’s legacy includes a major influence on logic, computer science, and digital circuit design, and he is commemorated worldwide.

# Geoffrey Hinton
Geoffrey Hinton (born 1947) is a pioneering British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist, renowned as a founding figure of deep learning and neural networks. Educated at Cambridge and Edinburgh, he contributed foundational work on backpropagation, Boltzmann machines, and capsule networks. Co-director of influential AI research programs and early leader in neural network commercialization, Hinton resigned from Google in 2023 to speak freely about AI risks. He has received numerous honors including the Turing Award (2018) and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for his contributions to machine learning. Hinton recently expressed concerns about AI’s potential existential risks, economic impacts, and advocated regulatory oversight. His family lineage links back to George Boole and notable scientists.

# Kenneth E. Iverson
Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004), a Canadian mathematician and computer scientist, invented the APL programming language, pioneering array processing notations and functional programming concepts. He studied at Queen’s University and Harvard, where he developed innovative notation suited for computer-based data processing. Iverson worked at Harvard, IBM, I.P. Sharp Associates, and founded Jsoftware Inc., continuing the evolution of APL into the J programming language. His work influenced computational education and programming language theory. Iverson received the Turing Award (1979) and numerous honors, and contributed extensively to programming language design, implementation, and teaching.

# Ismail al-Jazari
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–c.1206) was a medieval Muslim polymath, engineer, and inventor from Upper Mesopotamia, famed for his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*. Serving as chief engineer to the Artuqid dynasty, he designed advanced automata, water clocks, and mechanical devices incorporating innovations such as the camshaft, crankshaft, and escapement mechanism. His pioneering water-raising machines, automata (like drink-serving figures and musical robots), and elaborate water clocks reflect practical engineering artistry. His designs influenced future mechanical engineering and automata. Besides mechanics, al-Jazari was an accomplished artist who illustrated his work with miniatures.

# Jacek Karpiński
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and inventor noted for creating some of the earliest transistor-based computing devices in Eastern Europe. A WWII resistance fighter, he developed innovative machines including the AAH analyzer, the transistor differential analyzer AKAT-1, an early perceptron, and the high-speed KAR-65 computer. He designed the K-202 minicomputer incorporating advanced features like memory segmentation and asynchronous design, but political and institutional challenges limited its production. Later, he worked in Switzerland and advised on computing in Poland. Posthumously honored with national orders, Karpiński remains a significant yet controversial figure in Polish computing history.

# Kurt Gödel
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) was an Austrian-American logician and mathematician, best known for his incompleteness theorems, which proved that any sufficiently powerful axiomatic system cannot be both consistent and complete. Educated in Vienna, he contributed foundational work in mathematical logic, including the completeness theorem for first-order logic and results on set theory axioms. Fleeing Nazi Europe, he emigrated to Princeton, becoming a close friend of Einstein. Gödel made important contributions to philosophy and physics, including solutions in general relativity permitting closed timelike curves. Known for his profound thought and mental health struggles, he formulated an ontological proof of God and left a lasting legacy in logic and philosophy.

# Federico Faggin
Federico Faggin (born 1941) is an Italian physicist, inventor, and entrepreneur, celebrated for pioneering silicon-gate MOS technology and leading the design of the Intel 4004, the world’s first commercial microprocessor. After early work at Olivetti and SGS-Fairchild, he advanced the silicon-gate process at Fairchild Semiconductor, enabling modern integrated circuits. At Intel, he devised breakthrough circuit designs and led the microprocessor project. Later, he co-founded Zilog, developing the influential Z80 CPU. Faggin also co-founded Synaptics, introducing the touchpad and early touchscreen technology. His recent work includes consciousness theory based on quantum principles, supported by the Federico and Elvia Faggin Foundation.

# Lynn Conway
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and electrical engineer who revolutionized VLSI design methodology and computer architecture. Educated at MIT and Columbia, she worked at IBM’s Advanced Computing Systems before transitioning gender in 1968, after which IBM fired her. Resuming her career stealthily, she joined Xerox PARC, collaborating to create the Mead-Conway VLSI design methodology and founded the MOSIS chip fabrication service, profoundly impacting chip design and industry. Conway was also a transgender rights activist. Her career bridged technology innovation and social progress, earning numerous honors including IEEE Fellow and National Academy of Engineering membership.

# Grace Hopper
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was an American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral, instrumental in developing early programming languages and compiler technology. She earned a Ph.D. in mathematics and served on the Harvard Mark I programming team during WWII. Hopper pioneered the idea of machine-independent languages, leading to the development of COBOL, the first major programming language for business. Known as “Grandma COBOL,” she popularized terms like “debugging,” contributed to programming standards, and advocated for networked computing. Hopper retired as the oldest serving U.S. naval officer and received many accolades, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom posthumously.

# Donald Knuth
Donald Knuth (born 1938) is an American computer scientist, mathematician, and professor emeritus at Stanford University, best known for his seminal series *The Art of Computer Programming*. Starting with early interests in mathematics and programming languages, he has made foundational contributions to algorithm analysis, digital typography (TeX), and literate programming. Knuth’s meticulous work has deeply influenced computer science theory and practice. He has received numerous prestigious awards, including the Turing Award, National Medal of Science, and Kyoto Prize. A devout Lutheran and accomplished organist, he combines scholarship with creativity and humor.

# Gottlob Frege
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German logician, mathematician, and philosopher, widely considered the father of modern logic and analytic philosophy. His pioneering work introduced axiomatic predicate logic, quantified variables, and formal treatments of mathematical concepts, laying the groundwork for arithmetic's reduction to logic (logicism). His *Begriffsschrift* and *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* influenced Russell, Wittgenstein, and Gödel. Frege formulated the distinction between sense and reference in language, central to philosophy of language. Despite his intellectual brilliance, his system was undermined by Russell's paradox. Late in life, Frege expressed controversial antisemitic views.

# Claude Shannon
Claude Shannon (1916–2001) was an American mathematician and electrical engineer, founder of information theory. He revolutionized digital circuit design through applying Boolean algebra to switching circuits in his 1937 master's thesis. Shannon's 1948 paper *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* introduced the concept of information entropy and provided a foundation for digital communication and data compression. He contributed to cryptography, machine learning, artificial intelligence, and signal processing. Known as “the father of the information age,” Shannon also engaged in recreational inventions and artificial intelligence experiments. His legacy is celebrated globally through awards, statues, and centenary events.

# Al-Khwarizmi
Al-Khwārizmī (c. 780–850) was a Persian scholar and mathematician whose works laid foundations for algebra and introduced the Hindu-Arabic numeral system to the West. Serving at Baghdad’s House of Wisdom, he authored *Al-Jabr*, systematizing solutions of linear and quadratic equations and giving rise to the term “algebra.” His works on arithmetic, astronomy, and geography significantly advanced Islamic science and influenced medieval Europe through Latin translations. He developed systematic computational methods and tabulated trigonometric functions. Al-Khwārizmī’s name survives in “algorithm,” reflecting his profound impact on mathematics and science.

# Edsger W. Dijkstra
Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist known for fundamental contributions to algorithms, programming methodology, and operating systems. He formulated Dijkstra’s algorithm for shortest paths, advocated structured programming, and pioneered concepts like semaphores and the THE operating system. Dijkstra was a prolific writer, famous for his hand-written EWD manuscripts, and outspoken critic of unstructured programming and software engineering shortcomings. His intellectual rigor, teaching style, and emphasis on simplicity influenced computer science profoundly. He received the Turing Award in 1972 and held academic positions in Europe and the US.

# Per Brinch Hansen
Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) was a Danish computer scientist who pioneered operating systems, concurrent programming, and parallel computing. He led development of the RC 4000 multiprogramming system, introduced the operating system kernel concept, and co-developed the monitor synchronization construct. His texts—*Operating System Principles* and *The Architecture of Concurrent Programs*—are classic works. He created the first concurrent programming language, Concurrent Pascal, and contributed to remote procedure call concepts. After academic positions in Denmark and the US, he continued advancing parallel programming paradigms. He was recognized with IEEE Computer Pioneer Award among others.

# Herbert A. Simon
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was an American polymath and Nobel laureate whose work spanned economics, psychology, artificial intelligence, decision theory, and organizational studies. He introduced the concept of bounded rationality, explaining human decision-making under cognitive and informational constraints, and developed influential models of administrative behavior. As a pioneer in AI, he co-created early problem-solving programs and theories of human cognition. Simon’s interdisciplinary research transformed economics and social science, earning him the 1978 Nobel Prize in Economics and the 1975 Turing Award. His prolific writings and insights remain foundational across multiple scientific fields.
