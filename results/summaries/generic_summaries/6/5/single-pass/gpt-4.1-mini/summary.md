# Al-Khwarizmi  
Al-Khwarizmi, a Persian mathematician born around 780 in Khwarazm (now Turkmenistan or Uzbekistan), worked mainly between 813 and 833 in Baghdad under the Abbasid Caliph al-Ma'mun. He led the House of Wisdom’s library and contributed to mathematics, astronomy, cartography, and history, translating works from Greek and Sanskrit. His name is linked to foundational developments in algebra (from his book *Al-Jabr*) and the spread of Hindu–Arabic numerals, whose Latin translation introduced the term "algorithm". He improved Ptolemy's geographic data and developed mechanical devices including water clocks and automata.

His algebra systematically solved linear and quadratic equations using methods called al-jabr (restoration) and al-muqabala (balancing), standardizing six equation types. Though his notation was rhetorical, his work laid the groundwork for modern algebra. Al-Khwarizmi also authored astronomy texts with trigonometric tables, geographical coordinates updating Ptolemy’s *Geography*, and treatises on calendars and astrolabes. His contributions influenced medieval European science through Latin translations.

# Gottlob Frege  
Born in 1848 in Germany, Frege studied mathematics, physics, and philosophy focusing eventually on logic. He invented axiomatic predicate logic and a formal language (*Begriffsschrift*) that rigorously treated functions and variables, aiming to base arithmetic on logic (logicism). His work solved the problem of multiple generality in logic, influencing foundations of mathematics, analytic philosophy, and semantic theories. Although his *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* was undermined by Russell’s paradox, his work remains foundational, with modern logicians salvaging aspects.

Frege pioneered key philosophy of language concepts—sense vs. reference, compositionality, and context principle—shaping analytic philosophy. Personally introverted, he held conservative and virulently anti-Semitic political views later in life. His major publications include *Begriffsschrift* (1879), *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884), and *Basic Laws of Arithmetic* (1893, 1903).

# Per Brinch Hansen  
Danish computer scientist Per Brinch Hansen (1938–2007) innovated in operating systems, concurrent and distributed programming, and parallel computing over a five-decade career. After electrical engineering studies and early work at Regnecentralen, he led development of the RC 4000 multiprogramming system, introducing kernel design and separating mechanisms from policies. His 1973 textbook *Operating System Principles* became a classic.

At Caltech and later USC, he invented Concurrent Pascal and coined the monitor concept for safe concurrent programming. He developed languages such as Edison and Joyce for parallel/distributed processing and influenced programming paradigms toward reusability. At Syracuse University, he explored parallel algorithm structures, culminating in the publication *Studies in Computational Science*. Hansen was honored with IEEE fellowships, the Computer Pioneer Award, and various academic professorships.

# Grace Hopper  
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was a pioneering American computer scientist and naval officer who developed some of the earliest compilers and promoted English-like programming languages. After earning a PhD in mathematics from Yale, she served in WWII with the Navy, working on the Harvard Mark I computer. She suggested the idea of compilers in the early 1950s and developed the A-0 compiler, leading to early programming languages FLOW-MATIC and influentially, COBOL—a business-oriented language designed to be close to English.

Hopper advocated for programming language standardization, testing, and naval computing modernization. She retired and was recalled multiple times, eventually reaching the rank of rear admiral. Known for popularizing the term "debugging" and her nanoseconds teaching aids, Hopper received numerous honors, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom (posthumous), 40 honorary degrees, and a Navy destroyer named after her.

# Herbert A. Simon  
Herbert Simon (1916–2001) was a renowned social scientist, economist, psychologist, and computer scientist noted for pioneering decision-making theory, bounded rationality, and artificial intelligence. Born in Milwaukee, he studied political science and economics, earning a PhD from the University of Chicago. Simon’s *Administrative Behavior* (1947) revolutionized understanding of organizational decision-making by integrating cognitive limitations into rational choice models.

He co-developed artificial intelligence programs like the Logic Theory Machine and General Problem Solver, contributed to theories of human problem solving and learning, and influenced behavioral economics with concepts like satisficing. Simon received numerous accolades including the 1975 Turing Award and the 1978 Nobel Prize in Economics for his research on decision-making within organizations.

# Federico Faggin  
Federico Faggin (born 1941, Italy) is a physicist and electrical engineer who pioneered silicon-gate MOS technology and led the design of the world’s first commercial microprocessor, the Intel 4004, in 1971. After early work at Olivetti and SGS-Fairchild where he developed silicon-gate technology, Faggin’s methodology and innovations (e.g., buried contacts, bootstrap loads) enabled fitting a CPU on a single chip. He subsequently contributed to designs of Intel’s early microprocessors (8008, 4040, 8080) and co-founded Zilog, creating the Z80 microprocessor, influential in early personal computers.

Later ventures included founding Synaptics (early touchpads and neural networks) and leading Foveon (advanced imaging sensors). Faggin has also developed a quantum theory of consciousness. He has received numerous international awards including the Kyoto Prize, National Medal of Technology, and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame.

# Edsger W. Dijkstra  
Edsger Dijkstra (1930–2002), Dutch computer scientist, is celebrated for fundamental contributions to algorithms, programming methodology, and software engineering. After studying physics, he worked as the Netherlands’ first programmer at the Mathematisch Centrum, where he developed Dijkstra’s shortest path algorithm and contributed to ALGOL 60 compiler development. He coined the monitor concept, advanced structured programming, and opposed the Goto statement (famously in "Go To Statement Considered Harmful", 1968).

He developed the THE multiprogramming system and concepts central to concurrency and deadlock avoidance (semaphores, banker's algorithm). Known for his rigorous, elegant formal proofs and prolific manuscripts (EWD reports), Dijkstra’s work underpins modern computing theory. He received the 1972 Turing Award and numerous honors.

# Jacek Karpiński  
Polish engineer Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a pioneer in computing and electronics under challenging political conditions. After severe wartime injuries, he excelled academically and worked on early computers and devices including the AAH harmonic analyzer and the transistor-based AKAT-1 differential analyzer. He built Poland's fastest computer KAR-65 and the innovative K-202 minicomputer, which featured modularity, floating point, and memory segmentation but was not mass-produced due to political obstacles.

Karpiński developed one of the earliest perceptrons in communist Europe. Despite international recognition via a UNESCO scholarship, his career was marked by conflicts, censorship, and espionage involvements. His influential but controversial contributions reside in Polish computing history.

# Claude Shannon  
Claude Shannon (1916–2001), American mathematician and electrical engineer, founded information theory. His 1937 MIT master’s thesis established Boolean algebra as a tool for designing digital circuits. During WWII, he worked on cryptography and fire-control systems. In 1948, his seminal paper *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* introduced information entropy and revolutionized telecommunications, cryptography, and data compression.

Shannon contributed to early AI with a learning mechanical mouse (Theseus) and authored foundational papers on computer chess programming. He taught at MIT and was recognized for creativity in engineering, inventing juggling machines and pioneering wearable computing. He received major awards including the National Medal of Science and the Kyoto Prize. His work underlies the digital age.

# Kenneth E. Iverson  
Ken Iverson (1920–2004), Canadian mathematician and computer scientist, developed APL, a concise and powerful language for data processing and mathematical notation. Educated at Queen’s University and Harvard under Howard Aiken, Iverson was instrumental in designing notation formalizing arrays and operators, leading to IBM’s System/360 formal description and implementation of APL\360.

He worked at IBM, I.P. Sharp Associates, and founded Jsoftware Inc., developing the J programming language as a successor to APL. Iverson’s emphasis on clarity and mathematical expressiveness earned him honors including the 1979 Turing Award, IBM Fellowship, and IEEE awards.

# Donald Knuth  
Donald Knuth (born 1938) is an American computer scientist renowned for authoring the multi-volume *The Art of Computer Programming*, a comprehensive analysis of algorithms. Educated at Case Institute and Caltech, he developed early ALGOL compilers, contributed foundational work to algorithm analysis, and pioneered digital typesetting with TeX and Metafont.

Knuth introduced literate programming, treating code as literature, and has received numerous top awards including the Turing Award, National Medal of Science, and Kyoto Prize. He is a distinguished academic, musician, and author known for clarity, humor, and attention to detail.

# George Boole  
George Boole (1815–1864), English mathematician and logician, founded Boolean algebra, formalizing logical operations with algebraic methods. Largely self-taught, he became the first professor of mathematics at Queen’s College, Cork. His works laid groundwork for digital logic and computer science.

Boole’s algebra treated logic operations as algebraic expressions, influencing the foundations of set theory, probability, and computer architecture. He received honors including Fellowship of the Royal Society and the Keith Medal. His legacy endures in modern computing via Boolean logic and data types.

# Lynn Conway  
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was an American computer scientist and transgender activist who revolutionized VLSI chip design alongside Carver Mead, pioneering scalable design rules and multi-project wafer technology. After beginning her career at IBM and pioneering superscalar supercomputer design, she transitioned in 1968 and was unjustly fired.

Conway later advanced VLSI methodologies at Xerox PARC and DARPA, and shaped industry standards and education in chip design. She advocated for transgender rights and visibility in STEM. Among her many honors are IEEE Fellow, National Academy of Engineering membership, and induction into the Computer History Museum.

# Ramon Llull  
Ramon Llull (1232/3–1315/6) was a Majorcan philosopher, logician, and missionary known for creating the *Ars*—a combinatorial system of universal logic intended to prove theological truths and convert non-Christians. Influenced by Islamic and Christian thought, Llull’s Art used symbolic letters and diagrams for logical deduction. 

He wrote dialogues, developed early computational devices, and urged language education for missionary work. His water clocks, combinatorial trees, and concept of correlatives shaped medieval logic. Beatified in 1847, Llull’s legacy is recognized in philosophy, logic, literature, and as a precursor to computation theory.

# Kurt Gödel  
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978), Austrian-American logician and mathematician, revolutionized logic with his incompleteness theorems proving inherent limitations in formal axiomatic systems, revealing that no consistent system capable of arithmetic can be both complete and able to prove its own consistency.

He also proved the completeness of first-order logic and contributed to set theory (constructible universe). Gödel influenced philosophy and physics, discovering rotating universe solutions in general relativity. A close friend of Einstein, Gödel held strong religious and philosophical views. His work deeply reshaped logic, mathematics, and the foundations of computation.

# George Antheil  
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American avant-garde composer notable for *Ballet Mécanique*, a radical, mechanistic musical work employing player pianos, percussion, and airplane propellers. His early modernist, percussive style was both celebrated and controversial in Europe and US. He composed film scores in Hollywood and engaged in diverse pursuits including writing and radio.

Antheil co-invented the frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology with actress Hedy Lamarr during WWII, foundational to modern wireless communications. Despite setbacks and fluctuating reputation, his innovations influenced 20th-century music and technology.

# Ismail al-Jazari  
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–1206) was a medieval Arab polymath and engineer known for his highly sophisticated mechanical devices, detailed in *The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*. He invented early camshafts, crankshafts, escapement mechanisms, and automated humanoid machines including hand-washing automata and musical robots.

Al-Jazari pioneered water-raising devices, complex water clocks (e.g., elephant clock with automata), and mechanical controls, influencing both Islamic and later European engineering. His practical approach combined craftsmanship with innovation and included artistic miniature illustrations. Many early robotics concepts trace back to his work.

# Geoffrey Hinton  
Geoffrey Hinton (born 1947) is a British-Canadian cognitive psychologist and computer scientist, a pioneer in artificial neural networks and deep learning. Educated at Cambridge and Edinburgh, he made fundamental advances in machine learning, including backpropagation, Boltzmann machines, and capsule networks. 

Hinton co-founded DNNresearch (acquired by Google), led major AI research at University of Toronto, and has mentored many leading AI researchers. He received the 2018 Turing Award and 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for contributions to neural networks. Recently, he has expressed concerns about the rapid development and risks of AI systems.
