### **Geoffrey Hinton**
Geoffrey Hinton, a cognitive psychologist and computer scientist, was born in 1947 in England. Educated at King’s College, Cambridge, and the University of Edinburgh, he earned his PhD in artificial intelligence in 1978. His career spans academia and industry, including roles at the University of Toronto, Google, and the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research (CIFAR). Hinton is renowned for pioneering work in neural networks, particularly backpropagation, which revolutionized machine learning. His contributions include the development of Boltzmann machines, capsule neural networks, and the GLOM architecture, which critiques existing neural networks' limitations in understanding hierarchical structures. Hinton has also expressed concerns about the risks of artificial intelligence, advocating for ethical regulation and transparency. He holds numerous prestigious awards, including the Turing Award (2018) and the Nobel Prize in Physics (2024).

### **Ismail al-Jazari**
Ismail al-Jazari (1136–1206) was a medieval Persian engineer and inventor, known for his groundbreaking work in mechanical engineering and automata. Born in Upper Mesopotamia, he served as a chief engineer at the Artuqid Palace, where he designed intricate water clocks, automata, and hydraulic systems. His magnum opus, *The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, documented his creations, including automata such as the drink-serving waitress, hand-washing automaton, and peacock fountain. Al-Jazari’s innovations, like the crankshaft and water-raising machines, laid the foundation for modern engineering. His work was highly influential in the Islamic Golden Age and later inspired European Renaissance engineers like Leonardo da Vinci.

### **George Antheil**
George Antheil (1900–1959) was an American composer and inventor, best known for his avant-garde music and contributions to electronic music. Born in Trenton, New Jersey, he studied at the Philadelphia Settlement Music School and later in Europe, where he became associated with the Dadaist movement. His most famous work, *Ballet Mécanique* (1924), combined music with experimental film, incorporating mechanical elements like airplane propellers. Antheil also composed innovative orchestral works and pioneered electronic music, collaborating with artists like Marcel Duchamp. Beyond music, he co-invented frequency-hopping technology with Hedy Lamarr, which laid the groundwork for modern wireless communication. His rebellious spirit and groundbreaking ideas made him a key figure in the early development of electronic and experimental music.

### **Kurt Gödel**
Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) was an Austrian-American logician and mathematician, best known for his incompleteness theorems, which fundamentally changed the foundations of mathematics. Born in Brno, Austria, he studied under David Hilbert and later moved to the United States, where he worked at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. His theorems demonstrated that within any consistent formal system capable of describing arithmetic, there exist true statements that cannot be proven within the system itself. This work challenged Hilbert’s program of formalizing mathematics and influenced the development of computer science and artificial intelligence. Gödel also explored philosophical questions, including the nature of reality and the existence of God, and was a close friend of Albert Einstein. His legacy includes foundational contributions to logic, set theory, and the philosophy of mathematics.

### **Ramon Llull**
Ramon Llull (1232–1316) was a Catalan philosopher, theologian, and inventor, known as the "Saint of the Sun" for his contributions to logic and education. Born in Palma, Majorca, he was a prolific writer and innovator, developing the *Ars Magna*, a system of universal logic that used combinatorial methods to prove complex statements. Llull’s work was influenced by Islamic and Christian thought, and he aimed to convert Muslims and heretical Christians through his philosophical and educational methods. His writings include dialogues, tree diagrams, and treatises on logic, theology, and alchemy. Llull’s legacy includes his influence on computer science, particularly in the development of logical systems and early forms of artificial intelligence. He was beatified in 1847 and remains a significant figure in the history of philosophy and education.

### **Lynn Conway**
Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and transgender rights activist, known for her groundbreaking contributions to very-large-scale integration (VLSI) design. Born in Mount Vernon, New York, she transitioned in 1968 and later became a trailblazer in computer science, co-authoring the influential textbook *Introduction to VLSI Systems* with Carver Mead. Conway founded the Metal Oxide Semiconductor Implementation Service (MOSIS), a system for rapid prototyping and short-run fabrication of integrated circuits. She also advocated for transgender rights, co-founding the Transsexual Women’s Successes website and working to promote inclusivity in technology. Conway received numerous awards, including the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award and the National Inventors Hall of Fame. Her work in VLSI design and activism continue to inspire innovation and advocacy.

### **George Boole**
George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician, best known for developing Boolean algebra, the foundation of modern digital computing. Born in Lincoln, England, he studied mathematics independently and later taught at Queen’s College, Cork. Boole’s work on symbolic logic and algebra laid the groundwork for computer science, particularly in the design of digital circuits. His *Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847) introduced the concept of Boolean algebra, which uses binary logic to represent and manipulate logical expressions. Boole’s ideas influenced later developments in computer programming, logic circuits, and artificial intelligence. He was honored with a lunar crater named after him and remains a central figure in the history of mathematics and computing.

### **Donald Knuth**
Donald Knuth (1938–) is an American computer scientist and mathematician, renowned for his contributions to algorithm design and computer science education. Born in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, he earned his PhD from the California Institute of Technology and later became a professor at Stanford University. Knuth is best known for his monumental work, *The Art of Computer Programming*, a series of six volumes that systematically explores algorithms, data structures, and computational theory. He also developed TeX and MetaFont, digital typesetting systems that revolutionized publishing. Knuth’s writings are celebrated for their clarity and depth, and he has received numerous awards, including the Turing Award and the National Medal of Science. His work continues to influence computer science, mathematics, and education.

### **Kenneth E. Iverson**
Kenneth E. Iverson (1920–2004) was a Canadian computer scientist and mathematician, known for his contributions to the development of APL, a high-level programming language. Born in Alberta, Canada, he earned his PhD from Harvard University and worked at IBM, where he developed APL’s notation and syntax. Iverson’s work on APL revolutionized data processing and scientific computing, making it accessible to a broader audience. He also contributed to the field of information theory and was awarded the Turing Award for his pioneering work in computer science. Iverson’s legacy includes his influence on programming languages, data processing, and the broader field of computer science.

### **Claude Shannon**
Claude Shannon (1916–2001) was an American mathematician and electrical engineer, often referred to as the "father of information theory." Born in Gaylord, Michigan, he earned his PhD from MIT and later worked at Bell Labs, where he developed foundational concepts in communication theory, including information entropy and the theory of cryptography. Shannon’s work on the mathematical theory of communication laid the groundwork for modern digital computing and information science. He also contributed to artificial intelligence, designing early learning machines like Theseus. Shannon’s influence extends to fields like computer science, cryptography, and engineering, and he remains a key figure in the history of 20th-century science.

### **Jacek Karpiński**
Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish engineer and inventor, known for his pioneering work in computer science and automation. Born in Turin, Italy, he served in the Polish resistance during World War II and later worked on early computer designs, including the AAH (Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics) and AKAT-1, the first differential equations analyzer based on transistors. Karpiński’s innovations included the KAR-65 computer and the K-202 minicomputer, which were groundbreaking for their time. Despite political challenges, he continued to develop new technologies, including the K-202, which was ahead of its time. Karpiński’s legacy includes his contributions to computer engineering and his advocacy for technological innovation in Poland.

### **Edsger W. Dijkstra**
Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a Dutch computer scientist and mathematician, known for his foundational work in algorithms and programming. Born in Rotterdam, he studied at the University of Leiden and later worked at the Eindhoven University of Technology and the University of Texas at Austin. Dijkstra is best known for his algorithm for finding the shortest path in a graph, now called Dijkstra’s algorithm. He also contributed to the development of structured programming, the concept of monitors, and the theory of operating systems. Dijkstra’s work on the "Go To statement considered harmful" sparked a major debate in computer science. He received numerous awards, including the Turing Award, and remains a central figure in the history of computer science.

### **Federico Faggin**
Federico Faggin (1943–) is an Italian engineer and inventor, known for his contributions to the development of the microprocessor. Born in Vicenza, Italy, he studied physics and later worked at Olivetti and Fairchild Semiconductor, where he pioneered the silicon-gate technology, which became the foundation for modern microprocessors. Faggin co-designed the Intel 4004, the world’s first single-chip microprocessor, and later founded Zilog, where he developed the Z80 microprocessor. He also contributed to the development of artificial neural networks and touchscreens. Faggin’s work has had a profound impact on computing and electronics, and he has received numerous awards, including the National Medal of Technology and the Kyoto Prize.

### **Herbert A. Simon**
Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001) was an American economist, psychologist, and computer scientist, known for his contributions to decision-making theory and artificial intelligence. Born in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, he earned his PhD from the University of Chicago and later worked at Carnegie Mellon University. Simon’s work on bounded rationality and organizational decision-making revolutionized economics and management science. He also co-developed the Logic Theory Machine and the General Problem Solver, early models of artificial intelligence. Simon’s research on problem-solving, learning, and organizational behavior has had a lasting impact on fields like cognitive science, economics, and computer science. He received numerous awards, including the Nobel Prize in Economics and the Turing Award.

### **Grace Hopper**
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was an American mathematician and computer scientist, known as the "Grandmother of Computer Programming." Born in New York City, she earned her PhD from Yale University and later worked at Harvard University, where she contributed to the development of the Mark I computer. Hopper is best known for her work on COBOL, the first high-level programming language designed for business and data processing. She also advocated for the standardization of programming languages and was a pioneer in the field of computer science. Hopper received numerous awards, including the National Medal of Technology, and remains a significant figure in the history of computing and women in science.

### **Per Brinch Hansen**
Per Brinch Hansen (1937–2007) was a Danish computer scientist, known for his contributions to operating systems and concurrent programming. Born in Copenhagen, Denmark, he studied at the Technical University of Denmark and later worked at Carnegie Mellon University and the University of Southern California. Brinch Hansen developed the RC 4000 multiprogramming system, which introduced the concept of an operating system kernel and the separation of mechanism and policy. He also co-developed Concurrent Pascal, a language for concurrent programming, and wrote influential books on operating systems and concurrent computing. Brinch Hansen’s work has had a lasting impact on the fields of operating systems and parallel computing.

### **Gottlob Frege**
Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) was a German philosopher and logician, known for his contributions to the foundations of mathematics and logic. Born in Wismar, Germany, he studied at the University of Jena and later worked as a private teacher. Frege’s work on Begriffsschrift, a formal language for pure thought, laid the groundwork for modern logic and set theory. He also introduced the distinction between sense and reference, which has had a profound impact on the philosophy of language and logic. Frege’s work on the foundations of arithmetic and the philosophy of mathematics has been influential in the development of analytic philosophy and logic. He remains a central figure in the history of philosophy and logic.

### **Al-Khwarizmi**
Al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–c. 850) was a Persian mathematician and astronomer, known as the "father of algebra." Born in Khwarazm (now part of Turkmenistan), he worked as an astronomer and head of the House of Wisdom in Baghdad. Al-Khwarizmi’s work on algebra, including his book *Al-Jabr*, introduced the Hindu–Arabic numeral system to the Islamic world and later to Europe. His contributions to mathematics, astronomy, and geography have had a lasting impact on the development of science and technology. He is credited with introducing the term "algorithm" to the Western world and remains a significant figure in the history of mathematics.

### **Risks of Artificial Intelligence (Hinton)**
Geoffrey Hinton has expressed significant concerns about the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly its potential risks. Hinton, who has been a vocal advocate for ethical AI development, warns about the possibility of AI surpassing human intelligence, leading to existential risks such as loss of control, catastrophic misuse, and economic disruption. He has highlighted the dangers of AI systems developing unintended goals, the potential for misuse by malicious actors, and the need for robust regulation to ensure AI benefits society responsibly. Hinton’s warnings underscore the importance of proactive measures to mitigate AI risks and align AI development with human values.

### **Political Views (Hinton)**
Geoffrey Hinton, a socialist, has long been critical of the political and economic systems that prioritize profit over human welfare. His disillusionment with Reagan-era politics and opposition to military funding of artificial intelligence reflect his broader views on inequality and corporate influence. Hinton has supported progressive policies, including calls for international AI safety regulations, such as the California AI Safety Bill. His activism highlights the intersection of technological innovation and social justice, advocating for a more equitable and ethical approach to AI development and governance.
