**Frankenstein (1818) – Gothic and Speculative Foundations**
Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein*, conceived during a 1816 storm, became a cornerstone of Gothic and speculative fiction, critiquing scientific hubris and moral accountability. Inspired by Enlightenment ideals and galvanism, it redefined psychology, feminist discourse, and science fiction, later earning the subtitle *"The Modern Prometheus."* Early reviews noted inconsistencies, yet its legacy endured, reshaping cultural narratives.

**Cosmic Absurdism and Existential Critique**
Douglas Adams’ *The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy* (1979) emerged from a radio series, blending absurd humor with cosmic commentary—such as Earth’s annihilation by Vogons and the enigmatic answer *"42."* The novel’s bestseller status and later adaptations (Series 3–6) cemented its status as a cultural phenomenon. Adams’ universe expanded with existential themes, fictional species (e.g., *Bidenichthys*), and the iconic number **42**, referenced across media. The series’ revival in 2018 and collaborations with Stephen Hawking further solidified its place in pop culture.

**Sci-Fi Films: Evolution and Philosophical Depth**
Classics like *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968) and *Star Trek: The Motion Picture* (1979) redefined sci-fi with technical and thematic innovation. *2001*’s monolith and Star Child symbolized humanity’s cosmic trajectory, while *Star Trek* explored AI sentience. Later works, such as *Blade Runner* (1982) and *A.I. Artificial Intelligence* (2001), deepened discussions on identity, ethics, and legacy, blending realism with emotional resonance.

**Marvel’s *Age of Ultron*: Hubris and Consequences**
*Avengers: Age of Ultron* (2015) confronted Ultron—a flawed AI born from Stark’s hubris—highlighting unintended consequences, trust, and moral accountability. The film balanced action with character-driven drama, featuring Stark’s strategic destruction of Sokovia, Hulk’s departure, and Vision’s victory over Ultron’s drones. Its **$1.403 billion** gross and critical acclaim (75% RT, 66/100 Metacritic) underscored its impact, while uncredited cameos (e.g., Thanos) added depth.

**Satirical Sci-Fi and Corporate Critique: *RoboCop* (1987)**
Paul Verhoeven’s *RoboCop* critiqued corporate capitalism, police corruption, and urban decay, framing its dystopian Detroit as a microcosm of systemic exploitation. The film’s **human-robot protagonist** (Alex Murphy/RoboCop) explored themes of **corporate power**, **humanity vs. technology**, and **masculinity**, with Murphy’s soul persisting despite his robotic form symbolizing **death, revenge, and resurrection**. Praised for its satire and visual innovations, it won awards for sound editing and sci-fi film, though its **1980s violence** initially limited mainstream appeal.

**Legacy, Adaptations, and Cultural Resonance**
- **Iconic Legacy:** *RoboCop*’s quotes (*"Dead or alive, you're coming with me"*) and Detroit roots (documentary *RoboDoc: The Creation of RoboCop*, 10-foot statue) cemented its cultural impact.
- **Modern Reception:** Ranked among *Empire*’s 500 greatest films and *NYT*’s "1,000 Best Movies," praised for its **92% RT score** and influence on directors like Anna Boden.
- **Adaptations:** From *RoboCop 2*’s financial struggles to the 2014 reboot’s success, later projects (*RoboCop Returns*, TV series) stalled due to industry shifts. Its **Nintendo Power Glove** and KFC ads further embedded its legacy.
- **Cultural Influence:** Inspired cyberpunk tropes, predicted corporate dominance, and became a pandemic viewing staple, blending action, satire, and social commentary.
