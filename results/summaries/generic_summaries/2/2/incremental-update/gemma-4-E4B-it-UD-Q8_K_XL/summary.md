**Algorithmic Bias and Mitigation in AI**
Algorithmic bias, arising from flawed training data, leads to unfair outcomes in fields like healthcare and criminal justice, sometimes manifesting as language or gender skew in Large Language Models (LLMs). Addressing this requires integrating technical solutions (like bias detection and Explainable AI) with regulatory compliance (e.g., GDPR). Defining mathematical fairness is complex, utilizing metrics categorized by predicted outcomes ($R$), combined outcomes ($R$ and $Y$), and predicted probabilities ($S$), including Demographic Parity, Predictive Parity, Equal Opportunity, and Test-Fairness. Furthermore, fairness can be defined as **Group Fairness** (e.g., Equal Confusion Fairness), **Individual Fairness** (treating similar individuals similarly, achievable via FTU or FTA), or through **Causality-based Metrics** like counterfactual fairness.

Bias mitigation is systematically applied across three phases:
1.  **Preprocessing:** Modifies the input data, such as using **Reweighing** to assign weights that counteract statistical dependencies.
2.  **Inprocessing:** Corrects bias during training by adding fairness constraints to the optimization objective; an example is **Adversarial debiasing**, which pits a predictor against an adversary trying to guess sensitive attributes from the output.
3.  **Postprocessing:** Adjusts model outputs after training, often by using different classification thresholds for protected groups or employing **Reject Option Based Classification** for low-confidence predictions.

**AI Intelligence Assessment and Cognition**
The evaluation of AI intelligence has advanced past simple imitation games, incorporating tests like the Reverse Turing Test/CAPTCHA, the Total Turing Test, and the Lovelace Test (for novel creation), alongside quantitative measures like Kolmogorov Complexity. **Embodied cognition** offers an alternative view, positing that mental functions emerge from the body's interaction with the environment through perception-action loops and Sensorimotor Contingencies (SMCs).

**Superintelligence, Transhumanism, and Governance**
The concept of the Technological Singularity forecasts rapid, superhuman technological progress, with risks including goal misalignment and economic disruption, potentially manifesting as Hard or Soft Takeoff. **Transhumanism** seeks to transcend biological limitations using AI and biotech, raising concerns over socio-economic divisions. Governance frameworks are evolving, exemplified by the EU's AI Act, which mandates human oversight. A comprehensive approach to moral agency integrates technical safeguards (e.g., Llama Guard), institutional cooperation (e.g., Partnership on AI), and policy guidance (e.g., UNESCO) while managing the increasing data density associated with potential human evolution.
