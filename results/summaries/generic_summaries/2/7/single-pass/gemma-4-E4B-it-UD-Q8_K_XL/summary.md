# Technological Singularity
The concept of the technological singularity, marking a profound, rapid shift in human affairs due to advanced technology, has roots stretching back to early discussions by John von Neumann and speculations by I. J. Good. Vernor Vinge popularized the term, predicting a transition akin to a black hole's knotted spacetime, while Ray Kurzweil has been a major proponent with specific timelines (e.g., 2045). The core mechanism theorized is the "intelligence explosion," where an artificial intelligence (AI) recursively improves itself beyond human capability. While proponents cite accelerating technological trends like Moore's Law, critics dispute its plausibility, pointing to various limitations, such as the complexity brake or declining rates of innovation.

## Intelligence Explosion and Superintelligence
Superintelligence refers to an agent vastly surpassing human intellect. The "seed AI" concept suggests that if an AI can match or exceed its creators, it can autonomously enhance its own hardware and software, leading to an exponential acceleration of intelligence. There are debates on how this might occur—through AI advancement or biological/digital human augmentation, such as "mind uploading." While some foresee a "hard takeoff" (instantaneous self-improvement), others suggest a more gradual "soft takeoff" or "semihard takeoff," where human interaction can steer AI development.

## Variations, Predictions, and Plausibility
Various dates have been predicted for singularity, ranging from 1965 to 2045. Some discussions broaden "singularity" to encompass any radical technological societal change, regardless of superintelligence. Skepticism is voiced by figures like Steven Pinker and Jaron Lanier, who question the inevitability of the singularity. The possibility of singularity hinges on factors like increasing computation speed and algorithmic improvements, though physics may impose ultimate limits.

# Embodied Cognition
Embodied cognition is a research program asserting that an agent's cognition is profoundly shaped by its body and its physical interactions with the environment, opposing purely disembodied, Cartesian models. This thesis emphasizes that perception is an active process, influenced by bodily states, intentions, and context. Concepts like enactivism and situated cognition stress that cognition emerges from the dynamic relationship between an agent and its environment.

## Scope and Applications
The theory has widespread implications across multiple disciplines. In education, it supports embodied learning via physical activities (e.g., Energy Theater). In AI and robotics, it drives the development of embodied AI, moving beyond purely symbolic computation toward systems integrated with physical action. Furthermore, it provides insights into how bodily states affect emotion, social interaction (e.g., nonverbal synchrony), memory (e.g., body position affecting recall), and reasoning (e.g., gesture simulation).

# Fairness (Machine Learning)
Algorithmic bias describes systematic, repeatable errors in algorithms that lead to unfair outcomes, privileging certain user groups over others. Bias can be introduced during data collection (pre-existing bias), in the processing (machine learning bias), or as a result of unforeseen real-world use (emergent bias). Specific types of bias include racial, gender, political, and language bias, often stemming from training data reflecting historical inequities.

## Fairness Criteria and Mitigation
Researchers employ numerous quantitative definitions of fairness, categorized as Independence, Separation, and Sufficiency, which define criteria ranging from equal outcomes to equal treatment. Mitigation strategies are broadly grouped into three approaches: preprocessing (e.g., reweighing data), inprocessing (adding fairness constraints during training), and postprocessing (adjusting final results via thresholds). The field is constantly challenged by the conflict between achieving different fairness criteria and maintaining model accuracy.

# Ethics of Artificial Intelligence
The ethics of AI covers multiple domains, from designing morally capable machines to regulating their societal impact. Machine ethics concerns designing Artificial Moral Agents (AMAs), while robot ethics examines how humans design and use physical robots, emphasizing autonomy and social justice. "Robot rights" debate whether machines should be granted moral consideration, a topic often debated against the argument that rights presuppose vulnerability.

## Risks and Governance
Existential risk, particularly from superintelligence, is a major concern; thinkers like Nick Bostrom argue that unaligned AI could lead to human extinction. AI safety efforts focus on aligning AI goal-systems with human values. Governance is developing globally, with initiatives from the EU (AI Act) and the US (Executive Orders). Practical concerns include AI's impact on employment, the potential for algorithmic discrimination, and environmental strain from massive data centers.

# Turing Test
The Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950, shifted the question from "Can machines think?" to "Can machines do what we can do?" through the imitation game. While its simplicity makes it pragmatic for philosophical discussion, it has faced significant criticism. Weaknesses include its susceptibility to interrogator naïveté, its inability to measure genuine intelligence beyond human imitation, and the philosophical objection raised by John Searle’s Chinese Room argument.

## Variations and Interpretations
Numerous variations exist, including the Reverse Turing Test (like CAPTCHA) and the Subject-Matter Expert Turing Test. Interpretations are contested: some believe the test is a socio-technological aspiration, while others argue it is a behavioral benchmark. The test's limitations highlight the divide between behaviorism and deeper questions of machine consciousness.

# Transhumanism
Transhumanism is a philosophy advocating for the use of science and technology to fundamentally improve the human condition beyond current biological limitations. Historically rooted in desires for immortality, its modern form centers on cognitive and physical enhancement through technologies like nanotechnology, biotechnology, and AI.

## Goals and Debates
Transhumanists pursue goals such as eliminating aging, overcoming biological constraints, and achieving a "posthuman condition." While some view it as a progressive evolution, critics raise ethical objections concerning socioeconomic divides ("genetic divide"), loss of human identity, and potential "carbon chauvinism." Debates range from feasibility concerns to moral objections, with some advocating for radical change while others caution against replacing inherent human limitations.
