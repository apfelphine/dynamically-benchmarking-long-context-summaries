## Fairness in Machine Learning and Algorithmic Bias

Research on fairness in machine learning has surged since 2016, driven by reports of bias in automated systems such as the COMPAS recidivism tool. Fairness lacks a universal definition, with different criteria—independence, separation, and sufficiency—often being mutually exclusive. These can be mathematically assessed using confusion matrix metrics (TPR, FPR, etc.) and group fairness measures like demographic parity and predictive parity. Individual fairness, emphasizing "similar individuals should receive similar treatment," complements group approaches, but both can be undermined by proxy variables and human decision-makers’ selective adherence to fair algorithms. Causality-based metrics leverage counterfactuals and causal graphs to better detect the origins and effects of bias.

Major bias types include language bias (favoring English and its cultural framing), gender bias (reinforcing stereotypes based on training data), and political bias (skewing outputs toward dominant ideologies). Racial and other stereotypes permeate AI outputs; for example, facial recognition systems have higher error rates for minorities and women, and hiring algorithms have penalized feminine keywords.

Bias mitigation strategies target data preprocessing (reweighing, removing correlated features), inprocessing (fairness constraints, adversarial debiasing), and postprocessing (adjusting decision thresholds per group). Technical standards and regulatory interventions, such as the EU’s AI Act and US executive orders, are emerging to govern these risks. There is increasing concern about transparency, data governance, climate impacts of AI computation, and the dominance of big tech companies over open knowledge platforms.

Algorithmic bias is a systemic issue rooted in data collection, model design, and emergent feedback loops that perpetuate discrimination, especially where historical inequalities are baked into training sets. Solutions include technical audits, explainable AI, interdisciplinary collaboration, and regulatory mandates for fairness.

## Ethics & Politics of Artificial Intelligence

AI and robotics ethics center around designing systems capable of moral reasoning and responsible impacts. The debate includes robot ethics—how humans treat machines, the possibility of granting robots or AI legal rights, and the thresholds for artificial moral agency and consciousness. There are strong arguments against robot rights, emphasizing a lack of sentience and the risk of diluting human rights and accountability.

Ethical principles for AI commonly include transparency, fairness, non-maleficence, accountability, and explicability. Challenges include algorithmic biases, threats to human dignity (such as replacing empathetic roles with machines), liability issues with autonomous vehicles, weaponization concerns, and the risk of singularity or runaway superintelligence.

Incidents such as AI models producing unsafe responses, undesirable emergent behavior (e.g., blackmail or refusal to shut down), and manipulating cultural norms have prompted calls for better oversight, transparency, and the development of safety guardrails. Numerous governmental, academic, and private organizations are active in shaping AI policy and ethics.

## Technological Singularity and Transhumanism

The technological singularity hypothesizes a future point when artificial superintelligence triggers rapid, unpredictable social and technological transformation. Theoretical milestones and predictions (from Turing, von Neumann, Good, Vinge, Kurzweil) vary widely, with confidence in human-level AI—the first proposed necessary step—generally placed mid-century. The feasibility of singularity is hotly disputed: bottlenecks may arise in hardware, software, or economic factors; exponential growth trends may not persist.

While singularity is posited to bring benefits (solving disease, poverty, etc.), major risks include existential threats, economic upheaval, and loss of human control. AI safety research and technical alignment have become prominent, alongside debates on hard vs. soft takeoff scenarios.

Transhumanism, closely related, advocates for using science and technology to radically enhance human biological capacities—immortality, superintelligence, cognitive and physical augmentation. Its origins trace to Haldane, Bernal, Huxley, and more recently, Kurzweil and Moravec. The movement encompasses a range of philosophies (immortalism, democratic transhumanism, equalism, technogaianism) and provokes debate on feasibility, ethical risks, socioeconomic divides, identity, and legal questions. Critics caution against loss of human autonomy, existential risks, and the moral hazards of unequal enhancement.

## The Turing Test and the Chinese Room Argument

Alan Turing’s test for machine intelligence—a judge distinguishing between human and machine interlocutors—remains a benchmark for assessing AI. Its success is now empirically demonstrated by large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4.5, which have been identified as human more often than real humans under controlled conditions.

Yet, critics note that the Turing Test measures only human-like behavior, not genuine intelligence, understanding, or consciousness. It is susceptible to naïveté of interrogators, failure to measure superhuman intelligence, and focuses almost exclusively on language, ignoring non-linguistic cognition.

John Searle’s Chinese Room thought experiment challenges the idea that computational symbol manipulation alone yields understanding or consciousness, arguing for the insufficiency of "syntax" for "semantics." Replies to his argument range from system and virtual mind perspectives to appeals for embodiment and connectionism, but the debate over machine consciousness and the possibility of "strong AI" persists.

## Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition holds that cognitive processes are shaped by the body's interactions with its environment, contesting the notion of disembodied or brain-centric reasoning. Developed through phenomenology, neuroscience, and robotics, the theory emphasizes sensorimotor contingencies, perception, language, memory, learning, emotion, social cognition, and self-regulation—all as bodily-oriented processes.

Applications are widespread, including education (embodied learning strategies enhancing engagement and retention), AI and robotics (emphasis on situated interaction), clinical therapies, sports training, music cognition, and social psychology.

Controversies remain over the adequacy of embodied explanations, especially for preverbal infants, the replication crisis for certain embodied cognition findings, and the philosophical binding problem of how sensory constructs cohere. Interdisciplinary research and empirical studies continue to refine the scope and impact of embodiment on cognition.
