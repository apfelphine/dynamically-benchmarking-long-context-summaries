## Transhumanism
Transhumanism is a movement centered on fundamentally improving the human condition via technology, aiming for enhancements in lifespan, cognition, and body capabilities. Its roots trace back to ancient quests for immortality and philosophical traditions. Early 20th-century thinkers like Haldane and Bernal envisaged applying genetics and bionics to human evolution, while Julian Huxley coined the term. Since the 1980s, organizations and thinkers, such as FM-2030, Max More, and Nick Bostrom, have advanced transhumanist theory, focusing on human enhancement, technological singularity, life extension, and equal access to enhancement technologies.

The movement explores the implications and ethics of radically altering human nature—addressing issues like social justice, possible new species, and existential risks, alongside various currents including abolitionism, extropianism, and democratic transhumanism. Some engage with spirituality, seeing technology as a means to achieve goals long held by religions, but most emphasize practical improvements. Transhumanist practices include body modifications, cognitive enhancers, and support for technologies like nanotech, AI, mind uploading, and cryonics, with attention to cognitive liberty and morphological freedom.

The debate around transhumanism encompasses feasibility concerns, ethical risks (e.g., intrinsic immorality, loss of human identity, social divides), and possible catastrophic outcomes. Critics warn of dehumanization, unequal access, and environmental or existential threats. Supporters counter that advances can augment equality and societal well-being. Political, cultural, and eugenic dimensions further complicate its reception, especially as transhumanism is sometimes used as a propagandistic foil by anti-democratic movements.

## Chinese Room
The Chinese room is a thought experiment by John Searle challenging the notion that computers can "think" or "understand" as humans do. Searle imagines himself in a room manipulating Chinese symbols using a rule book, producing responses indistinguishable from a real Chinese speaker yet lacking understanding. He concludes that merely processing syntax (rules) is not sufficient for semantics (meaning/consciousness), thus refuting the "strong AI" position that the right program can create a mind.

The argument sparked extensive debate in philosophy and AI, raising questions about functionalism, consciousness, and the limits of computational models. Replies range from claims that the "system" as a whole might possess understanding, to arguments that real-world interaction or brain-like simulations could create genuine minds, and critiques that Searle's intuitions rely on questionable assumptions about consciousness and subjectivity. The Chinese room also highlights distinctions between simulating and having mental states, and remains a central point of contention regarding AI's potential for sentience.

## Algorithmic Bias
Algorithmic bias refers to systematic errors in computer algorithms that produce unfair outcomes favoring certain groups over others. Bias arises from human decisions in data selection, programming, and design, as well as from reinforcing stereotypes in machine learning models. Historical and contemporary failures—from medical school admissions to facial recognition systems—illustrate how algorithmic bias amplifies existing societal inequalities.

Bias can be pre-existing (rooted in social ideologies), technical (arising from system limitations), or emergent (due to new uses or contexts). Machine learning systems also perpetuate language, gender, political, and racial biases embedded in their training data. The impact spans commercial practices, employment, lending, law enforcement, healthcare, and more, often disadvantaging marginalized groups. Obstacles to research include defining fairness, system complexity, lack of transparency, and limited data on sensitive categories.

Solutions include technical audits, reweighing, transparency measures, regulatory frameworks (e.g., GDPR, AI Act), and promoting diversity in AI development. Bias mitigation is an active interdisciplinary research area, seeking to balance algorithmic efficiency with social equity.

## Turing Test
Proposed by Alan Turing, the Turing test is a method for assessing whether a machine exhibits human-like intelligence through a conversational imitation game. If an interrogator cannot reliably distinguish between a human and a computer based solely on responses, the machine is said to pass the test. The test influenced philosophical debates on machine intelligence, consciousness, and the distinction between simulating versus possessing a mind.

Variations include CAPTCHA (reverse Turing test), subject-matter expert tests, and extensions to embodied or non-linguistic intelligence. Large language models like ChatGPT and GPT-4.5 have recently passed rigorous Turing tests, highlighting both the progress and limitations (e.g., naivete of judges, difficulty measuring intelligence beyond human behaviors). Critics argue the test conflates human-likeness with intelligence, does not directly address consciousness, and may distract from practical AI goals.

## Ethics of Artificial Intelligence
AI ethics is concerned with the design and deployment of artificial moral agents and robots, addressing questions of transparency, fairness, accountability, and impact on society. Key issues include algorithmic bias, climate impacts, transparency, open-source dilemmas, strain on public knowledge platforms, regulatory needs, and liability in autonomous systems. Ethical challenges extend to worries about weaponization, misuse, existential risks (e.g., singularity), and threats to human dignity.

Debates encompass the potential rights or moral standing of robots/AI, with most scholars distinguishing technical personhood from moral patienthood. Solutions involve technical safeguards, interdisciplinary collaboration, regulatory frameworks, and monitoring. Institutional efforts include global initiatives, government policies, academic research, and advocacy organizations. AI ethics is deeply informed by history, fiction, and evolving societal values.

## Fairness in Machine Learning
Fairness in machine learning addresses the need for algorithms to provide equitable treatment across groups and individuals. Definitions are varied and sometimes mutually incompatible, leading to controversies when deploying AI in domains like criminal justice, employment, and healthcare. Fairness criteria are classed as group-based (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds), individual-based (similar individuals receive similar outcomes), or causality-based (counterfactual fairness).

Bias mitigation is pursued through data preprocessing, in-processing constraints, post-processing adjustments, and explainable AI. While technical measures improve fairness, ultimate outcomes often hinge on human decision-making, and philosophical and legal frameworks remain underdeveloped.

## Embodied Cognition
Embodied cognition is a theoretical framework arguing that cognition is shaped by bodily experiences and interactions with the world, in contrast to views that see the mind as divorced from physical reality. Rooted in philosophy, psychology, neuroscience, and AI, the theory emphasizes the importance of sensorimotor processes for perception, language, memory, learning, reasoning, emotion, and social cognition.

Applications span education, robotics, clinical psychology, sports, music, and social psychology, demonstrating benefits from integrating body-based experiences into cognitive and practical tasks. Controversies persist around methodological challenges and the interpretation of empirical findings, especially regarding the role of embodiment in early development and cognition.

## Technological Singularity
The technological singularity is a hypothesized future event where artificial intelligence will surpass human intelligence, leading to unpredictable and rapid societal transformation. The concept originated with John von Neumann and was popularized by Vernor Vinge and Ray Kurzweil. Central is the notion of an "intelligence explosion": an AI capable of recursive self-improvement would escalate its capabilities—potentially beyond human control.

Predictions about timing vary, with estimates ranging from the 2040s to the end of the 21st century. Critics challenge the plausibility due to technical, physical, and economic constraints, as well as issues like exponential growth limits, complexity brakes, job displacement, and risks of unfriendly AI. The singularity scenario encompasses broader questions about superintelligence, human enhancement, societal impacts, and evolving definitions of personhood and evolution. Efforts to align AI goals with human values and anticipate risks are ongoing in multiple research and policy arenas.
