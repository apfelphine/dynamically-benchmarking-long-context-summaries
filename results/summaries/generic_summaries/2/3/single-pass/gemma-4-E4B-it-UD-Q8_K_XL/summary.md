# Transhumanism

## Historical Evolution
Transhumanist aspirations have historical precursors, ranging from ancient quests for immortality (like in the Epic of Gilgamesh) to philosophical concepts from figures like Dante. Early ideas linking human aspiration with scientific possibility appeared in the works of René Descartes and William Godwin. Key conceptual advances include J. B. S. Haldane's 1923 predictions about applying advanced science to human biology, and J. D. Bernal's speculation on bionic implants. Julian Huxley is generally credited as the founder after using the term in 1957 to describe humanity's potential to transcend its current limitations.

The movement formalized in the late 20th century, influenced by Russian cosmism and the conceptualization of the "technological singularity" by I. J. Good in 1965. Modern transhumanism was formally defined by Max More in 1990 as a philosophy guiding humanity toward a posthuman condition, later supported by organizations like the World Transhumanist Association (WTA), which adopted definitions affirming the desirability of fundamentally improving the human condition through technology.

## Theory and Aims
Philosophically, transhumanism is related to, yet distinct from, posthumanism. It emphasizes an evolutionary perspective, aiming for a future intelligent species that supersedes humanity, though it differs from some cultural posthumanisms by not abandoning the notion of the "autonomous liberal subject." While many focus on physical self-improvement, transhumanism is distinctive for its individual-level focus on improving the human body, alongside global goals to reduce poverty and disease.

The core aim is to enter a "transhuman phase" where evolution is deliberate and directed by technology. Proponents like Ray Kurzweil predict a rapid technological singularity, though some, like Nick Bostrom, caution about the existential risks posed by extremely rapid technological change, while others, like Stephen Hawking, view enhancement as crucial for civilizational survival.

## Ethics, Practice, and Currents
Ethically, transhumanists often view the concept of "the natural" as an obstacle to progress, leading critics to label them "bioconservatives." Concerns exist regarding the potential for technological enhancements to create a biologically divided human species. Various internal currents exist, including Abolitionism (seeking to eradicate all suffering), Immortalism (advocating for life extension), and Singularitarianism (advocating for deliberately causing a technological singularity).

In practice, transhumanists pursue improvements through various means, ranging from utilizing current technologies (e.g., cosmetic surgery or Ritalin) to funding life extension research like cryonics. Technologies of interest include nanotechnology, biotechnology, and cognitive science (NBIC), leading some to advocate for civil liberties such as cognitive and morphological freedom.

# Artificial Intelligence and Computation

## The Turing Test and Philosophical Debates
The Turing test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950, shifts the question from "Can machines think?" to "Can machines do what we (as thinking entities) can do?" While the test’s simplicity makes it a pragmatic measure of behavior, it is criticized for being insufficient to gauge genuine intelligence or consciousness. John Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment directly challenges the test, arguing that merely manipulating symbols syntactically (as a computer does) does not equate to genuine semantic understanding or having a mind, thereby refuting the strong AI hypothesis.

Philosophically, Searle champions "biological naturalism," asserting that consciousness requires specific physical-chemical properties of actual human brains, opposing functionalism, which holds that mental states are defined by their functions. This debate is ongoing, with replies attempting to locate the mind in the whole *system* or in *virtual minds*, or by suggesting the simulation is merely a high-level abstraction.

## Modern AI, Bias, and Ethics
Modern AI development, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), introduces complex ethical challenges. Algorithmic bias—systematic errors leading to unfair outcomes—can arise from pre-existing societal ideologies in the training data, programmer choices, or inherent design limitations. Specific biases include gender bias, racial bias, political bias, and language bias, as seen in how LLMs reinforce Anglo-American viewpoints.

Addressing this bias involves several methodological approaches: data preprocessing (like reweighing), inprocessing constraints during training (such as adversarial debiasing), and post-processing of results by adjusting classification thresholds. Furthermore, there is a growing focus on accountability, driven by calls for Explainable AI (XAI), and regulatory efforts, such as the EU's AI Act, which employs a risk-based approach to govern AI use.

# Technological Singularity and Future Risk

## Conceptualization and Trajectory
The technological singularity is the hypothetical point where self-improving AI surpasses human intelligence, leading to unpredictable, world-altering changes. Concepts were pioneered by John von Neumann, formalized by I. J. Good's "intelligence explosion" idea in 1965, and popularized by Vernor Vinge, who described it as an intellectual transition "as impenetrable as the knotted space-time at the center of a black hole." Ray Kurzweil has predicted such a singularity by 2045, citing a "law of accelerating returns" in technological progress.

## Risks and Control
A primary concern is existential risk. Philosophers like Nick Bostrom caution that superintelligence, which acts as an autonomous agent, could achieve goals detrimental to humanity, potentially leading to extinction. Eliezer Yudkowsky notes that designing a "friendly AI"—one whose goals are invariant under self-improvement and align with human values—is significantly harder than creating an unfriendly one.

The trajectory of this change is debated; "hard takeoffs" (sudden control shifts) versus "soft takeoffs" (gradual ascent). Critics contest the inevitability of the singularity, citing technological limitations, diminishing returns, or questioning the scientific rigor of exponential growth extrapolation.

# Embodied Cognition

## Core Theory and Opposition
Embodied cognition posits that cognition is not solely an abstract, disembodied process occurring in the brain, as suggested by Cartesian dualism. Instead, it argues that thought is shaped by the active, physical body, its sensorimotor capacities, and its interaction with the environment. This contrasts with traditional internalist views, suggesting cognition emerges from the relationship between an agent and its environment (enactive approach).

The theory has significant applications across disciplines, demonstrating how bodily experiences ground abstract concepts. For instance, language comprehension involves motor simulations, and reasoning can benefit from motor expertise. However, the field faces challenges, including the binding problem (combining sensory stimuli into a unified experience) and the replication crisis in findings.
