**Final, Unified Summary (Refined and Compressed):**

Transhumanism and the technological singularity represent two central pillars of contemporary AI and futurist thought. Transhumanism advocates for the use of technology to enhance human capabilities—extending life, improving health, and transcending biological limits—through neural interfaces, cognitive drugs, and biological enhancements. Rooted in Enlightenment ideals and ancient immortality dreams, it is shaped by diverse philosophical currents, including singularitarianism, equalism, and abolitionism, and faces ethical challenges related to equity, dehumanization, and the moral status of artificial beings. The singularity—a hypothetical point at which artificial intelligence surpasses human intelligence—was first explored in science fiction and formalized by thinkers like Vernor Vinge, Ray Solomonoff, and Hans Moravec, with Vinge popularizing the term in the 1980s and emphasizing the impossibility of realistically depicting post-singularity intelligence. Ray Solomonoff mathematically described an "infinity point" in AI self-improvement, while Minsky and Tipler proposed related ideas on superintelligent machines and human-like robots. Ray Kurzweil advanced the concept through *The Singularity Is Near*, founding Singularity University, and promoting exponential growth models. Despite shared themes, definitions remain inconsistent and debated. Political and economic implications—including job displacement—have been highlighted by figures such as Barack Obama. Critics, including Pinker, Modis, and Lanier, challenge the feasibility of a sudden intelligence explosion, citing flawed assumptions, economic limits, and physical constraints. Risks such as uncontrolled AI, autonomous weapons, and misalignment with human values persist, as warned by Bostrom and Hawking. The singularity remains a powerful, contested concept shaping public discourse, policy, and research in AI safety and ethical governance. Embodied cognition, asserting that cognition arises from sensorimotor interaction with the environment, provides a complementary framework for understanding memory, learning, and social cognition—though it faces methodological challenges due to replication issues and priming effects. Together, these ideas reflect a dynamic, interdisciplinary landscape where technological progress, ethical responsibility, and human dignity are in constant negotiation.
