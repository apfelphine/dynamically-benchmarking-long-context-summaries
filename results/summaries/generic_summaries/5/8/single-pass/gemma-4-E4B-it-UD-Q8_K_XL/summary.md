# Blaise Pascal
Pascal was a French polymath, known for his early intellectual brilliance. His early life in Clermont-Ferrand led him to Paris, where at age 16, he produced *Essai pour les coniques*, his first serious work, which articulated Pascal's theorem on conics. His father, Étienne Pascal, eventually faced financial ruin due to Cardinal Richelieu's defaults, forcing the family from Paris.

In 1642, Pascal invented the Pascaline, a mechanical calculator for addition and subtraction. His mathematical contributions were profound: in 1654, his correspondence with Pierre de Fermat gave birth to the theory of probability, introducing expected value. He also created Pascal's triangle in his *Traité du triangle arithmétique* (published posthumously in 1665) and provided an early statement of the principle of mathematical induction.

Pascal made significant advances in physics, contributing to fluid mechanics, inventing the hydraulic press and syringe. In the study of vacuums, his experiments with barometers in 1647 and 1648 proved that atmospheric pressure varies with altitude, refuting the plenum theory. In his later years, he authored *The Provincial Letters* (1656–57), a scathing critique of casuistry, and his posthumously published *Pensées* is a masterpiece of French prose dealing with faith and the human condition.

# Richard Stallman
Richard Stallman, born in 1953, began his career in computing, earning degrees from Harvard and MIT. He was an early advocate for unrestricted access to computer systems, becoming a prominent hacker in MIT's AI Laboratory.

His shift came when proprietary software became the norm in the late 1970s, leading him to declare it a "crime against humanity" to restrict user freedom. In 1984, he quit MIT to develop the GNU project, aiming for a free operating system compatible with Unix. He popularized the concept of copyleft and released the GNU General Public License (GPL).

Stallman advocates fiercely for the philosophy of free software, emphasizing that freedom is a moral value, not just a pragmatic one. He campaigns against software patents and digital rights management (DRM), proposing a "reduced copyright." Despite controversy, he continues to champion free software and has returned to the Free Software Foundation (FSF).

# Hedy Lamarr
Hedy Lamarr (born Hedwig Eva Maria Kiesler) was an actress who achieved international fame in Hollywood, where she was promoted as the "world's most beautiful woman." Her career saw her frequently typecast as a glamorous seductress, despite her lack of acting challenges.

While married to arms dealer Fritz Mandl, Lamarr developed a latent talent for science, learning about military technology. In the late 1930s, she collaborated with composer George Antheil to invent a frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology to prevent torpedo jamming. This invention was granted a U.S. patent in 1942.

Lamarr’s later years involved increasing seclusion. Despite her acting career decline, her inventive contributions, particularly the frequency-hopping concept, have led to numerous accolades, including posthumous induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame and the naming of various technologies and awards in her honor.

# Alfred North Whitehead
Alfred North Whitehead was a mathematician and philosopher known for his profound metaphysical contributions. After studying at Cambridge, he collaborated with Bertrand Russell on *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), a landmark work in mathematical logic, though Gödel’s later theorem limited its ultimate scope.

Moving to the United States, his philosophical work evolved into process philosophy, detailed in *Process and Reality* (1929). He rejected scientific materialism, advocating for an event-based ontology where fundamental elements are interrelated and experiential, a concept often termed "panexperientialism."

Whitehead’s views on God differed from traditional views, proposing a "dipolar" God—a primordial, unchanging source of possibility, and a consequent nature that is eternally changed by the world. His philosophy has significantly influenced process theology, ecology, and modern educational theory.

# Ada Lovelace
Ada Lovelace, daughter of Lord Byron, displayed extraordinary mathematical aptitude from a young age, fostered by her mother’s desire to counteract perceived insanity in her father. She became closely associated with Charles Babbage and his Analytical Engine.

Lovelace translated Luigi Menabrea's paper on the Analytical Engine and augmented it with seven extensive notes. In Note G, she detailed an algorithm for calculating Bernoulli numbers, an accomplishment often recognized as the world's first published computer program. Critically, she grasped the Engine’s potential beyond mere number crunching, realizing it could manipulate symbols to express any process, marking a transition from calculation to general-purpose computation.

Her work remains foundational to computer science. The computer language Ada was named in her honor, and her vision of computation is central to modern theory, even as some scholars debate the extent of her direct programming contribution versus Babbage's prior work.

# Leonardo Torres Quevedo
Leonardo Torres Quevedo was a prodigious Spanish inventor and engineer. He started by experimenting with cableways and cable cars in Molledo, developing a safe, multi-cable support system for aerial transport, which was later applied to the Niagara Spanish Aerocar.

Torres was also a pioneer in analogue calculating machines, presenting work on algebraic machines to the Spanish Royal Academy of Sciences. He developed sophisticated mechanical devices, including a machine capable of computing the roots of arbitrary polynomials. Furthermore, he was a pioneer in remote control technology with the Telekino, demonstrating autonomous mechanical movement.

In theoretical computing, Torres introduced a formal language for describing mechanical drawings, comparable to modern programming languages. His work on the chess automaton, *El Ajedrecista*, is considered one of the earliest computer games, showcasing integrated electromechanical automation.

# Philip Rubin
Philip Rubin is a researcher whose work synthesizes computational, linguistic, physiological, and psychological approaches to embodied cognition, especially concerning speech. He is renowned for developing foundational models in speech synthesis and perception.

His early work at Haskins Laboratories included the ASY articulatory synthesis program, which controlled speech production by modeling vocal tract physiology. He further developed the SWS sinewave synthesis system, used to study spatiotemporal aspects of speech signals.

Rubin’s theoretical contributions emphasize a dynamical systems/action theory perspective on speech, focusing on the spatiotemporal coordination of global system aspects rather than isolated cues. He has also been heavily involved in science policy and ethics, serving in leadership roles at the National Science Foundation (NSF) and the Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) in the White House, focusing on human subjects research.

# Alan Turing
Alan Turing was a towering figure in mathematics and computer science. In the mid-1930s, he tackled the Entscheidungsproblem, reformulating Gödel's incompleteness theorems into his paper "On Computable Numbers," which introduced the concept of the Turing machine and the universal Turing machine, establishing the theoretical basis for all modern computation.

During World War II, he was a leading cryptanalyst at Bletchley Park, where he developed the functional specification of the bombe machine to break the Enigma cipher. He also devised the sophisticated Turingery technique against the Lorenz cipher.

Later, Turing made seminal contributions to mathematics and biology, inventing the Turing test to define machine intelligence. In mathematical biology, his "Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" (1952) proposed that reaction-diffusion systems could account for biological patterns. His life was marked by his homosexuality, which led to a conviction for "gross indecency" and subsequent chemical castration, resulting in his death in 1954.

# Vannevar Bush
Vannevar Bush was a key figure in American science administration and technology. Early in his career, he invented and patented the "profile tracer," a device for surveying. He made significant contributions to electrical engineering, including the development of the Spencer Thermostat and involvement with the Raytheon Company.

During World War II, Bush was instrumental in forming the National Defense Research Committee (NDRC) and later directing the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD), thereby shaping U.S. scientific research policy. He played a vital role in advocating for basic research, culminating in the establishment of the National Science Foundation (NSF).

His most influential theoretical concept, articulated in "As We May Think" (1945), was the memex—a conceptual device for memory augmentation. This vision is widely regarded as a precursor to the World Wide Web and modern information science, predicting the challenges of information overload.

# Charles Babbage
Charles Babbage was a seminal inventor and mathematician, considered the father of the computer. He was instrumental in founding the Royal Astronomical Society in 1820, motivated by the need to eliminate errors from mathematical and astronomical tables through mechanization.

He designed the Difference Engine, intended to automatically compute polynomial functions using finite differences. More significantly, he conceptualized the Analytical Engine, a general-purpose machine programmed via punched cards. This machine featured features like sequential control, branching, and looping, making it theoretically Turing-complete. Ada Lovelace helped him develop the first algorithm for this machine.

Babbage's work extended into economics with *On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures* (1832), where he introduced the "Babbage principle," arguing that efficient division of labor increases profitability. His interests also spanned metrology, cryptography, and natural theology, showing a broad scientific curiosity.
