# Blaise Pascal  
Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, writer, and philosopher. Early gifted in mathematics, by age 16 he formulated Pascal's theorem in projective geometry. He invented the Pascaline mechanical calculator at 19 to aid his father’s tax calculations. Pascal collaborated with Fermat to found probability theory, introduced Pascal's triangle with recursive properties, and contributed to cycloid studies. In physics, he advanced fluid mechanics and validated the concept of vacuum, notably via the Puy de Dôme barometric experiment. Pascal experienced a deep religious conversion, producing influential works such as the *Provincial Letters* critiquing Jesuit casuistry and *Pensées*, a profound defense of Christianity. His legacy endures in mathematics, philosophy, and theology.  

# Richard Stallman  
Richard Stallman (b. 1953) is an American programmer and free software activist. Early exposed to computing through summer work and university programs, Stallman excelled at Harvard and MIT, working at the MIT AI Lab on software like Emacs. Disturbed by the rise of proprietary software restricting user freedoms, he launched the GNU Project (1983) to create free Unix-compatible software and founded the Free Software Foundation (1985) to promote software freedom. He pioneered the concept of copyleft with the GNU General Public License, enabling legal protection for free software. Stallman has been a controversial figure due to his uncompromising stance on software freedom and public comments, leading to his temporary resignations in 2019 and return in 2021 amid community debate. Besides software development, he advocates against proprietary software, DRM, and surveillance.  

# Hedy Lamarr  
Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000), born Hedwig Kiesler, was an Austrian-American actress renowned for her beauty and Hollywood film career, including *Samson and Delilah* (1950). Secretly a gifted inventor, Lamarr co-developed a frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology during World War II—originally intended to guide torpedoes—though initially ignored by the Navy. This invention laid groundwork for modern wireless communication. Despite six marriages and personal struggles, she maintained a creative life inventing devices such as improved traffic stoplights. Lamarr was posthumously recognized for her scientific contributions, receiving awards like the Electronic Frontier Foundation’s Pioneer Award and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. She remains an iconic figure in both entertainment and technology history.  

# Alfred North Whitehead  
Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician, logician, and philosopher. Initially a Cambridge mathematician, he co-authored *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a seminal work in mathematical logic. Later shifting to philosophy, Whitehead became known for "process philosophy," emphasizing reality as interrelated events rather than static matter, and proposing a metaphysics integrating experience and creativity. At Harvard, he wrote major philosophical works including *Science and the Modern World* and *Process and Reality*, though his writings were noted for their difficulty. Whitehead’s thought influenced theology (process theology), ecology, education, and various scientific disciplines, and continues to inspire interdisciplinary study, particularly in North America and China.  

# Ada Lovelace  
Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), daughter of Lord Byron, was an English mathematician regarded as the first computer programmer. Educated in mathematics and science due to her mother's insistence, she became close to Charles Babbage and translated Luigi Menabrea’s paper on his Analytical Engine, augmenting it with extensive notes including a detailed algorithm for Bernoulli numbers. Her insights anticipated the capability of computers to process symbolic not just numeric data and foreshadowed modern computing. Ada also emphasized imagination in mathematical applications. Despite controversies over extent of her contributions, her legacy is celebrated, inspiring computer language and awards named Ada, and an annual Ada Lovelace Day promoting women in STEM.  

# Leonardo Torres Quevedo  
Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a Spanish engineer and inventor. Known for pioneering work in cable cars, he designed and built early aerial cableways including the enduring Niagara Spanish Aerocar. He developed early analog computing machines capable of solving polynomial equations via novel mechanical-logarithmic methods. Torres invented the Telekino, one of the first remote control systems demonstrated with wireless radio, and constructed the first electromechanical chess-playing automaton, *El Ajedrecista*, capable of autonomous play. He contributed to dirigibles aerostatics and proposed innovations such as mooring masts for airships. Torres was a key figure in early automation and computing theories, earning numerous honors and leaving a lasting technological legacy in Spain and beyond.  

# Philip Rubin  
Philip Rubin (b. 1949) is an American cognitive scientist and speech researcher known for his interdisciplinary work on speech production, perception, and synthesis. At Haskins Laboratories, he developed articulatory and sinewave synthesis models (ASY, CASY, SWS) advancing computational speech modeling. Rubin introduced the concept of speech gestures as dynamical control units and explored audiovisual speech perception. He served as a science administrator at NSF and OSTP, leading programs in cognitive neuroscience and science policy, and advocating ethical standards in human research. Rubin continues contributions in behavioral science leadership and science advocacy, combining technical innovation with policy expertise.  

# Alan Turing  
Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, logician, and pioneer of computer science. He formalized the concept of algorithms with the Turing machine and proved the undecidability of the halting problem, foundational in computability theory. During WWII, Turing played a crucial role at Bletchley Park in breaking German Enigma and Lorenz ciphers using the design of the bombe machine and statistical techniques, significantly shortening the war. Postwar, he contributed to early computer designs (ACE), proposed the Turing test for machine intelligence, and initiated computational approaches to biology by modeling morphogenesis. Convicted in 1952 for homosexuality, then illegal, he underwent chemical castration and died under ambiguous circumstances, likely suicide, in 1954. His legacy endures as a founder of modern computing and artificial intelligence.  

# Vannevar Bush  
Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was an influential American engineer, inventor, and science administrator. Educated at Tufts and MIT, he invented the differential analyzer, an early analog computer. As president of the Carnegie Institution and chairman of NACA, he shaped US scientific policy pre-WWII. During WWII, he led the National Defense Research Committee and Office of Scientific Research and Development, coordinating key projects including radar development, proximity fuzes, and the Manhattan Project. Postwar, Bush advocated for sustained federal support of basic scientific research in *Science, The Endless Frontier*, laying groundwork for the National Science Foundation. He coined the concept of the memex, a theoretical mechanized information system anticipating hypertext and the Web. Bush’s legacy is foundational to American science and technology policy.  

# Charles Babbage  
Charles Babbage (1791–1871) was an English mathematician, philosopher, inventor, and mechanical engineer credited as a "father of the computer." He conceived the Difference Engine to automate polynomial calculations and later developed the more general Analytical Engine, incorporating programmable punched cards, loops, and conditional branching—concepts foundational to modern computers. Though neither machine was completed in his lifetime due to funding and personality conflicts, his designs anticipated computer architecture. Collaborating with Ada Lovelace, he inspired the first algorithm for the Analytical Engine. Babbage was also notable for contributions to economics (division of labor), cryptography, natural theology, and founding scientific societies. His legacy persists in computing and Victorian science history.
