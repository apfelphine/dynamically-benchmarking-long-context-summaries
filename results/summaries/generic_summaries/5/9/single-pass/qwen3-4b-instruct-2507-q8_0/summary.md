### Richard Stallman
Richard Stallman, born in 1953, is a computer scientist and the founder of the free software movement. He began his career at MIT, where he developed the GNU project and the GNU General Public License (GPL), advocating for software freedom and user rights. He promoted copyleft, ensuring that software remains free and open for modification and distribution. Stallman criticized proprietary software and DRM, promoting a "reduced copyright" model. He is known for his activism, including opposing software patents and advocating for open-source and free software. His work has been controversial, especially regarding his past statements on child pornography and his relationship with Epstein. He resigned from MIT and the Free Software Foundation in 2019 due to controversy but later returned. Stallman emphasizes terminology, rejecting "open-source" in favor of "free software," and insists on using "GNU/Linux" to honor the project's philosophy.

### Alfred North Whitehead
Alfred North Whitehead, born in 1861, was a British mathematician and philosopher. He studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, and later became a prominent figure in philosophy, known for his work on process philosophy. His major contributions include *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell and *The Concept of Nature*. Whitehead's philosophy emphasizes dynamic, process-based reality, where events are primary and interconnected, rejecting static matter. He believed in a universe where change is fundamental and entities are defined by their experiences and relationships. His work influenced theology, education, and science, particularly in ecology and environmental ethics. He is remembered for his complex metaphysical system, which views reality as a network of interrelated events, and for his advocacy of education as a holistic, experiential process.

### Blaise Pascal
Blaise Pascal, born in 1623, was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, and philosopher. Early in life, he demonstrated exceptional mathematical talent, producing a significant work on conics. He invented the mechanical calculator, the Pascaline, and made foundational contributions to probability theory through his work with Pierre de Fermat. His *Pensées* is a philosophical masterpiece exploring faith, human nature, and the existence of God. In physics, Pascal contributed to fluid dynamics, proving that hydrostatic pressure depends on elevation, and conducted experiments on vacuum, challenging the idea of an "aether" filling space. He also invented the first known public transportation system, the carrosses à cinq sols. Pascal's legacy includes foundational work in mathematics, probability, and philosophy, and he is remembered as a pioneer in both scientific and spiritual thought.

### Leonardo Torres Quevedo
Leonardo Torres Quevedo, born in 1852, was a Spanish engineer and inventor. He is known for pioneering work in cableways, mechanical calculating machines, and remote control technology. He designed the first aerial cable car and the Spanish Aerocar, a cableway spanning Niagara Falls. He developed an early mechanical calculating machine using logarithmic scales and invented the "endless spindle" mechanism. Torres also designed an early airship with a trilobed structure and developed the "Telekino," one of the first radio-controlled devices. He introduced a formal language for describing mechanical drawings and was a key figure in the development of automation. His work in computing and remote control laid the groundwork for modern engineering and robotics, and he is celebrated for his innovative, interdisciplinary contributions to technology.

### Hedy Lamarr
Hedy Lamarr, born in 1914, was an Austrian-born actress and inventor. She gained fame through her film roles, particularly in *Ecstasy* and *Samson and Delilah*, and was known for her glamorous image. During World War II, she co-invented frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology with composer George Antheil, a method now used in wireless communication to prevent jamming. Though initially rejected by the U.S. military, her invention laid the foundation for modern Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Lamarr later became a vocal advocate for women in science and technology. She is recognized as a pioneer in both entertainment and engineering, and her legacy includes being honored with a Google Doodle, a NASA asteroid named after her, and a statue in Vienna.

### Alan Turing
Alan Turing, born in 1912, was a British mathematician and computer scientist. He is best known for his foundational work in computing, including the concept of the Turing machine and the Turing test, which evaluates machine intelligence. During World War II, he played a crucial role in breaking German ciphers at Bletchley Park, contributing to the war effort. He developed early computers, including the ACE, and made pioneering contributions to mathematical biology with his theory of morphogenesis, which explains how patterns form in living organisms. Turing faced persecution for being gay, being convicted of "gross indecency" and subjected to chemical castration. He died in 1954, widely believed to have taken his own life. In 2009, the UK government formally apologized for his treatment, and he is now celebrated as a key figure in computer science and artificial intelligence.

### Ada Lovelace
Ada Lovelace, born in 1815, was a British mathematician and writer, known as the world's first computer programmer. She collaborated with Charles Babbage on the Analytical Engine, writing detailed notes that included an algorithm for computing Bernoulli numbers—considered the first published computer program. She foresaw that machines could manipulate symbols beyond numbers, a key insight in the development of modern computing. Lovelace also explored the potential of computing in music and art, and her work laid the theoretical foundation for programming. She is celebrated for her visionary thinking and is honored through the Ada programming language, Ada Lovelace Day, and numerous awards and institutions named after her.

### Charles Babbage
Charles Babbage, born in 1800, was a British mathematician and mechanical engineer, often called the "father of the computer." He designed the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine, early mechanical computing devices that laid the groundwork for modern computers. His work on automated calculation and programmable machines influenced the development of computing. Babbage also contributed to economics, publishing *On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures*, which introduced the "Babbage principle" of division of labor. He was involved in cryptography, meteorology, and the early development of scientific societies. Though his machines were never fully completed due to funding and technical challenges, his ideas were foundational to the field of computer science and are recognized in modern computing, including the Babbage Building at the University of Plymouth and the Babbage Award.

### Philip Rubin
Philip Rubin is a psychologist and computational scientist known for his work in speech synthesis, articulatory modeling, and embodied cognition. He developed the ASY and SWS programs for articulatory synthesis and contributed to the theoretical understanding of speech production as a dynamic, gestural process. His work on sinewave synthesis and perceptual organization has influenced speech perception research. He served in key leadership roles in science policy, including at the National Science Foundation and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy. Rubin is also known for his advocacy in science ethics, particularly regarding human subjects research and the use of technology in intelligence and security. He has held leadership roles in academic and scientific organizations and is recognized for bridging research and policy in behavioral and cognitive sciences.

### Vannevar Bush
Vannevar Bush, born in 1890, was an American scientist and administrator who played a pivotal role in U.S. science policy during World War II. As chairman of the National Defense Research Committee (NDRC) and later the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD), he coordinated scientific efforts to support the war, including the development of radar, proximity fuzes, and the atomic bomb. He advocated for the Manhattan Project and promoted scientific collaboration between the U.S. and Britain. After the war, he championed the creation of the National Science Foundation (NSF) to support basic research. His influential essay "As We May Think" introduced the concept of the memex, a precursor to the modern internet and digital information systems. Bush is remembered as a visionary leader who helped shape the U.S. scientific and technological landscape.
