# Concise Summary

## Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman, born in 1953 in New York City, demonstrated early interest and aptitude in computers and science, leading him to Harvard University and the MIT AI Laboratory, where he contributed pioneering work in AI and software development. Frustrated by the rise of proprietary software, he spearheaded the GNU Project in 1984 to create a free, Unix-compatible operating system and founded the Free Software Foundation. Stallman advocated the concept of “copyleft” and the GNU General Public License (GPL), shaping the free software movement and influencing the development of tools like Emacs, GCC, and the naming debate over GNU/Linux.

Stallman’s activism extends to essays and speeches on software freedom, copyright reduction, and technological ethics. He protests against software patents, DRM, and surveillance, and is known for his focus on precise terminology like “free software” over “open source.” He has campaigned globally for free software adoption by governments and institutions.

He became embroiled in controversy related to comments on the Jeffrey Epstein scandal and resigned from MIT and FSF in 2019, returning to FSF’s board in 2021 amidst continued debate. Stallman lives in Boston, writes parody music, and in 2023 announced remission from follicular lymphoma. He has been honored with numerous awards for his technical and social contributions.

## Alfred North Whitehead

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician and philosopher. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, he contributed foundational works to mathematics, such as "A Treatise on Universal Algebra" and the co-authored "Principia Mathematica" with Bertrand Russell, which became a cornerstone in mathematical logic despite Gödel’s later proof of the limits of formal systems.

Whitehead’s career evolved from mathematics at Cambridge and Imperial College London to philosophy and administration, culminating in his influential tenure at Harvard. He is renowned for process philosophy, arguing that reality is fundamentally relational and event-based rather than a collection of static objects. In metaphysics, he proposed “process and reality” as interconnected events, developed a unique theory of perception (“prehension”), and formulated a novel concept of God that influenced process theology.

Whitehead’s views on education stressed imagination, interdisciplinary learning, and the dangers of rote knowledge. His legacy spans science, ecology, education, and theology, with growing interest in his ideas globally, including in China. Although his philosophical work is noted for its difficulty, it continues to inspire transdisciplinary approaches in ecology, business, and theology.

## Blaise Pascal

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, writer, and philosopher. He showed precocious talent in mathematics, pioneering the study of conic sections and inventing the Pascaline, an early mechanical calculator. Pascal’s work in probability theory with Fermat laid the groundwork for the field and introduced concepts like expected value.

In physics, Pascal contributed to fluid mechanics, hydrostatics (Pascal’s law), and the study of vacuum and air pressure, conducting foundational experiments that led to the SI unit “pascal.” His adult life was marked by religious engagement with Jansenism, followed by a dramatic conversion and religious writings, notably the "Provincial Letters" and "Pensées," which defend faith through philosophical reasoning and probabilistic argument (Pascal’s Wager).

Pascal’s philosophy emphasized fideism and critiqued both rationalism and empiricism. He influenced French prose and literature, developed principles in mathematical induction and binomial coefficients (Pascal’s triangle), and is recognized as a precursor to public transportation. His legacy endures in mathematics, philosophy, cultural influences, and commemorative institutions.

## Leonardo Torres Quevedo

Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a Spanish engineer and inventive pioneer in mechanics, automation, and computing. He developed robust cableway systems (notably the Niagara Spanish Aerocar), advanced analog and digital calculating machines (precursors to modern computers), and significant innovations in aerostatics, including his trilobed airships used in WWI.

Torres pioneered radio control technology (Telekino), invented automated chess-playing machines (El Ajedrecista), and proposed designs applying floating-point arithmetic and program control, foreshadowing contemporary digital computers. He contributed to formal language for machine description, educational technology, and naval designs, and advocated Esperanto for international cooperation.

His work was celebrated posthumously through research institutes, awards, statues, and continued operation of his inventions. Torres influenced modern engineering, robotics, computational theory, and remains important in the history of automation and computing.

## Hedy Lamarr

Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) was an Austrian-American film actress and inventor. After a successful European acting career and a controlling marriage to munitions magnate Friedrich Mandl, she escaped to Hollywood, starring in numerous films and gaining fame for her beauty.

During WWII, Lamarr, with composer George Antheil, invented frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology to prevent torpedo jamming, a breakthrough that became foundational for secure wireless communications. Despite the U.S. Navy’s rejection, the technology underpins today's Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and GPS.

Her later years included legal battles, seclusion, and recognition of her scientific achievements. Lamarr was posthumously honored with numerous awards, induction to the National Inventors Hall of Fame, and cultural tributes. Her inventive legacy is as significant as her cinematic career.

## Alan Turing

Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, computer scientist, logician, and cryptanalyst. Educated at Cambridge and Princeton, he formulated the concept of the Turing machine—the foundation of modern computers—and proved the Entscheidungsproblem unsolvable, laying the groundwork for computational theory.

During WWII, Turing led efforts at Bletchley Park to break the German Enigma and Lorenz ciphers, inventing the “bombe” and contributing decisive cryptanalytic advances. Postwar, he designed early computers (ACE, Manchester Mark 1), pioneered the concept of artificial intelligence (the Turing test), and contributed to mathematical biology with work on morphogenesis.

Turing was persecuted for homosexuality, convicted in 1952 and subjected to chemical castration. He died in 1954, with suicide widely presumed but contested. He was posthumously pardoned, and the “Alan Turing law” now retroactively pardons those convicted under historical anti-homosexuality statutes. Turing’s legacy shapes computing, AI, cryptography, and LGBTQ+ rights.

## Ada Lovelace

Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), daughter of Lord Byron, was an English mathematician and writer. Her mathematical education intertwined with her social standing and health struggles. Lovelace is renowned for translating and expanding Luigi Menabrea’s notes on Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine, articulating its potential to process not just numbers, but any symbols, anticipating general-purpose computing and computer programming.

Her detailed “Notes” included what is considered the first published computer program (although Babbage authored earlier, unpublished programs). Lovelace foresaw computers composing music and manipulating symbols, distinguishing between mechanism and logic. Though her contributions were later debated, her vision for computing’s possibilities is widely celebrated.

Ada Lovelace is memorialized in programming languages, medals, commemorations, and by influential annual events promoting women in STEM.

## Charles Babbage

Charles Babbage (1791–1871) was an English mathematician, inventor, and mechanical engineer. Dissatisfied with mathematical errors in human-calculated tables, he conceptualized and partially built the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine—mechanical devices that laid the blueprint for modern computers, including features like conditional branching, memory, and input/output separation.

Babbage’s early career involved reforming British science, contributions to industrial economics (Babbage principle), science policy, and cryptography. He fostered associations with leading scientists and reformist groups, influencing the founding of the Royal Astronomical Society and the British Association for the Advancement of Science.

Although his engines were incomplete, later reconstructions proved their viability, and his impact extends to computation, operational research, and scientific administration. His collaborations with Ada Lovelace and influence on generations are foundational to computer science.

## Philip Rubin

Philip Rubin (b. 1949) is an American cognitive scientist and science policy leader. He is recognized for innovations in speech synthesis, articulatory and sinewave synthesis, and computational modeling, including key software systems (ASY, SWS, CASY, TADA). His research advanced understanding of embodied cognition, perceptual organization, dynamical systems, events, gestures, and multimodal speech.

Rubin held numerous science administration roles, including Director of Behavioral and Cognitive Sciences at NSF, Assistant Director at the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, and leadership in FABBS and national ethics committees. He played a major role in neuroscience initiatives, ethical research oversight, and public science advocacy.

His honors include fellowships in major scientific societies and leadership in educational, regulatory, and research organizations. Rubin’s influence spans linguistic science, technology, policy, bioethics, and popular culture.

## Vannevar Bush

Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was an American engineer, inventor, and science administrator. He developed analog computers (differential analyzer), advanced vacuum tube technology, and co-founded Raytheon. As President of the Carnegie Institution and advisor during WWII, Bush organized U.S. scientific mobilization, leading the National Defense Research Committee and the Office of Scientific Research and Development, overseeing innovations like radar, the proximity fuze, and the Manhattan Project.

After WWII, Bush advocated for government support of basic research, influencing U.S. science policy through his seminal report "Science, The Endless Frontier." He conceptualized the “memex,” a microfilm-based information retrieval system, foreshadowing hypertext and the World Wide Web. Bush’s efforts led to the creation of the National Science Foundation.

He served on major panels, board memberships, and received numerous honors, including the National Medal of Science. Bush remains a foundational figure in U.S. science policy, technology, and the development of large-scale research infrastructure.
