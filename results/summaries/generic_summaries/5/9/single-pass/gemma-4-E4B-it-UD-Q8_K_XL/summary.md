## Richard Stallman
Richard Stallman was born in 1953 and initially showed interest in computers early on. His early professional experience included writing programs at IBM and later working in the hacker community at MIT, where he was known as RMS.

The shift towards proprietary software in the late 1970s spurred his activism. He founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF) and initiated the GNU project in 1983, advocating for the freedom to study and modify software. Stallman popularized copyleft through the GNU General Public License (GPL).

## Alfred North Whitehead
Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was an English mathematician and philosopher. His early career involved significant contributions to mathematics, including co-authoring *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell.

He later dedicated himself to philosophy, developing "process philosophy." This metaphysical system rejects the idea of static, irreducible matter in favor of an event-based ontology where reality is composed of interrelated processes. His philosophy also offered perspectives on education, value, and a unique conception of God.

## Blaise Pascal
Blaise Pascal was a brilliant 17th-century mathematician and physicist. He made foundational contributions to probability theory through correspondence with Pierre de Fermat, leading to the introduction of the concept of expected value.

Pascal also invented the mechanical calculator, the Pascaline. In physics, he was instrumental in proving that atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude through his experiments concerning the vacuum. In literature, his *Provincial Letters* is celebrated for its wit and satire.

## Leonardo Torres Quevedo
Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a prodigious Spanish engineer. His early work focused on developing cableways and cable cars, leading to patented safety systems for transport.

He was a pioneer in analogue calculating machines, presenting detailed theoretical and working prototypes for complex computations. Furthermore, Torres pioneered radio control with the Telekino and contributed to formal language design for mechanical drawings.

## Hedy Lamarr
Hedy Lamarr was an actress whose early fame brought her to Hollywood, where she was often typecast as a glamorous seductress. Despite her film career, she was an inventor.

In collaboration with composer George Antheil, Lamarr developed the concept of frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology to guide torpedoes, an invention later recognized as a foundation for modern wireless security. She received numerous awards for her contributions to both film and invention.

## Alan Turing
Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a foundational figure in computer science and artificial intelligence. His 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers" introduced the concept of the Turing machine, proving the limits of computation and establishing the theoretical basis for modern computing.

During WWII, he was a leading cryptanalyst at Bletchley Park, where his work on the Enigma cipher, including the design of the bombe, was crucial to shortening the war in Europe. Later, he applied his ideas to mathematical biology, publishing "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis."

## Ada Lovelace
Ada Lovelace (1815–1852) is recognized as an early visionary in computing. Working with Charles Babbage on the Analytical Engine, she translated Luigi Menabrea's notes and added extensive annotations.

Her most significant contribution, Note G, detailed an algorithm for calculating Bernoulli numbers using the machine, a concept often cited as the first published computer program. She recognized the machine's potential extended beyond mere number crunching to manipulating symbols, anticipating the concept of general-purpose computation.

## Charles Babbage
Charles Babbage (1792–1871) is widely considered the "father of the computer." He designed two major mechanical calculating machines: the Difference Engine, intended to compute polynomial functions, and the Analytical Engine.

The Analytical Engine, which featured a design separating data and program memory and used punched cards for programming, is considered a precursor to the modern computer and theoretically Turing-complete. He was also an influential thinker on industrial organization and natural theology.

## Philip Rubin
Philip Rubin's research integrates computational, linguistic, and physiological approaches to study embodied cognition, particularly focusing on the biology of speech and language.

He pioneered the design of computational models like ASY (articulatory synthesis) and SWS (sinewave synthesis), which model the physiology and acoustics of speech production. Furthermore, he has been deeply involved in science policy, serving in leadership roles at the NSF and OSTP, focusing on research ethics and human subjects protection.

## Vannevar Bush
Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was a pioneering scientist and administrator. He worked extensively at the Carnegie Institution of Washington and the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA), significantly influencing the direction of American science and defense research during WWII.

His most famous contribution is the "memex" concept, detailed in his 1945 essay "As We May Think." This visionary concept proposed a mechanized, microfilm-based device designed for associative information retrieval, which is regarded as a precursor to the World Wide Web.
