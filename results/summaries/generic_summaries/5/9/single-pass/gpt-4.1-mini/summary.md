# Summary

## Richard Stallman
Richard Stallman (born 1953) is a pioneering figure in the free software movement. Raised in New York City and educated at Harvard and MIT, he became a key hacker at MIT's AI Lab, contributing to projects like Emacs and Lisp machine OSes. Reacting against proprietary software restrictions in the late 1970s and early 1980s, Stallman launched the GNU Project in 1983 to create a fully free Unix-compatible operating system and founded the Free Software Foundation. He introduced the concept of copyleft and the GNU General Public License (GPL). While the GNU system lacked a kernel, Linux filled that gap, leading to the GNU/Linux naming debate. Stallman has been an outspoken advocate for software freedom, opposing DRM, software patents, and surveillance, emphasizing moral principles behind free software.

Controversies include his 2019 resignation from MIT and FSF following backlash over comments about the Jeffrey Epstein scandal and previous writings on underage sex laws, though he later rejoined the FSF board in 2021 amid divided community responses. Stallman continues to influence free software advocacy and development.

## Alfred North Whitehead
Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was an English mathematician and philosopher, known for co-authoring *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell and for founding process philosophy. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, Whitehead initially focused on mathematics and physics before turning to philosophy in later life. His major mathematical works include *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* and *An Introduction to Mathematics*.

Philosophically, Whitehead rejected materialism and substance-based ontology, proposing a process-oriented, event-based metaphysics where reality consists of interrelated occasions of experience. His metaphysics integrates God as a dipolar entity engaged with the universe's ongoing process. Whitehead's work influenced theology (process theology), education reform emphasizing imagination and interconnected knowledge, ecology, and organizational theory. Although his philosophy is complex and not widely understood, it continues to inspire diverse fields globally, notably in China and American progressive theology.

## Blaise Pascal
Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, writer, and Christian philosopher. A child prodigy educated by his father, he made early contributions to geometry and mechanical calculators, inventing the Pascaline to aid tax calculations. Alongside Pierre de Fermat, he laid foundational work for probability theory.

Pascal's work in physics includes studies on fluid pressure (Pascal's law), vacuum, and atmospheric pressure. He experienced a profound Jansenist religious conversion in 1654, which greatly influenced his writings, including the *Pensées* and *Provincial Letters*, known for their literary and theological impact. Pascal’s philosophical stance combined skepticism of pure reason with faith-based fideism. He also pioneered early public transportation concepts. Pascal died from illness at 39, leaving a legacy in mathematics, science, and philosophy.

## Leonardo Torres Quevedo
Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a Spanish engineer and inventor with contributions spanning cableways, analog computation, aerostatics, remote control, and early computing. He developed pioneering cable car systems, including the lasting Niagara Spanish Aerocar. Torres built analog calculating machines capable of solving complex polynomials and designed dirigibles with novel mooring methods.

He invented the Telekino, an early radio control system, which demonstrated one of the first unmanned remotely controlled vehicles. Torres also created *El Ajedrecista*, an electromechanical chess automaton capable of autonomous play, considered the first computer game. His 1914 essays on Automatics foreshadowed future developments in automation and computing. Despite facing skepticism, his innovations laid groundwork in computing, robotics, and transportation. Torres was a devout Catholic, active Esperantist, and received numerous honors in Spain and internationally. Several institutions and awards commemorate his legacy.

## Hedy Lamarr
Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) was an Austrian-American actress and inventor. Starting in European cinema with controversial fame from the film *Ecstasy*, Lamarr moved to Hollywood in 1938, becoming a renowned film star known for glamorous roles. She was married six times and had three children.

Beyond acting, Lamarr co-invented frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology during WWII with composer George Antheil to improve torpedo guidance and prevent signal jamming. Though initially rejected by the Navy, this invention laid the foundation for modern wireless communications, including Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Lamarr received posthumous recognition for her scientific contributions and was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. She lived her later years privately, passing away from heart disease.

## Alan Turing
Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, logician, and cryptanalyst, foundational to computer science and artificial intelligence. Educated at Cambridge and Princeton, he formulated the concept of the Turing machine, proving limits of computation and solving the Entscheidungsproblem. His work established the theoretical basis for modern computers.

During WWII, Turing led codebreaking efforts against the German Enigma cipher at Bletchley Park, developing the bombe machine instrumental in Allied victory. Post-war, Turing contributed to early computer designs, proposed the Turing test for machine intelligence, and pioneered research in mathematical biology with his morphogenesis model.

Turing was prosecuted in 1952 for homosexuality, leading to chemical castration. He died in 1954 from cyanide poisoning, with debate about suicide or accident. In 2013 he received a posthumous royal pardon, and his legacy is celebrated for its profound impact on science and technology.

## Ada Lovelace
Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), daughter of poet Lord Byron, was an English mathematician and writer considered the first computer programmer. Educated rigorously in mathematics to counteract her father's perceived madness, she collaborated with Charles Babbage, translating and expanding upon his *Analytical Engine* lectures. Her notes include what is recognized as the first algorithm intended for implementation on a machine.

Lovelace envisioned the Analytical Engine’s capabilities beyond computation to symbolic manipulation, foreseeing modern computing’s versatility. Her work emphasized intuition and imagination in science. Despite illness and personal challenges, she produced foundational contributions to computing theory. Numerous computing languages, awards, and events bear her name to honor her influence on STEM and women in technology.

## Charles Babbage
Charles Babbage (1791–1871) was an English mathematician, philosopher, inventor, and mechanical engineer, dubbed the "father of the computer." He originated the concept of programmable computing machines. Babbage designed and partially built the Difference Engine to automate mathematical table calculations and conceived the more advanced Analytical Engine, the first design for a general-purpose computer employing features like punch-card programming and conditional control flow.

A fellow of the Royal Society and Lucasian Professor, he was also active in public life and scientific reform. Babbage’s work influenced economics, manufacturing, cryptography, and natural theology. Despite funding and political challenges preventing full realization of his machines during his life, modern reconstructions demonstrate the feasibility of his designs. His legacy endures as a pioneer of computing and industrial science.

## Philip Rubin
Philip Rubin (born 1949) is an American cognitive scientist and speech researcher specializing in articulatory synthesis, speech perception, and signal processing. Educated at Brandeis and the University of Connecticut, Rubin developed influential computational models like ASY (articulatory synthesizer), SWS (sinewave synthesis), and CASY, advancing the understanding of how speech is produced and perceived.

He contributed to theoretical frameworks incorporating dynamical systems, gestures, and embodiment in speech science. Rubin has held leadership roles at Haskins Laboratories, the National Science Foundation (NSF), and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP), advocating for interdisciplinary research, neuroscience, and ethical oversight in human-subjects research and emerging technologies. He remains a prominent figure linking cognitive science, linguistics, and science policy.

## Vannevar Bush
Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was an American engineer, inventor, and science administrator. Educated at Tufts and MIT, he invented early analog computing devices such as the differential analyzer. He co-founded firms including Raytheon and contributed to circuit theory and electrical engineering.

During WWII, Bush headed the National Defense Research Committee and the Office of Scientific Research and Development, coordinating scientific research efforts including radar development, the Manhattan Project, and the proximity fuze. He championed government support for basic research, leading to the creation of the National Science Foundation.

Bush is renowned for the visionary 1945 essay "As We May Think," which conceptualized the memex, a precursor to hypertext and the modern internet. He influenced post-war science policy and received numerous honors for his leadership in science and technology.
