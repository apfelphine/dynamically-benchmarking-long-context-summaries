## Richard Stallman: Software Freedom and Hacker Culture

Richard Stallman, born in 1953, revolutionized software freedom and hacker culture through his work at Harvard and MIT, creation of EMACS and GNU utilities, and founding the GNU Project (1983) and Free Software Foundation (1985). He launched copyleft and the GNU GPL, advocating software ethics, privacy, and resistance to restrictive technologies. Stallman is recognized for his multilingualism, honors, and Boston-based life.

## Alfred North Whitehead: Legacy in Mathematics and Philosophy

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947), co-author of *Principia Mathematica*, contributed influential ideas to mathematics and philosophy, especially process philosophy, shaping theology, science, education, and ecology.

## Blaise Pascal: Mathematics, Science, and Theology

Blaise Pascal advanced probability theory, mathematics, fluid mechanics, invented the Pascaline calculator, and wrote religious essays emphasizing faith and intuition.

## Leonardo Torres Quevedo: Engineering Pioneer

Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) pioneered automation, electromechanical calculators, remote control, cableway engineering, and naval and aeronautical inventions. He led Spain's Laboratory of Automation, promoted Esperanto, and received major honors.

## Hedy Lamarr: Actress and Inventor

Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) gained fame as an actress and as co-inventor of radio frequency hopping, earning significant technical and cultural recognition.

## Alan Turing: Foundations of Computing

Alan Turing (1912–1954) founded computability theory, solved the halting problem, was a WWII codebreaker, and advanced AI and biology, earning widespread posthumous acclaim.

## Ada Lovelace and Charles Babbage: Originators of Computing

Ada Lovelace (1815–1852) is known as the first computer programmer; with Charles Babbage (1791–1871), she laid early foundations for computing with the Analytical Engine.

## Philip Rubin: Science, Speech Technology, and Public Policy

Philip Rubin (b. 1949) is a leader in speech technology, cognitive science, neuroscience, technology policy, and public service, contributing to multimodal and sine-wave speech research.

## Vannevar Bush: Shaping Science, Technology, and Policy

Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was a pioneering engineer, inventor, and science administrator. His leadership at Tufts, MIT, and Harvard drove technological advances, and during WWII, he organized science mobilization and managed key wartime projects (including radar and the atomic bomb). Bush conceptualized the "memex," inspiring modern computing and information science. His essay "As We May Think" influenced visionaries such as Douglas Engelbart and Ted Nelson. Bush vigorously advocated government support for basic research, culminating in the creation of the National Science Foundation in 1950. He received major honors such as the Edison Medal, Public Welfare Medal, National Medal of Science, and international awards including the British Order of the Empire and French Legion of Honor. The National Science Foundation's Vannevar Bush Award commemorates his legacy. His papers are held in leading archives, notably the Library of Congress, and he has been depicted in popular films like "The Beginning or the End" (1947) and "Oppenheimer" (2023). Bush is remembered as the "Grand Old Man of American science" for his lasting leadership and policy contributions.
