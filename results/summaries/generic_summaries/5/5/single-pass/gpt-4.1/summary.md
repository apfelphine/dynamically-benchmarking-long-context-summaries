## Philip Rubin

Philip Rubin is an American cognitive scientist recognized for his interdisciplinary work merging psychology, linguistics, engineering, and computational modeling to advance understanding of speech production and perception. Rubin contributed foundational research on speech technology from the early 1970s, including the Network Voice Protocol and pulse-code modulation for digitized speech. He developed pioneering tools like ASY (articulatory synthesis program) and SWS (sinewave synthesis system), which helped explore the biological bases of speech and its perceptual organization. His theoretical approach emphasizes dynamical systems, embodied cognition, and event-based models in speech, influencing current computational approaches.

Rubin has had key leadership and advocacy roles in U.S. science policy, including directing behavioral sciences at NSF, chairing major science boards, and serving in the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, where he led neuroscience and research policy initiatives. He is a vocal advocate for ethical research and has served on committees overseeing human subjects protections and research ethics, including critical assessments of security technologies. He has founded several influential scientific organizations and has contributed to public engagement and education, especially broadening participation in science.

Rubin has received numerous honors from major scientific societies for his research and policy leadership. He remains active in museum work, university governance, and advisory boards. His publications span speech synthesis, cognitive science, research ethics, and science policy.

## Ada Lovelace

Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), daughter of Lord Byron, is celebrated as a mathematician and writer, often called the "first computer programmer" due to her work describing the Analytical Engine designed by Charles Babbage. Lovelace’s notes on Babbage’s machine, including the first algorithm published for it, highlighted its capacity for general-purpose computation and symbolic manipulation—a century before modern computers. While there is debate about the extent of her technical contribution versus Babbage’s, Lovelace is widely acknowledged for her insight into the broader implications of programmable machines.

Her life was shaped by her mother’s ambitions, complex family relationships, and frail health. Privately educated in mathematics and science, she integrated poetic imagination with scientific reasoning, valuing intuition alongside analytical skill. Lovelace’s legacy has been celebrated and commemorated in numerous ways, with events, awards, educational institutions, and a programming language named in her honor. Her contributions continue to inspire women in STEM and feature prominently in popular culture, literature, and technology.

## Vannevar Bush

Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was a prominent American engineer, inventor, and science administrator whose work shaped 20th-century U.S. science policy and technological innovation. He invented numerous devices, including the differential analyzer (an early analog computer), played an instrumental role in founding Raytheon, and developed digital circuit theories. During WWII, Bush chaired the National Defense Research Committee and then the Office of Scientific Research and Development, overseeing military technological advances such as radar, the proximity fuze, and the Manhattan Project.

Bush advocated for organized government support of scientific research, leading to the creation of the National Science Foundation. His visionary essay "As We May Think" described the memex concept—a precursor to hypertext and the World Wide Web. Bush's influence expanded into education, scientific administration, and U.S. government policy, and he received numerous honors including the National Medal of Science. His legacy persists in computing, science policy, and information science.

## Hedy Lamarr

Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) was an Austrian-American actress and self-taught inventor. Renowned for her Hollywood career in the 1930s–1950s, Lamarr’s most lasting scientific contribution was co-inventing frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology during WWII alongside George Antheil, originally designed to protect radio-guided torpedoes from jamming. Although not adopted by the Navy at the time, her invention laid foundational principles for modern wireless communications, including WiFi and Bluetooth.

Besides her scientific pursuits, Lamarr had a tumultuous personal life and a successful film career, often cast as an exotic beauty. She struggled with restrictive studio roles and sought creative outlets through inventing. Lamarr received posthumous recognition for her technical achievements, including induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. Her life and work have been celebrated in awards, documentaries, stage productions, and commemorative events, marking her as a pioneering figure bridging cinema and technology.

## Charles Babbage

Charles Babbage (1791–1871) was a British mathematician, philosopher, inventor, and mechanical engineer who conceived the first mechanical computers, most notably the Difference Engine and Analytical Engine. His designs, though only partially built during his lifetime, demonstrated key concepts of programmable computing, including separate memory and control units, conditional operations, and input/output mechanisms. Babbage collaborated closely with Ada Lovelace, whose notes further elucidated the Analytical Engine's capabilities.

Babbage’s contributions extended to statistics, industrial organization, economics, and metrology. He advocated for scientific reform and operational research, influencing the British scientific community and government policy. He also worked on encryption, public transportation design, and other innovations. His legacy is celebrated in computing history, with numerous awards, institutions, and cultural references named in his honor.

## Blaise Pascal

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, writer, and philosopher. As a child prodigy, he contributed foundational work in projective geometry, probability theory (with de Fermat), and invented the Pascaline, an early mechanical calculator. In physics, Pascal made seminal advances in fluid dynamics and pressure, and established the SI unit for pressure (the pascal).

Pascal was deeply interested in philosophy and theology, writing influential works like the Pensées and the Provincial Letters, which blended skepticism, faith, and literary brilliance. He inaugurated mass public transport and developed principles influential in the fields of mathematics, physics, and philosophy. His legacy endures in mathematics, the sciences, theology, and popular culture.

## Alan Turing

Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, logician, cryptanalyst, and computer scientist. He formulated the concept of the Turing machine, addressing the limits of computation and proving the undecidability of the halting problem, foundational for theoretical computer science. During WWII, Turing led cryptanalysis efforts at Bletchley Park, primarily breaking Nazi Enigma and Lorenz codes, dramatically influencing the war’s outcome.

After the war, Turing contributed to the development of early computers and artificial intelligence (proposing the "Turing Test"), and mathematical biology (morphogenesis). Despite his pivotal scientific achievements, Turing’s career and life were cut short by prosecution for homosexuality, resulting in his death under controversial circumstances. He has received posthumous apologies, a royal pardon, and recognition as one of the most influential figures in modern computing and science.

## Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman (b. 1953) is an American programmer, activist, and founder of the Free Software Movement and the GNU Project. Stallman developed influential software tools (GNU Emacs, GCC, GDB), established the Free Software Foundation, and popularized ethical concepts like copyleft and free software licensing, especially the GNU General Public License (GPL). His work enabled the widespread adoption of open-source operating systems, notably GNU/Linux.

Stallman is known for his advocacy of software freedom, opposition to proprietary software, and efforts to reduce copyright restrictions. He emphasizes terminology clarity and resists the mainstream adoption of open source over free software principles. Despite controversy surrounding personal conduct, Stallman remains a driving force in software ethics and digital rights, receiving numerous awards for his contributions.

## Leonardo Torres Quevedo

Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a Spanish engineer, inventor, and mathematician whose work bridged mechanical, electrical, and computing technologies. He invented pioneering cableways, including the Niagara Spanish Aerocar, and advanced analog computing machines for solving mathematical equations. Torres developed remote-control technology (the Telekino), contributed innovative airship designs (Astra-Torres dirigibles), and built one of the earliest chess-playing automata (El Ajedrecista), a milestone in the history of automatics and robotics.

Torres advocated for symbolic notation in engineering and founded the Laboratory of Automation in Spain, promoting scientific research and instrumentation. He was also active in the Spanish Esperanto movement and scientific terminology standardization. Torres received numerous international honors, and his legacy in computation, control systems, and engineering is commemorated in awards, museums, and technical institutions.

## Alfred North Whitehead

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was an English mathematician, philosopher, and educator. Collaborating with Bertrand Russell, he co-authored Principia Mathematica, a seminal work in mathematical logic. Whitehead wrote foundational texts on universal algebra and mathematical philosophy, also developing the concept of extensive abstraction influential in computer science and ontology.

In philosophy, Whitehead pioneered process philosophy, proposing a universe understood as interconnected events and processes rather than inert matter. His metaphysics influenced theology (process theology), science, education, and environmental ethics. Whitehead’s work shaped modern thinking on cosmology, perception, educational theory, and the relationship between science and values. Though challenging and less mainstream, his thought remains influential in select academic, philosophical, and theological circles worldwide.
