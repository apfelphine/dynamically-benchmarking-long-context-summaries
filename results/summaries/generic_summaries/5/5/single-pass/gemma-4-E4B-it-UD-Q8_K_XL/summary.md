## Philip Rubin

**Research and Career**
Philip Rubin's research integrates computational, engineering, linguistic, physiological, and psychological methods to study embodied cognition, focusing especially on the biological bases of speech and language. His key areas include articulatory synthesis, speech perception, signal processing, and modeling complex temporal events. He has held leadership roles in science administration, policy, and advocacy.

**Speech Technology and Theory**
Starting in the early 1970s, Rubin contributed to foundational speech technology issues, including work on ARPANET Voice Protocol and digitized speech extensions. He designed significant software systems such as ASY (Haskins articulatory synthesis program) and SWS (Haskins sinewave synthesis program). Theoretically, his work emphasizes a dynamical systems/action theory perspective on speech, focusing on the spatiotemporal coordination of global system aspects rather than momentary signal cues. His gestural computational system, CASY, allows replicating MRI images of speakers.

**Policy and Administration**
Rubin served as the Director of the Division of Behavioral and Cognitive Sciences (BCS) at the National Science Foundation (NSF) from 2000 to 2003, and later served as a senior advisor at the NSF. He held significant science policy roles, including Chair of the National Academies Board on Behavioral, Cognitive, and Sensory Sciences (2006–2011). He also served in the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) from 2012 to 2015, where he led the White House's neuroscience initiative.

**Ethical Issues**
Rubin has extensive involvement in research ethics, serving as the NSF ex officio representative to NHRPAC during the Clinton and Bush administrations. Post-NSF, he continued advising on human subjects issues, lecturing, and co-authoring reports, while also voicing concerns over the ethical oversight of technologies used by intelligence and military communities.

## Ada Lovelace

**Biography and Early Life**
Born in 1816, Ada Lovelace was the daughter of Lord Byron. Despite early illnesses, she developed profound mathematical and technological skills. Her mother, Lady Byron, heavily managed her life, though Lovelace’s intellectual drive persisted.

**Education and Work**
Her mathematical interests were fostered by private tutors, with Augustus De Morgan assisting her studies. She became closely associated with Charles Babbage, whose Analytical Engine fascinated her. In her 1842–43 translation of Menabrea’s paper, Lovelace augmented the text with seven extensive notes (A-G). These notes, particularly Note G, detail a method for calculating Bernoulli numbers and are often cited as the world's first published computer program.

**Contributions and Legacy**
Lovelace recognized that the Analytical Engine’s potential extended beyond mere number crunching, realizing it could manipulate symbols—a concept foundational to modern computing. While her status as the "first computer programmer" is debated, her insight into the machine's general-purpose symbolic manipulation capability was crucial. Her legacy is widely commemorated across technology, science, and culture.

## Charles Babbage

**Early Life and Education**
Born around 1791, Charles Babbage was educated in England, eventually attending Trinity College, Cambridge. He excelled academically but later pursued a career path that was inconsistent.

**Engineering and Mechanical Devices**
Babbage was instrumental in founding the Royal Astronomical Society in 1820, motivated by the need to reduce astronomical calculation errors through mechanization. He conceived of the Difference Engine (1822), designed to compute polynomial functions using the method of finite differences. Though unfinished, prototypes remain in museums.

**The Analytical Engine**
His major contribution was the Analytical Engine, a concept for a general-purpose, programmable machine. This design separated data and program memory, used punched cards for instruction, and featured conditional jumps. His work on mechanical calculation, alongside his collaboration with Ada Lovelace, established him as a computer pioneer.

**Other Contributions**
Babbage also wrote *On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures* (1832), an early work of operational research that introduced the "Babbage principle" regarding division of labor. Furthermore, he made significant contributions to cryptography, solving Vigenère ciphers, and explored concepts in natural theology and metrology.

## Alan Turing

**Early Life and Education**
Alan Turing was born in 1912 in London. His early aptitude for science and mathematics was recognized from a young age. He pursued higher education at King's College, Cambridge, earning first-class honours in mathematics in 1934.

**Theoretical Contributions to Computing**
Between 1935 and 1936, Turing tackled the decidability of mathematical problems. His paper, "On Computable Numbers," reformulated Gödel's results and introduced the concept of the "Turing machine," a theoretical model capable of performing any conceivable algorithmic computation. This paper is considered one of the most influential in history.

**Wartime Cryptanalysis and Post-War Work**
During WWII, Turing was a leading cryptanalyst at Bletchley Park, where he worked on breaking German Enigma ciphers. He designed the *bombe*, an electromechanical device for this purpose, and developed statistical techniques like Banburismus. Post-war, he designed the ACE (Automatic Computing Engine), one of the first detailed designs of a stored-program computer. He also pioneered the Turing test to define machine intelligence.

**Later Research and Personal Life**
Turing later shifted focus to mathematical biology, publishing "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" (1952), which proposed reaction-diffusion systems could account for biological patterns. His personal life was marked by his relationship with Christopher Morcom and his subsequent conviction in 1952 for "gross indecency," which led to chemical castration. He died in 1954.

## Richard Stallman

**Early Life and Programming Work**
Stallman began showing interest in computers in his youth. At Harvard and later MIT, he worked in the hacker community, developing software for systems like TECO and Emacs. He became an ardent critic of restricted computer access in research labs.

**The GNU Project and Free Software**
The shift towards proprietary software in the late 1970s and early 1980s fueled his opposition to closed systems. In 1983, he launched the GNU project to create a free operating system compatible with Unix. He founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF) and popularized the concept of *copyleft*, codified in the GNU General Public License (GPL).

**Activism and Philosophy**
Stallman views software freedom as a moral imperative, not just a pragmatic one, and insists on the correct terminology ("free software" vs. "open source"). He has campaigned against digital rights management (DRM) and corporate surveillance. His advocacy led to major international endorsements, such as the adoption of free software in schools in Kerala, India.

## Leonardo Torres Quevedo

**Engineering and Automatics**
Torres Quevedo was a Spanish engineer known for his diverse technical inventions. His early work focused on cableways, including a pioneering aerial funicular in Switzerland, and later, the Niagara Spanish Aerocar. He was also a pioneer in remote control technology with the **Telekino**, successfully demonstrating unmanned control of vehicles in 1903.

**Calculating Machines and Logic**
He made significant contributions to calculating machines. He presented a paper on algebraic machines in 1895 and developed a sophisticated analogue calculating machine. His work demonstrated the mechanical calculation of arbitrary polynomial roots, featuring a unique "endless spindle" mechanism for logarithmic calculations.

**Computing and Automation Concepts**
Torres laid theoretical groundwork for modern computing. In 1915, he authored "Essays on Automatics," distinguishing between simple and thoughtful automata. He designed a theoretical machine capable of automatic calculation using a read-only program and introduced the concept of floating-point arithmetic. Furthermore, he created *El Ajedrecista* (The Chess Player) around 1910, an electromechanical automaton considered one of the first computer games.

## Blaise Pascal

**Mathematical and Scientific Breakthroughs**
Pascal was a brilliant mathematician and physicist. He produced his first major work, *Essai pour les coniques* (1639), which established Pascal's theorem regarding conics. In 1654, his correspondence with Pierre de Fermat gave birth to the mathematical theory of probability, introducing the notion of expected value. His *Traité du triangle arithmétique* (1665) introduced Pascal's triangle and provided an early statement of the principle of mathematical induction.

**Physics and Vacuum Experiments**
In physics, Pascal made vital contributions to fluid mechanics and hydrostatics, giving the SI unit of pressure his name. More critically, through experiments with Torricelli's barometer, he rigorously investigated the concept of a vacuum in 1647, proving that air pressure decreases with altitude, a finding confirmed by an experiment on Puy de Dôme.

**Philosophy and Literature**
As a philosopher, he opposed both Descartes' rationalism and empiricism, favoring fideism, and wrote on the necessity of intuition in mathematics. His theological work, the *Pensées*, is a masterpiece of French prose, using philosophical debate to argue for the necessity of faith. He also invented one of the first land-based public transportation systems, the *carrosses à cinq sols*.

## Alfred North Whitehead

**Philosophy and Metaphysics**
Whitehead is renowned for developing "process philosophy," which fundamentally rejects scientific materialism. He posits that reality is composed of discrete, interrelated "occasions of experience" that are fundamentally dynamic ("becoming") rather than static "being." He introduced the concept of "prehension," a form of perception that applies to all entities, conscious or unconscious.

**Theology and Ethics**
His theology offers a unique vision of God as a "dipolar" entity: the primordial nature (unlimited potentiality) and the consequent nature (God's reception of the world's activity). He argues that religion is fundamentally an individual act that expands into a universal community, making the world a community.

**Influence on Science and Education**
Whitehead’s philosophy has profound influence on ecological civilization, sustainability, and educational theory. He advocated against "inert ideas" in education, arguing for transdisciplinary learning rooted in general principles. His work has spurred interest in process theology and provided metaphysical frameworks for fields ranging from ecology to business administration.

## Vannevar Bush

**Early Career and Inventions**
Born in 1890, Bush graduated from Tufts College with a dual bachelor's and master's degree in 1913, during which he patented a "profile tracer" mapping device. His early engineering work at General Electric spanned various projects.

**Research Leadership and Technological Development**
He worked with the National Research Council starting in 1917, developing submarine detection methods. Later, he invented the differential analyzer, an analog computer that mechanically simulated and plotted differential equations. He also co-founded companies like Raytheon, which utilized the S-tube voltage regulator.

**Science Policy and Vision**
Bush became a key leader in science administration, serving as president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington and later chairing the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA, predecessor to NASA). During WWII, he was central to establishing the National Defense Research Committee (NDRC) and later directing the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD). He was instrumental in convincing the government to initiate the Manhattan Project.

**Visionary Concepts**
In his 1945 essay, *As We May Think*, Bush introduced the concept of the **Memex**—a microfilm-based device intended to augment memory by linking information associatively—a concept widely regarded as a precursor to the World Wide Web. He strongly advocated for federal patronage of basic scientific research.
