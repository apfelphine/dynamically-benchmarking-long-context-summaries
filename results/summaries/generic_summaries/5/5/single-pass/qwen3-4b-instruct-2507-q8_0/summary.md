### Philip Rubin  
Philip Rubin is a prominent figure in cognitive science and speech technology, known for his interdisciplinary research combining computational, engineering, linguistic, physiological, and psychological approaches. He earned a BA in psychology and linguistics from Brandeis University and a PhD in experimental psychology from the University of Connecticut under Michael Turvey, Ignatius Mattingly, Philip Lieberman, and Alvin Liberman.  

His work spans articulatory synthesis, speech perception, and signal processing, with key contributions including the development of ASY and SWS—computational models for speech production and sinewave synthesis. He designed the HADES signal processing system and the SPIEL programming language (a precursor to MATLAB). Rubin also led the NIH/NICHD-funded P-01 program on the nature and acquisition of the speech code and reading.  

He played a significant role in science policy and advocacy, serving as Director of the NSF’s Division of Behavioral and Cognitive Sciences, Chair of the National Academies Board on Behavioral, Cognitive, and Sensory Sciences, and President of the Federation of Associations in Behavioral and Brain Sciences (FABBS). He is also known for his ethical stances, particularly in opposing the use of voice stress technologies and criticizing the TSA’s SPOT program.  

Rubin has been recognized with numerous honors, including Fellowships from the Acoustical Society of America, American Association for the Advancement of Science, and the American Psychological Association, and the 2015 Distinguished Service Award from COSSA.  

### Ada Lovelace  
Ada Lovelace (1815–1852) was a pioneering mathematician and writer, best known for her work on Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine. She is often credited as the world’s first computer programmer for her detailed algorithm in Note G, which computed Bernoulli numbers. Her work emphasized the machine’s potential to manipulate symbols, not just numbers, anticipating modern computing.  

Lovelace’s contributions include a visionary essay on the Analytical Engine’s capabilities, which highlighted the importance of spatiotemporal coordination in computation. She also explored audiovisual speech and multimodality, advancing the understanding of human perception.  

Her legacy is celebrated in various forms, including the programming language Ada, the Ada Lovelace Day, and the Ada Lovelace Award. She is honored in institutions and public spaces worldwide, and her work has been widely recognized in science, technology, and education.  

### Vannevar Bush  
Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was a key figure in the development of U.S. science and technology policy. A physicist and engineer, he served as president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington and was instrumental in founding the National Science Foundation (NSF) after World War II.  

His vision for scientific advancement was articulated in "As We May Think," which introduced the concept of the memex—a machine for storing and retrieving information through associative links, a precursor to the World Wide Web. Bush advocated for basic research as the foundation of technological progress and promoted interdisciplinary collaboration.  

He played a pivotal role in the U.S. war effort, leading the National Defense Research Committee (NDRC) and the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD), where he oversaw the development of radar, the proximity fuze, and the Manhattan Project. His leadership helped establish the U.S. as a leader in scientific innovation.  

Bush received numerous honors, including the National Medal of Science and the AIEE Edison Medal. He is remembered as a visionary who laid the foundation for modern science policy and research funding.  

### Hedy Lamarr  
Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) was an Austrian-born actress and inventor. She gained fame for her roles in classic Hollywood films, including *Samson and Delilah* and *Ecstasy*, where she was celebrated for her beauty and performances.  

In the 1940s, she co-invented frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology with composer George Antheil, a method that prevents signal jamming and is foundational to modern wireless communication. Though initially rejected by the Navy, her invention laid the groundwork for secure wireless systems used in GPS, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth.  

Lamarr’s contributions to technology were recognized posthumously, including induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame and the naming of an asteroid after her. She is also celebrated in popular culture, including in films and music.  

### Charles Babbage  
Charles Babbage (1800–1871) was a British mathematician and inventor, regarded as a father of the computer. He designed the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine, the first mechanical computers capable of performing complex calculations and executing programs.  

The Analytical Engine introduced key concepts such as conditional branching, loops, and programmable instruction sets, forming the basis of modern computing. His work on the engine was later expanded by Ada Lovelace, who developed an algorithm for it.  

Babbage also contributed to mathematics, probability theory, and cryptography, and was a pioneer in the field of mechanical computation. His legacy is honored through institutions like the Charles Babbage Institute and the Babbage programming language.  

### Alan Turing  
Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician and computer scientist who made foundational contributions to computing and artificial intelligence. He introduced the concept of the Turing machine, a theoretical device capable of computing any algorithmic problem, and proposed the Turing test as a standard for machine intelligence.  

During World War II, Turing worked at Bletchley Park, where he played a key role in breaking German Enigma and Lorenz codes, shortening the war by years. His work in cryptography and computing laid the foundation for modern computer science.  

After the war, he worked on the design of the ACE computer and developed early ideas in artificial intelligence. Tragically, he was prosecuted for homosexuality in 1952 and died by suicide in 1954. In 2009, the UK government formally apologized for his treatment.  

Turing is celebrated as a pioneer in computer science and AI, with honors including the Turing Award and the naming of the Turing machine and the Pascal microarchitecture in his honor.  

### Richard Stallman  
Richard Stallman (1953–present) is a computer scientist and advocate for free software. He founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF) and the GNU Project, which developed free and open-source software tools like the GNU Compiler Collection (GCC) and GNU Emacs.  

Stallman championed software freedom, opposing proprietary software and advocating for copyleft licensing to ensure users could modify and redistribute software. He promoted open-source development and criticized software patents and digital rights management (DRM).  

He has been a vocal critic of corporate control over technology and has supported open access to knowledge, including the creation of GNUPedia, a precursor to Wikipedia. His work has influenced the open-source movement and modern software development.  

### Leonardo Torres Quevedo  
Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a Spanish engineer and inventor known for pioneering work in mechanical and electrical systems. He developed the first aerial cable cars, including the Niagara Spanish Aerocar, and created early analog computing machines capable of solving complex equations.  

He also invented the Telekino, a radio-controlled remote system, and designed the first chess-playing automaton, El Ajedrecista. His work in automation, control systems, and mechanical computation anticipated modern computing and robotics.  

Quevedo is recognized for his contributions to engineering, with honors including the Echegaray Medal and numerous international awards. His legacy endures in institutions, educational programs, and public commemorations, including a Google Doodle in 2012.  

### Alfred North Whitehead  
Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician and philosopher, best known for his work in mathematical logic and metaphysics. He co-authored *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell and developed a process philosophy that viewed reality as composed of interconnected events rather than static matter.  

His philosophy emphasized creativity, experience, and relationality, influencing fields such as theology, ecology, education, and science. He advocated for a holistic, value-based education system and critiqued materialism in science.  

Whitehead’s ideas have had a lasting impact on process theology, environmental ethics, and modern science. His work is studied in philosophy, theology, and education, and he remains a key figure in 20th-century intellectual thought.
