# Hedy Lamarr
Lamarr, born Hedwig Eva Maria Kiesler in 1914, began her career in European cinema before achieving international fame in 1933 with the controversial film *Ecstasy*. Her subsequent marriage to arms merchant Friedrich Mandl, which she claimed stifled her acting career, led her to flee to Paris and eventually to Hollywood in 1937. In the US, she was rebranded as Hedy Lamarr and became known for her roles as a glamorous seductress, though she often felt bored due to limited acting challenges.

Beyond acting, Lamarr was interested in science. During her marriage, she was exposed to military technology discussions, which nurtured her interest in applied science. Collaborating with composer George Antheil, she co-invented a frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology in 1942, which was patented, although the Navy initially rejected its application for torpedo guidance. After her film career began a decline, she remained active in Hollywood before retiring. She passed away in 2000.

# Blaise Pascal
Blaise Pascal was a brilliant French mathematician, physicist, and philosopher. Early in his career, he produced *Essai pour les coniques* (Essay on Conics), and later, in 1642, invented the Pascaline, a mechanical calculator for addition and subtraction. His collaboration with Pierre de Fermat led to the development of the mathematical theory of probability.

In physics, Pascal advanced understanding of fluid mechanics, inventing the hydraulic press, and in 1647, he conducted critical experiments demonstrating the existence of a vacuum above a mercury column in a barometer, challenging the prevailing plenum theory. His later philosophical and religious work, notably the *Pensées*, deeply influenced the concept of human existence and faith.

# Ada Lovelace
Ada Lovelace, the daughter of Lord Byron, possessed extraordinary mathematical aptitude, nurtured by her mother's focus on scientific rigor. She became closely acquainted with Charles Babbage, and in the 1840s, she translated Luigi Menabrea's paper on Babbage's Analytical Engine.

Her most significant contribution was adding extensive notes (Notes A-G) to the translation, which detailed an algorithm for calculating Bernoulli numbers. This work is recognized as the world's first published computer program, as she envisioned the machine's potential far beyond mere calculation—realizing it could manipulate symbols, not just quantities. Lovelace died in 1852, but her legacy has been honored through modern technology, including the Ada programming language and numerous awards.

# Richard Stallman
Richard Stallman is a computer scientist and the founder of the GNU Project. Driven by his belief in user freedom, he became an ardent critic of proprietary software and restrictive licenses, which he views as a "crime against humanity." In the late 1970s, the shift toward closed-source software catalyzed his activism.

In 1983, he launched the GNU project to create a free operating system compatible with Unix. He popularized the concept of copyleft via the GNU General Public License (GPL). Stallman advocates for a "reduced copyright" and rejects the term "open-source," asserting that "free software is a political movement." After periods of controversy regarding his views on sexual matters, he eventually returned to the FSF board in 2021.

# Philip Rubin
Philip Rubin is a researcher whose work integrates computational, engineering, linguistic, physiological, and psychological approaches to study embodied cognition, particularly the biological basis of speech and language. He was instrumental in foundational speech technology starting in the 1970s.

Rubin designed key computational models, such as ASY (Haskins articulatory synthesis program) and SWS (sinewave synthesis program). Theoretically, he developed a dynamical systems/action theory perspective on speech, emphasizing spatiotemporal coordination rather than punctual signal features. He also played a significant role in science policy, advising the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP).

# Alfred North Whitehead
Alfred North Whitehead was a foundational philosopher and mathematician whose work is characterized by its radical metaphysical scope. He co-authored *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a monumental work of mathematical logic intended to prove all mathematical truths from a set of axioms.

Philosophically, he developed a "process philosophy," rejecting static materialist concepts. He posited that reality is composed of interrelated, dynamic "occasions of experience," leading to his concept of "prehension"—a perception that incorporates the perceived entity. His theology suggested God is a "dipolar" entity: primordial (unlimited potential) and consequent (receiving the world's activity). His ideas have heavily influenced contemporary ecology and education theory.

# Vannevar Bush
Vannevar Bush was an engineer, innovator, and influential scientific administrator, best known for his visionary essay, "As We May Think," published in *The Atlantic Monthly* in 1945. This essay introduced the concept of the *memex*, an envisioned memory augmentation device.

Bush applied his engineering genius across various fields, developing a profile tracer patent in his early career. During World War II, he played a crucial role in organizing scientific research via the National Defense Research Committee (NDRC) and later directed the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD). He strongly advocated for sustained government funding for basic scientific research, a principle that later guided the formation of the National Science Foundation (NSF).

# Leonardo Torres Quevedo
Leonardo Torres Quevedo was a prodigious Spanish engineer whose work spanned civil engineering, analogue computation, and remote control. He was a pioneer in mechanical and electrical automation, designing several groundbreaking devices.

In civil engineering, he designed various cableways, including a notable aerial funicular that remains in operation. In computation, he created early analogue mechanical calculating machines, and in 1910, he designed *El Ajedrecista*, a chess-playing automaton considered a precursor to modern video games. He also formalized a language for describing mechanical drawings and pioneered telecontrol with his *Telekino*.

# Charles Babbage
Charles Babbage was a pioneering mathematician and inventor, best known for his conceptual designs for mechanical computers. He was inspired by the need to eliminate human error in calculating complex mathematical and astronomical tables.

He designed two key machines: the Difference Engine, which automatically calculated polynomial functions, and the Analytical Engine. The Analytical Engine was revolutionary because it was intended to be general-purpose and programmed using punched cards, featuring concepts like conditional branching and looping. Ada Lovelace is credited with developing an algorithm for this engine. Babbage also made influential early work in operational research in *On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures*.

# Alan Turing
Alan Turing was a pivotal mathematician and computer scientist whose work fundamentally shaped modern computer science and artificial intelligence. He made profound contributions to mathematical logic and cryptanalysis.

His most famous achievement was proving the unsolvability of the decision problem in 1936, using the concept of the Turing machine, which defined what is computable. During WWII, he was a leading cryptanalyst at Bletchley Park, developing methods like Banburismus and Turingery to break German Enigma and Lorenz ciphers, which is estimated to have shortened the war. Later, he pioneered mathematical biology with his "Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis," proposing reaction-diffusion systems to model natural patterns.

# Julian Rubin
Philip Rubin is a leading expert in the intersection of computation, physiology, and language. His research centers on embodied cognition, specifically exploring the biological foundations of speech and language production.

Rubin designed numerous computational models for speech synthesis, including ASY (articulatory synthesis) and SWS (sinewave synthesis). Theoretically, he introduced a dynamical systems perspective, focusing on the spatiotemporal coordination of global aspects of speech. He has also been deeply involved in science policy, serving in leadership roles within the National Science Foundation (NSF) and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP).

# Ernest Whitehead
Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy, known as process philosophy, radically departs from static materialism by viewing reality as a continuum of interrelated, dynamic events. He argued that "all things flow," rejecting the idea of unchanging essences.

His concept of "prehension" describes perception as an incorporation of the perceived into the self. In theology, he conceived of God as a "dipolar" entity—eternal potential (primordial nature) and the recipient of the world's activity (consequent nature). His ideas have proven highly influential across ecology, education, and theology.

# Robert Stallman
Richard Stallman is the founder of the Free Software Foundation and a staunch advocate for free software. He champions software freedom as a political movement, contrasting it with the development model of "open source."

He vehemently opposes proprietary software and digital rights management (DRM), viewing restrictions on code sharing as unethical. Stallman insists that users must have the freedom to modify and redistribute software, and he maintains the distinction between "free software" and "proprietary software," advocating for the use of the term GNU/Linux.
