**Leonardo Torres Quevedo** pioneered mechanical automation, remote control, and early computing, laying groundwork for modern robotics and programmable machines. His visionary work in cableways, airships, and electromechanical systems—including *El Ajedrecista*, an electromechanical chess-playing automaton—predated modern computing by decades. **Alan Turing** established the theoretical foundations of computing with the Turing machine and the Church–Turing thesis, and his wartime codebreaking efforts were instrumental in shortening World War II. His later work in artificial intelligence and mathematical biology remains profoundly influential. **Alfred North Whitehead** revolutionized metaphysics with process philosophy, emphasizing change, creativity, and relationality in reality, influencing theology, education, and ecology. His ideas continue to inspire interdisciplinary approaches to sustainability and human existence. **Charles Babbage**, known as the "father of the computer," designed mechanical computing devices that inspired later innovators like Torres Quevedo and Lovelace. His work on the Analytical Engine introduced concepts of programmability and conditional logic, foundational to modern computing. **Richard Stallman** champions software freedom and open-source development, promoting ethical and accessible technology through the GNU Project and Free Software Foundation. **Vannevar Bush** shaped U.S. scientific policy during World War II, enabling major technological advances through strategic coordination and institutional innovation. **Blaise Pascal** made foundational contributions in mathematics, physics, and philosophy, including probability theory and the Pascaline calculator, while also exploring the limits of reason and faith. **Hedy Lamarr**, a Hollywood star and inventor, co-developed frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology, a cornerstone of modern wireless communication, and is celebrated in popular culture through films, documentaries, and media tributes. **Ada Lovelace**, the world’s first computer programmer, translated and expanded on Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine, adding seven extensive notes—now recognized as the first algorithm designed for a machine—making her a visionary in computing and a pioneer in envisioning machines capable of creative tasks beyond mere calculation. Despite personal challenges, including gambling debts and a religious transformation, she remained a visionary in science and technology, dying at 36 from cervical cancer in 1852. Her legacy endures as a foundational figure in the history of computing and artificial intelligence. **Note G**, in particular, is widely regarded as the first published computer program, describing an algorithm for calculating Bernoulli numbers and highlighting her insight into symbolic manipulation and general-purpose computation. Her recognition of the distinction between mechanical design and logical structure laid foundational ideas for computer science, and her contributions have been honored through the naming of the programming language **Ada** and numerous awards and institutions dedicated to women in technology.

In recognition of her pioneering role, **Ada Lovelace Day** is celebrated annually on the second Tuesday of October to promote women in STEM and inspire girls and women through role models. Institutions such as the **Ada Lovelace Building** at the University of Bristol, the **Ada Lovelace Institute**, and **Nvidia’s Ada Lovelace GPU architecture** bear her name. A Google Doodle, BBC biography, and a statue in London further honor her legacy. In 2018, the U.S. Senate designated October 9 as **National Ada Lovelace Day**, and the bicentenary in 2015 featured lectures, symposia, and exhibitions. Her influence extends into media, including plays, films, and games, and into educational and technological initiatives such as scholarships, programming languages, and AI projects—continuing to inspire diversity and recognition in science and technology. Additionally, she is celebrated in popular culture through blue and green plaques in London and Ealing, a school in Greenford, and buildings at Nottingham Trent University and other institutions in Nottinghamshire. Her 1843 "Notes" on Babbage’s Analytical Engine, published in *Scientific Memoirs*, are historically significant, with six known copies—three at Harvard, one at Oklahoma, one at the U.S. Air Force Academy, and one sold for £95,000 in 2018—highlighting their enduring value. A letter by her was forfeited to New York State in 2016 due to unpaid taxes by Martin Shkreli.
