**Leonardo Torres Quevedo** was a Spanish engineer and inventor known for pioneering work in cableways, analogue computing, and remote control. He developed the first aerial cable car in Spain (1885) and later the successful Mount Ulia cableway (1907) and the Niagara Spanish Aerocar (1914–1916), a cable ferry that remains in operation. His work in analogue calculating machines included a multi-wire system and a machine capable of solving complex algebraic equations. He also designed an early dirigible, proposed a docking station for airships, and invented the *Telekino*, a radio-controlled system used to remotely operate boats and vehicles. He introduced a formal language for mechanical drawings and was a key figure in establishing the Laboratory of Automation in Spain. He is also known for creating *El Ajedrecista*, an electromechanical chess-playing automaton, considered one of the first computer games. His legacy includes numerous patents, academic honors, and recognition in engineering and science, with the Leonardo Torres Quevedo National Research Award established in his honor.

**Alan Turing** was a British mathematician and computer scientist whose work laid the foundation for modern computing. He developed the concept of the *Turing machine*, a theoretical model capable of performing any computable task, and introduced the *Turing test* to evaluate machine intelligence. During World War II, he played a pivotal role in breaking German ciphers at Bletchley Park, significantly shortening the war. He also contributed to early computing with the design of the *ACE* (Automatic Computing Engine) and explored the application of computing to chess. Turing faced persecution for his homosexuality, was convicted in 1952, and died in 1954 under circumstances widely believed to be suicide. In 2009, the UK government formally apologized for his treatment, and he was posthumously pardoned in 2012. His legacy endures in computer science, artificial intelligence, and cryptography.

**Alfred North Whitehead** was a British mathematician, philosopher, and educator whose work bridged mathematics, logic, and philosophy. He co-authored *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a foundational work in mathematical logic. His philosophical contributions, particularly in *Process and Reality*, introduced a "process philosophy" emphasizing dynamic, interconnected events as the basis of reality, rather than static matter. He advocated for a holistic view of education, emphasizing imagination, values, and experiential learning, and influenced educational reform. Whitehead's ideas have had lasting impacts in philosophy, theology, ecology, and education, particularly in the development of process theology and the understanding of value in nature.

**Charles Babbage** was a British mathematician and inventor who is credited as a pioneer of mechanical computing. He designed the *Difference Engine*, a machine to automate mathematical table calculations, and the more advanced *Analytical Engine*, which introduced concepts foundational to modern computers, including memory, programming via punched cards, and conditional branching. Though never fully built during his lifetime, the Analytical Engine is considered a precursor to modern computers. Babbage also made contributions to the fields of statistics, cryptography, and metrology. His work influenced later developments in computing, and he is recognized for his visionary ideas and early efforts in automating computation.

**Richard Stallman** is an American computer scientist and software freedom advocate, best known for founding the GNU Project and the Free Software Foundation (FSF). He promoted the idea of free software, emphasizing user freedom to use, modify, and distribute software. He developed the GNU operating system, including key tools like GNU Emacs, GCC, and GDB, and introduced the concept of *copyleft*. Stallman has been a vocal critic of proprietary software, digital rights management (DRM), and software patents. He advocated for reduced copyright and supported open-source development. His work has profoundly influenced the open-source movement, and he has received numerous awards, including the Turing Award. He has also been involved in science policy, ethics, and public education, promoting free and open access to knowledge.

**Vannevar Bush** was an American engineer and science administrator who played a crucial role in shaping U.S. scientific policy during and after World War II. As a key figure in the creation of the National Defense Research Committee (NDRC) and the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD), he helped coordinate scientific efforts to support the war effort. He was instrumental in advancing the development of radar, proximity fuzes, and the atomic bomb. His concept of the *memex*, a device for storing and retrieving information through associative links, anticipated the development of the World Wide Web. After the war, he advocated for sustained government support of basic scientific research, leading to the creation of the National Science Foundation (NSF). His influence on science policy and technological innovation remains significant.

**Blaise Pascal** was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, and philosopher. He made significant contributions to probability theory, including the *Pascal's wager* and the *Pascal's triangle*. He invented the *Pascaline*, one of the first mechanical calculators, and conducted pioneering work in fluid mechanics, including the *Pascal's barrel experiment* and the theory of atmospheric pressure. A devout Catholic, he experienced a religious conversion and wrote extensively on theology, most notably in *The Provincial Letters* and *Pensées*. His work on the nature of God and human free will remains influential in philosophy. He is also recognized as a pioneer in the field of public transportation, having introduced one of the first public transport services in Europe.

**Philip Rubin** is an American psychologist and computer scientist known for his research in speech production, perception, and cognitive science. He developed computational models of speech, including the *ASY* and *SWS* systems for articulatory synthesis and sinewave synthesis. His work on dynamic systems and event-based models of speech production has advanced the understanding of how speech is generated and perceived. He has held leadership roles in science policy, including serving as Director of the NSF's Division of Behavioral and Cognitive Sciences and as Assistant Director for Social, Behavioral, and Economic Sciences at the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy. He is a strong advocate for ethical considerations in science and technology, particularly in relation to human subjects and national security. His contributions span both research and policy, influencing science education, technology ethics, and public engagement.

**Hedy Lamarr** was an Austrian-American actress and inventor. She gained fame through her Hollywood films, particularly *Ecstasy* (1933), and was known for her glamorous image. During her marriage to arms dealer Fritz Mandl, she became exposed to military technology and co-invented a frequency-hopping spread spectrum system with composer George Antheil, which laid the groundwork for modern wireless communication. Though initially rejected by the U.S. Navy, her invention was later recognized as a foundational contribution to secure wireless technology. She became a naturalized U.S. citizen and was posthumously inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2014. Her legacy is celebrated in film, music, and science, with numerous tributes, including a Google Doodle and an asteroid named after her.

**Ada Lovelace** was a British mathematician and writer, daughter of Lord Byron. She is recognized as the world’s first computer programmer for her detailed algorithm for Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine, which described a method for calculating Bernoulli numbers. Her insights into the potential of computing machines to manipulate symbols beyond numbers anticipated modern computing. She worked closely with Babbage and contributed to the understanding of the machine’s capabilities. Her legacy is honored through the programming language *Ada*, the *Ada Lovelace Day*, and numerous academic and cultural tributes. She is celebrated as a pioneer in computer science and a role model for women in STEM.
