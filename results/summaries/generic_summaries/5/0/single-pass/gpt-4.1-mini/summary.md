# Alfred North Whitehead
Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was an English mathematician, logician, and philosopher known for his foundational work in mathematical logic and process philosophy. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, he co-authored *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a landmark in 20th-century mathematical logic. Later in life, Whitehead transitioned to philosophy, developing a metaphysics centered on an event-based "process" ontology, opposing Cartesian dualism and emphasizing change, interrelation, and creativity in reality. He advanced “prehension” as a generalized perception applicable beyond conscious beings and proposed a dipolar conception of God integrating change and permanence. Whitehead authored influential works on education, advocating against rote learning ("inert ideas") and promoting imagination and transdisciplinarity. His process philosophy notably influenced theology, science, ecology, education, and business thought, although his writings remain complex and have limited mainstream philosophical influence. The Whitehead Research Project continues critical editions of his works.

# Leonardo Torres Quevedo
Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a Spanish civil engineer and inventor who pioneered innovations in cableways, analogue calculating machines, aerostatics, and early automation. Highlights include the development of multi-wire suspended cable cars capable of transporting people and the Niagara Spanish Aerocar, still in operation today. He created advanced electromechanical algebraic calculators with novel mechanisms for handling complex functions and roots of equations. Torres also contributed to dirigible airship design and invented the Telekino, an early radio-controlled unmanned vehicle demonstrated in 1906. His chess automaton *El Ajedrecista* was the first to play a complete chess endgame autonomously. Torres engaged in scientific administration, contributed to formal mechanical language development, and advocated for Esperanto. Despite challenges, his legacy is preserved in numerous honors, institutes, and commemorations in Spain and beyond.

# Richard Stallman
Richard Stallman (b. 1953) is an American software freedom activist and programmer, founder of the Free Software Foundation and the GNU Project aiming to create a free Unix-compatible operating system. Beginning his career at MIT’s AI Lab, Stallman contributed key software like GNU Emacs, GCC, and the GNU General Public License, popularizing copyleft to protect software freedom. He advocates software users’ rights to study, modify, and share software, viewing proprietary restrictions as unethical. Controversially, Stallman resigned from MIT and FSF in 2019 after backlash over comments related to the Epstein scandal but returned to the FSF board in 2021 amid divided opinion. He promotes specific terminology emphasizing freedom over "open source" and campaigns against digital rights management and software patents. Stallman is recognized with numerous awards and known for his uncompromising activism and eccentric personality.

# Alan Turing
Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, logician, and cryptanalyst, foundational to modern computing and artificial intelligence. Educated at Cambridge and Princeton, his 1936 paper introduced the Turing machine, formalizing computation and proving the limits of algorithmic solvability. During WWII, he played a central role in breaking Nazi Enigma codes at Bletchley Park, chiefly via developing the electromechanical bombe machine. Postwar, he contributed to early computer design (ACE), artificial intelligence theory (Turing test), and mathematical biology (morphogenesis). Turing was convicted in 1952 for homosexuality, then illegal in the UK, leading to hormonal treatment and eventual death under debated circumstances, ruled suicide. He was posthumously pardoned in 2013, and his legacy includes profound influence on computing, cryptography, and science.

# Blaise Pascal
Blaise Pascal (1623–1662), French mathematician, physicist, inventor, philosopher, and theologian, made foundational contributions to mathematics (probability theory, Pascal’s triangle), fluid mechanics, and early computing machines (Pascaline). He advanced experiments on atmospheric pressure and vacuums and invented the hydraulic press. His *Pensées* remains a seminal work in Christian philosophy, arguing for faith over pure reason and articulating Pascal’s Wager. His *Provincial Letters* satirized moral casuistry. Pascal’s religious conversion to Jansenism deeply influenced his late works. Despite early death, his enduring legacy spans mathematics, physics, philosophy, and the invention of public transport principles.

# Charles Babbage
Charles Babbage (1791–1871) was an English mathematician, engineer, and inventor, often called the "father of the computer" for designing the Difference Engine and Analytical Engine—early mechanical computing machines with features of modern computers. Educated at Cambridge, he was a leading figure in scientific societies and advocated for improvements in industrial production and science administration. His Difference Engine was designed for automatic polynomial calculations, while the Analytical Engine introduced programmable, general-purpose computation concepts using punched cards. Although his machines were never completed in his lifetime, Babbage’s work laid the groundwork for computing. He also contributed to economics, cryptography, natural theology, and social reform. Babbage’s legacy endures in computing history and numerous institutions bearing his name.

# Hedy Lamarr
Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000), born Hedwig Kiesler, was an Austrian-American actress and inventor. Celebrated as a glamorous Hollywood star, she co-invented frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology with George Antheil during WWII to prevent torpedo guidance jamming—a foundational concept for modern wireless communications like Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Despite lack of formal training, her exposure to munitions circles influenced her inventive pursuits. Lamarr was married six times, faced career challenges, and spent later years in seclusion. Posthumously, she has been widely recognized for her scientific contributions, receiving awards including induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame, and commemorations such as Google Doodles, memorials, and an asteroid named in her honor.

# Vannevar Bush
Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was an American engineer, inventor, and science administrator pivotal in shaping U.S. scientific research during and after WWII. Educated at Tufts and MIT, he invented analog computing devices like the differential analyzer and co-founded companies including Raytheon. As head of the National Defense Research Committee and later the Office of Scientific Research and Development, Bush coordinated major wartime technologies, including radar and the atomic bomb. He proposed the memex concept, a visionary precursor to hypertext and the modern internet. Postwar, Bush advocated federal support for basic research, influencing the creation of the National Science Foundation. His leadership significantly impacted science policy, technology development, and the organization of American science.

# Ada Lovelace
Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), Countess of Lovelace, was an English mathematician and writer, known for her collaboration with Charles Babbage on his Analytical Engine. She translated Luigi Menabrea’s paper on the Engine and added extensive notes including what is considered the first published algorithm, anticipating computers' future potential beyond mere calculation. Educated rigorously in mathematics despite health challenges, she combined scientific insight with poetic imagination and foresaw symbolic computation’s broad applications. Though she died young of cancer, her legacy endures as a pioneer of computing, with awards, programming languages, and commemorative events named after her. Debates continue about the extent of her programming contributions, but she is widely celebrated as a visionary in computer science history.

# Philip Rubin
Philip Rubin (b. 1949) is an American cognitive scientist and speech researcher known for advances in speech synthesis, perception, and articulatory modeling. Combining computational, linguistic, physiological, and psychological approaches, Rubin developed pioneering articulatory (ASY, CASY) and sinewave (SWS) speech synthesis programs and contributed to theories of embodied cognition, dynamic systems in speech, and audiovisual speech perception. He has held leadership roles at Haskins Laboratories, the National Science Foundation, and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, influencing science policy, ethics in research, and interdisciplinary collaboration. Rubin advocates for ethical oversight of emerging technologies and continues to contribute to speech science, computational modeling, and science policy.
