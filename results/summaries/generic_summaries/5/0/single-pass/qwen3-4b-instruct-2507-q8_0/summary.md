**Alfred North Whitehead**  
A British philosopher and mathematician, Whitehead made significant contributions to mathematics, logic, and philosophy. He co-authored *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a foundational work in mathematical logic. His philosophical system, *process philosophy*, rejected Cartesian dualism, proposing reality as dynamic events ("processes") rather than static matter. He emphasized change, interrelatedness, and experience as fundamental. Whitehead also advocated for educational reform, emphasizing experiential learning and imagination over rote memorization. He moved to the U.S. in 1924, where he became a professor at Harvard, producing influential works like *Science and the Modern World* and *Process and Reality*. Despite the difficulty of his writing, his ideas have influenced theology, ecology, and education.

**Leonardo Torres Quevedo**  
A Spanish engineer and inventor, Torres Quevedo pioneered mechanical computing and transportation. He developed the first cableway system and designed the *Niagara Spanish Aerocar*, a pioneering aerial cable car. He invented mechanical calculating machines and contributed to early computer science, including the concept of *automatics* and *remote control* via the *Telekino*. He also designed a *docking station* for airships and contributed to the development of *aerostatics*. Torres Quevedo was recognized with numerous honors and is remembered for his innovative designs in engineering and automation.

**Richard Stallman**  
A computer scientist and software freedom advocate, Stallman founded the *Free Software Foundation* and championed the *free software movement*. He developed *GNU Emacs*, *GCC*, and *GNU Make*, and promoted the concept of *copyleft*. Stallman is known for his advocacy of user freedom and against proprietary software, including software patents and DRM. He is a vocal critic of software monopolies and has supported open-source initiatives. He also contributed to the development of *the Turing test* and is known for his work in promoting free software and digital rights.

**Alan Turing**  
A British mathematician and computer scientist, Turing is renowned for his foundational work in computing and artificial intelligence. He introduced the concept of the *Turing machine* and the *Turing test*, which evaluates a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behavior. During WWII, he played a key role in breaking German ciphers at Bletchley Park, contributing significantly to the Allied victory. He also made pioneering contributions to *mathematical biology*, proposing a *reaction-diffusion system* to explain morphogenesis. After being convicted of homosexuality and undergoing chemical castration, Turing died in 1954. His legacy includes the *Turing Award* and the recognition of his work in computer science and AI.

**Blaise Pascal**  
A French mathematician, physicist, and philosopher, Pascal is known for his work in probability theory, fluid mechanics, and philosophy. He developed the *Pascal's triangle*, formulated *Pascal's law* in hydrostatics, and contributed to the study of the *cycloid*. His philosophical work, especially *Pensées*, is a cornerstone of modern thought on religion and human nature. He is credited with the invention of the *first mechanical calculator*, the *Pascaline*. Pascal also played a key role in the development of early scientific societies and is remembered for his influence on both science and theology.

**Charles Babbage**  
An English inventor and mathematician, Babbage designed the *Difference Engine* and the *Analytical Engine*, considered the first mechanical computers. His work laid the groundwork for modern computing, including the use of punched cards for programming. Babbage also contributed to the development of *operational research*, *statistical methods*, and *automatic control systems*. He advocated for the *endless frontier* of science and was instrumental in establishing government research institutions. Though his machines were never fully built during his lifetime, his ideas were foundational to the development of modern computers.

**Hedy Lamarr**  
An Austrian-born Hollywood actress and inventor, Lamarr is best known for her role in *Ecstasy* and her invention of *frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology*, a foundational concept in modern wireless communication. She co-developed the technology with composer George Antheil, which helped prevent jamming in radio signals. Despite being overlooked in her time, her work is now recognized as a key contribution to modern wireless technology. She is celebrated for her dual legacy in film and science, and her story is a symbol of innovation and resilience.

**Vannevar Bush**  
An American engineer and science administrator, Bush played a pivotal role in the U.S. scientific community during WWII. He founded the *Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD)* and was instrumental in advancing research in radar, proximity fuzes, and the atomic bomb. He advocated for *basic research* and established the *National Science Foundation (NSF)*. His visionary essay *As We May Think* introduced the concept of the *memex*, a precursor to the World Wide Web. Bush's leadership helped shape U.S. science policy and laid the foundation for modern research institutions.

**Ada Lovelace**  
A British mathematician and writer, Lovelace is regarded as the world's first computer programmer. She translated and expanded on Charles Babbage's *Analytical Engine*, including a detailed algorithm for calculating Bernoulli numbers. Her work demonstrated the potential of machines to go beyond mere calculation, anticipating modern computing. She is celebrated for her insight into the *general-purpose computing* capabilities of machines. In recognition of her contributions, the programming language *Ada* and numerous awards and institutions are named after her. She remains a symbol of women in STEM.

**Philip Rubin**  
A cognitive scientist and researcher in speech production and perception, Rubin developed computational models of speech synthesis, including *ASY* and *SWS*. He contributed to the understanding of *articulatory gestures*, *perceptual organization*, and *dynamical systems* in speech. He held leadership roles in science policy, including at the National Science Foundation and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy. Rubin has been a strong advocate for ethical issues in science and technology, particularly in human subjects research and the use of AI. His work bridges psychology, linguistics, and engineering, making significant contributions to embodied cognition and speech technology.
