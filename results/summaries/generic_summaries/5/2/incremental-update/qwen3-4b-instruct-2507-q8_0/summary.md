## Richard Stallman: A Champion of Free and Open-Source Software, Founder of the GNU Project and Free Software Foundation, Advocate for Software Freedom and Copyleft, and a Leading Voice in the Free Software Movement. He promotes "free software" over "open source" to emphasize user freedom and insists on using "GNU/Linux" or "GNU+Linux" to credit the GNU Project. A vocal critic of surveillance, he avoids cell phones and key cards and uses Tor for web access. In 2019, controversy arose over comments regarding Jeffrey Epstein and MIT professor Marvin Minsky, leading to accusations of defending Epstein and past writings on child pornography; he apologized, resigned from MIT and the Free Software Foundation (FSF), and returned to the FSF board in 2021 amid criticism and defunding from major open-source organizations. He lives in Boston, is childfree, speaks multiple languages, is an atheist, and has authored key publications including the *GNU Emacs Manual*, *GNU Make*, and the *GNU C Language Introduction and Reference Manual*, as well as *Free Software, Free Society*, a collection of essays advocating for software freedom. Diagnosed with follicular lymphoma in 2023, he is in remission.  
## Ada Lovelace: A Pioneering Mathematician and Writer, Daughter of Lord Byron, Who Developed a Strong Interest in Mathematics and Technology Despite a Difficult Childhood. She became a close friend of Mary Somerville and Charles Babbage, and is recognized as the first to envision the potential of computing machines beyond calculation. Her work on Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine, including an algorithm described in Note G—intended to compute Bernoulli numbers—is considered foundational in computer science and recognized as the world’s first published computer program. Lovelace envisioned the machine’s potential beyond numerical calculations, suggesting it could manipulate symbols and create music, highlighting early insights into general-purpose computing. While debates exist over her direct programming contributions—some argue Babbage authored earlier programs—her visionary understanding of computing as a flexible, symbolic system remains foundational. Her legacy is deeply embedded in popular culture, appearing in novels, plays, films, video games such as *Ada and the Engine*, *The Thrilling Adventures of Lovelace and Babbage*, and *Civilization VII*, as well as documentaries and TV series like *Victoria* and *Doctor Who*. Rare historical documents from her 1843 notes on the Analytical Engine are preserved in major universities and occasionally auctioned, underscoring their enduring historical and cultural significance.  

## Hedy Lamarr: A Hollywood actress and inventor, born Hedwig Eva Maria Kiesler in Vienna in 1914, who developed an early interest in technology and science. She gained fame through films such as *Samson and Delilah* (1950), a major success that won two Oscars, and became a prominent figure in Hollywood for her beauty and sophistication. Despite her screen success, she struggled with loneliness and homesickness off-screen. During World War II, she co-invented frequency-hopping technology with composer George Antheil, a foundational concept in modern wireless communication that later underpinned technologies like Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Though initially rejected by the Navy, her invention was eventually recognized and has had a lasting impact on telecommunications. She faced personal and professional challenges, including financial losses from her film *Loves of Three Queens* and a strained relationship with her son. After becoming a naturalized U.S. citizen in 1953, she faced legal disputes over her autobiography and image rights. She lived in seclusion during the 1970s and died in 2000 at age 85 from heart disease. Her legacy includes a Hollywood Walk of Fame star (1960), the Electronic Frontier Foundation’s Pioneer Award (1997), posthumous induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame (2014), an asteroid named after her (2019), a quantum telescope (2014), Google Doodles (2015, 2023), and a Star Trek vessel named in her honor (2024). She married six times, had three children, and faced controversy over her biological relationship with one son, later clarified by DNA testing. Her filmography spans from the 1930s to the 1950s, including notable roles in *Algiers*, *Samson and Delilah*, and *The Strange Woman*, and she appeared on several radio programs. References to her appear in *The Caine Mutiny*, *Blazing Saddles*, *Little Shop of Horrors*, *Half-Life 2*, *Bombshell: The Hedy Lamarr Story*, *Legends of Tomorrow*, and *Timeless*, further cementing her presence in both popular culture and scientific history.
