# Ada Lovelace

Ada Lovelace, born in 1815 to Lord Byron and Lady Byron, faced a challenging childhood marked by her father’s absence and a strict, scrutinizing mother concerned about hereditary insanity. Despite fragile health and restricted social freedoms, she developed strong mathematical talents through early education with notable tutors. Her fascination with flying, blending of poetic imagination and science, and complex personal life, including a scandalous youthful affair and later gambling debts, colored her adult years.

Lovelace is best remembered for her collaboration with Charles Babbage on his Analytical Engine. She translated Luigi Menabrea’s lecture on the machine, adding extensive notes including the first detailed algorithm for calculating Bernoulli numbers—often considered the world’s first computer program. She foresaw the machine’s potential beyond number crunching, anticipating modern computing’s symbolic processing capabilities. Her legacy is commemorated in awards, conferences, programming languages (Ada), and various institutions promoting women in STEM.

# Hedy Lamarr

Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000), born Hedwig Kiesler in Vienna, was a renowned actress and inventor. Her early European film career was marked by notoriety due to the controversial film *Ecstasy* and a controlling first marriage to arms dealer Friedrich Mandl, which exposed her to military technology and sparked her inventive interests. Escaping to Hollywood in 1938 as Hedy Lamarr, she became famous as a glamorous actress but grew bored with typecasting.

Lamarr co-invented a frequency-hopping spread spectrum technology intended to prevent torpedo jamming during WWII, patented with George Antheil. Despite initial rejection by the Navy, this invention laid groundwork for modern wireless communications. Her later years included Hollywood successes, personal struggles, legal issues, and seclusion. Posthumously, she has received numerous honors for both her cinematic and scientific contributions, including induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame.

# Philip Rubin

Philip Rubin is a distinguished cognitive scientist specializing in speech synthesis and perception. Educated at Brandeis and the University of Connecticut, his pioneering work includes articulatory and sinewave speech synthesis models (ASY and SWS), advancing understanding of speech production, multi-modal communication, and dynamical systems. Rubin contributed to articulatory phonology and gestural models, integrating biomechanics into speech research.

He has held significant leadership roles, including directing NSF's Division of Behavioral and Cognitive Sciences and serving in science policy at OSTP. Rubin’s work extends to science ethics, human subject protections, and advocacy for behavioral and cognitive sciences. He is also active in advisory capacities and has been recognized by numerous scientific societies for his research and policy contributions.

# Blaise Pascal

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, and philosopher. A child prodigy educated by his father, he made early contributions including Pascal’s theorem on conic sections and built one of the first mechanical calculators, the Pascaline. Alongside Pierre de Fermat, he laid foundational work for probability theory.

Pascal advanced mathematics with his treatise on the arithmetical triangle (now Pascal’s triangle), provided key insights in fluid mechanics (Pascal’s law), and conducted historic experiments on vacuum and atmospheric pressure. Religiously, he underwent profound conversions, producing influential theological writings such as *Pensées* and *Provincial Letters*, blending wit and defense of Christianity. He also pioneered early public transportation concepts with horse-drawn carriage networks.

# Vannevar Bush

Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was an American engineer, inventor, and science administrator. Educated at Tufts, MIT, and Harvard, he invented analog computers like the differential analyzer and contributed to electrical engineering and early digital circuit theory. Bush played a pivotal role during WWII as director of the Office of Scientific Research and Development, coordinating major research including radar, proximity fuzes, and the Manhattan Project.

He formulated the influential concept of the "memex," a proto-hypertext information system envisioning associative data linking, a precursor to the World Wide Web. Post-war, he advocated for federal support of basic research, leading to the creation of the National Science Foundation. Bush received numerous national and international honors and is remembered as a giant in shaping 20th-century science policy and technology.

# Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman (born 1953) is a computer programmer and activist, founder of the Free Software Movement and the GNU Project. Educated at Harvard and MIT, he developed key software tools such as GNU Emacs and GCC. Stallman championed software freedom against proprietary restrictions, inventing the concept of copyleft and the GNU General Public License (GPL).

He has been a vocal advocate for user rights, opposing software patents, DRM, and surveillance, and demanding precise terminology reflecting software freedom. Controversies over his social views led to his temporary resignation from MIT and FSF, but he remains active in GNU development. Stallman’s ethics and activism have profoundly influenced software culture and licensing worldwide.

# Charles Babbage

Charles Babbage (1791–1871) was an English mathematician, inventor, and computing pioneer. Educated at Cambridge, he critiqued British science and industrial efficiency and founded the British Association for the Advancement of Science. Motivated by errors in mathematical tables, Babbage designed the Difference Engine and later the more general Analytical Engine, featuring programmable punched cards, conditionals, and separate memory and control units.

Though his machines were never completed in his lifetime, his detailed designs laid the conceptual foundation for modern computers. Collaborating with Ada Lovelace, who contributed early programming insight, Babbage influenced fields from manufacturing to cryptography. His legacy endures through numerous memorials and ongoing projects to build his machines.

# Alfred North Whitehead

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician and philosopher. After a career in mathematics, including co-authoring *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, he transitioned to philosophy, producing influential works such as *Process and Reality*. Whitehead developed process philosophy, emphasizing interrelated events and change over static matter, viewing reality as experiential and relational.

A prolific educator and university administrator, Whitehead advocated educational reform emphasizing imagination and connected knowledge. His metaphysical system included a novel view of God and religion, inspiring process theology. Though difficult to penetrate, his thought influenced theology, ecology, physics, and education, with growing contemporary interest, especially in philosophy of organism and ecological ethics.

# Alan Turing

Alan Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, logician, cryptanalyst, and father of computer science. Educated at Cambridge and Princeton, he formalized computation with the Turing machine and proved the undecidability of the halting problem, foundational to theoretical computer science. At Bletchley Park during WWII, Turing led efforts to break German Enigma and Lorenz ciphers, designing the bombe machine, crucial to Allied victory.

Post-war, he pioneered early stored-program computers, proposed the Turing test for machine intelligence, and contributed to mathematical biology with morphogenesis models. Turing faced persecution for his homosexuality, leading to chemical castration and his early death, officially ruled suicide but debated. His legacy includes profound scientific contributions and later official pardons and recognition.

# Leonardo Torres Quevedo

Leonardo Torres Quevedo (1852–1936) was a Spanish engineer and inventor notable for pioneering electromechanical computing and automation. He developed innovative cable cars, including the still-operational Niagara Spanish Aerocar, and created early analogue calculating machines capable of solving complex equations with electromechanical components.

He innovated remote control technology with his 1903 Telekino system, one of the first radio-controlled devices, demonstrated controlling boats from shore. Torres designed dirigibles with advanced mooring masts and was an early pioneer in automated chess with *El Ajedrecista*, the first electromechanical chess automaton capable of playing an endgame unaided. His work anticipated aspects of modern robotics, programming, and machine control, earning international scientific honors and a lasting influence in engineering and computing history.
