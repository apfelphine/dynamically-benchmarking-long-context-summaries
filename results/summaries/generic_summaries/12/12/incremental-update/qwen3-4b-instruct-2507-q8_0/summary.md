- **National Merit Scholarship Program**: Identifies high-achieving U.S. high school students via the PSAT/NMSQT, awarding recognition and scholarships based on academic performance, extracurricular involvement, and recommendations. Eligible students must be U.S. citizens or permanent residents, enrolled full-time, and take the PSAT in their junior year. Approximately 16,000 earn Semifinalist status and 15,000 advance to Finalist status, with around 8,000 receiving Merit Scholarships (up to $2,500) and additional awards from corporate and college sponsors. Finalists may also qualify for Special Scholarships. Universities such as the University of Florida and Harvard enroll large numbers of scholars. Critics, including NACAC and FairTest, argue that relying solely on a single standardized test is unfair and lacks transparency, especially regarding state-specific score requirements and data availability.

- **United Negro College Fund (UNCF)**: Founded in 1944 to support African American education, the UNCF offers scholarships to students at HBCUs and beyond, with a majority of recipients being African American. - **Bright Futures Scholarship Program (Florida)**: A merit-based scholarship program in Florida, modeled after Georgia's HOPE Scholarship, initially provided full tuition coverage to over 39% of high school graduates. It faced criticism for disproportionately benefiting wealthier students and was restructured in 2011 with raised SAT requirements and reduced award amounts, impacting Black and Latino students more severely. In 2014, the U.S. Department of Education found evidence of disparate impacts on minorities but no intent to discriminate. In 2018, the program was overhauled into the **Florida Academic Scholar (FAS)**, **Florida Medallion Scholar (FMS)**, **Gold Seal Vocational**, and **Gold Seal CAPE** programs, restoring full tuition coverage for FAS and FMS. The program remains merit-based, requiring strict academic, test score, and service hour standards.  
  - **FAS (Full Academic Scholar)**: 100% tuition at public and private institutions.  
  - **FMS (Florida Academic Scholar)**: 100% for associate degrees at Florida colleges; 75% elsewhere.  
  - **GSV & GSC**: Per-credit funding for technical, career, or bachelor’s programs; no summer use.  
  - **ATS (Academic Top Scholar)**: Extra $44/credit (semester) or $29/credit (quarter) on top of FAS.

GPA drops allow reinstatement under certain conditions. Withdrawn courses require repayment. Deferrals up to 5 years; military enlistment adds full enlistment time plus 5 years. Degree completion in ≤7 semesters allows one semester of graduate study funding.

- **Edmund S. Muskie**: A prominent American political figure born in 1914 to Polish immigrant parents in Rumford, Maine, Muskie grew up speaking Polish before transitioning to English. He graduated as valedictorian from Stephens High School in 1932. He attended Bates College, where he excelled in debate and student government, and later earned a law degree from Cornell Law School with financial support from William Bingham II. After passing the Maine Bar in 1940, he opened a law practice in Waterville and served on the Zoning Board. He married Jane Gray in 1948 and had five children. During World War II, he served in the U.S. Navy Reserve from 1942 to 1945, working as a diesel engineer and later as a deck officer, including active duty on the USS Brackett. After returning to Maine, he entered politics, winning a seat in the Maine House of Representatives in 1946. He advocated for bipartisanship, served on key committees, and held leadership roles in local government and economic policy. He briefly served in the Office of Price Stabilization and later joined the Democratic National Committee. **He overcame a serious injury in 1953 that led to a period of unconsciousness and rehabilitation. He entered politics as a Democrat, running for governor in 1954 and winning an upset victory, becoming Maine’s first Roman Catholic governor and ending Republican dominance. During his two terms (1955–1959), he advanced environmental policies, expanded public services, promoted economic development, and reformed state institutions. His leadership significantly expanded the Democratic Party in Maine, reshaping the state’s political landscape. After resigning to take a U.S. Senate seat in 1959, Muskie served in the Senate from 1959 to 1980, winning three elections. His 1970 "election eve speech"—a powerful, bipartisan appeal against political fear and deceit—became a defining moment in his career and in American political discourse.**  
  - As a U.S. Senator from Maine (1959–1980), Muskie gained prominence through his advocacy for environmental protection, civil rights, and anti-war policies. He played a key role in passing major legislation, including the Clean Air Act (1963, 1970), Clean Water Act (1965, 1977), and the Civil Rights Act of 1964. A leading voice in the anti-Vietnam War movement, he criticized U.S. military actions and supported civil rights, earning the nickname "Mr. Clean" for his environmental efforts. He served as chair of the Senate Budget Committee, helping establish the Congressional Budget Office and formalize federal budgeting. Though he ran for president in 1968 as Humphrey’s running mate, he remained influential in Congress, shaping policy on environmental regulation, federalism, and intergovernmental cooperation.  
  - **Vice-Presidential Campaigns**: Muskie served as a vice-presidential candidate in the 1968 and 1972 presidential elections, narrowly losing in 1968 and facing a damaging "Canuck letter" scandal in 1972 that undermined his campaign.  
  - **Secretary of State**: After retiring in 1981, Muskie served as U.S. Secretary of State from 1980 to 1981, addressing the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, the Iran hostage crisis, and Cold War arms control, while advocating for diplomacy over confrontation. Despite a "draft Muskie" movement in 1980, he remained loyal to President Carter. After retiring in 1981, Muskie continued contributing to public policy through legal work and leadership roles at Georgetown University.  
  - **Additional Recognition**: Awarded the **Laetare Medal in 1981** and served on the **Tower Commission in 1987**, which critically examined the Reagan administration’s Iran-Contra affair. He died in **1996 at 81** after a battle with heart failure, with his grave stone later corrected to reflect the accurate date. Revered for his leadership in Maine, Muskie transformed the state’s political and economic landscape, ending the "as Maine goes" sentiment and advancing environmental, civil rights, and fiscal reforms. He is widely recognized as the **"father of the 1960s environmental movement"** for championing landmark environmental laws such as the Clean Air and Clean Water Acts, and as the **"father of the federal budget process"** for shaping modern budgeting. A respected diplomat, he led key negotiations with the Soviet Union and Iran, and was a strong civil rights advocate, including supporting the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Martin Luther King Jr. Day. Known for his integrity, directness, and intellectual rigor, Muskie left a lasting legacy in American politics, diplomacy, and environmental policy.

- **Margaret Busby's *Daughters of Africa* and *New Daughters of Africa***: Margaret Busby’s landmark anthology *Daughters of Africa* (1992) compiled writings by women of African descent and became a foundational text in Black feminist literature, inspiring global editions and acclaim. - **Marshall Scholarship**: Established in 1954 (corrected from 1953), enables intellectually distinguished American students to study in the UK, fostering mutual understanding between the U.S. and Britain. Initially awarding 12 scholars, it has grown to 50 annually since 2023, with the largest cohort in its 70-year history. Selection emphasizes academic excellence (minimum GPA of 3.7), leadership potential, and commitment to cross-cultural engagement. Scholars study at top UK institutions like Oxford, Cambridge, and UCL, contributing to knowledge in science, humanities, and the arts while acting as lifelong ambassadors. The scholarship mirrors the Rhodes and Fulbright programs in structure and goals, though with broader university access. Marshall Scholars are more likely to pursue careers in academia and research, whereas Rhodes Scholars are more diverse in professional paths. - **Rhodes Scholarship**: Established in 1902 by the will of Cecil Rhodes, is a prestigious postgraduate scholarship awarded to students from around the world to study at the University of Oxford. Founded to promote unity among English-speaking peoples and strengthen diplomatic ties between Britain and the U.S., the program emphasizes academic excellence, moral character, and leadership. Initially limited to British Empire nations, it expanded globally after 1993, with a current global acceptance rate of 0.7%, making it one of the most competitive scholarships. Selection criteria include scholastic achievement, leadership, and character, with a rigorous process involving university endorsements and national committees. The scholarship covers tuition, living expenses, and provides access to Oxford’s residential colleges and Rhodes House. Despite historical controversies over Rhodes’s white supremacist legacy, the program continues to support leaders in human rights and social justice, including transgender and non-binary applicants. In 2019, Hera Jay Brown became the first transgender woman selected, and in 2020, two non-binary scholars were also chosen. The Rhodes Trust has undergone legislative updates and received major funding, including a £75 million donation in 2013, and has seen leadership changes and recovery efforts.  
  - **Expansion and Inclusion**: Initially awarded only to male students from specific schools in South Africa and other Commonwealth countries, the scholarship admitted women in 1977 following the Sex Discrimination Act of 1975, with women now making up about 35% of U.S. scholars. The program has expanded globally, including to China and the UAE, marking its largest expansion since women's inclusion.  
  - **Controversies**: Critics, including scholars like Ntokozo Qwabe and the group Redress Rhodes, argue for renaming the scholarship, critical engagement with Rhodes’s legacy, and ending ceremonial tributes. Many Rhodes Scholars pursue careers in finance and private law rather than public service, which undermines the scholarship’s original mission of fostering public service and leadership in democratic governance.

- **Rhodes Scholarship Impact and Global Influence**: The Rhodes Scholarship has produced a wide range of influential scholars across academia, law, medicine, human rights, and the arts. While few have "changed the world," most have led respectable careers, with many becoming heads of government or prominent figures in their fields. Notable achievements include breakthroughs in medicine (e.g., penicillin, cancer genomics), advocacy for civil rights and social justice, and contributions to science, education, and policy. Rhodes Scholars have played key roles in advancing human rights in Africa, promoting educational equity, and innovating in surgery, genetics, and space exploration. The scholarship's model has inspired similar programs worldwide, with close parallels to the Marshall and Gates Cambridge Scholarships, though distinctions in focus and eligibility remain historically notable.
