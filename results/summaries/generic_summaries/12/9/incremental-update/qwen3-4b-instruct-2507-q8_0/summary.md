## **Miss America Pageant**

The **Miss America Pageant**, originating in 1920 as *The Fall Frolic* and evolving into a major cultural event by 1921 with the first title "Miss America" coined by Herb Test, began as a bathing and entertainment-focused spectacle. It historically excluded African Americans for decades, reflecting deep-rooted racism, and faced widespread criticism for sexism, militarism, and cultural exclusion. Early diversities were introduced with Jewish (Bess Myerson, 1945), Native American (1941), Latina (1948), and Asian-American (1948) contestants, though African American participation remained absent until 1970, when Cheryl Browne became the first Black contestant. Vanessa Williams won in 1984 but resigned amid a scandal, and Nina Davuluri became the first Indian-American winner in 2014.

Milestones in diversity and inclusion include Sharlene Wells Hawkes (first foreign-born bilingual Miss America), Heather Whitestone (first deaf Miss America), Nicole Johnson (first with diabetes), Angela Perez Baraquio (first Asian-American and Filipino-American), and Erin O’Flaherty (first openly lesbian Miss America in 2016). The pageant faced scrutiny over scholarship claims, with John Oliver criticizing the organization and social media backlash against Nina Davuluri. Despite reaffirming commitments in 2016, actual disbursements remain lower than claimed. The pageant returned to Atlantic City in 2014 and aired on ABC through 2019.

In 2018, *Miss America 2.0* launched with reforms—eliminating swimsuit competitions and appearance-based judging—to address objectification and promote inclusivity. However, many changes were reversed by 2023 under new leadership, signaling ongoing tensions between reform and tradition. The pageant moved to Connecticut in 2019, was postponed due to the pandemic, and saw its first transgender titleholder in 2022. In 2023, under CEO Robin Fleming, the format reverted to include a swimsuit-style fitness competition and introduced a "HER Story" storytelling category, with age limits raised to 28. A 2023 documentary highlighted historical issues of body-shaming and racism. In 2024, the organization filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, later requesting dismissal. Recent titleholders include Cassie Donegan (2026, New York), Abbie Stockard (2025, Alabama), and Madison Marsh (2024, Colorado). Hosts have included Billy Gilman and Nikki Novak (2025), with a long history of notable personalities from the 1980s to present.

The organization faced leadership scandals, including inappropriate emails leading to resignations and tensions over decisions such as Cara Mund’s claims of marginalization and legal disputes. Gretchen Carlson was appointed chairwoman in 2017, marking a pivotal shift during the *Miss America 2.0* era. The pageant’s evolution reflects broader societal shifts in race, gender, and cultural representation, making it a significant symbol of both exclusion and progressive change in American culture.

## **British Educational Institutions and the "King's Scholar" Title**

The term *"King's Scholar"* is a prestigious academic honor with deep historical roots in British education. At **Eton College**, 70 boys are admitted at age 13 and live in the original College buildings, wearing black gowns and participating in exclusive traditions. The title traces back to Edward II’s establishment of scholars at Cambridge and has evolved into a symbol of elite academic heritage. Notable alumni include George Orwell, John Maynard Keynes, and Boris Johnson.

At **Westminster School**, the *King's and Queen's Scholars* are selected through competitive exams, attend the oldest house (the College), and wear gowns during services in Westminster Abbey, reflecting their distinguished status.

At **King’s College Cambridge**, undergraduates who earn first-class degrees are awarded the title "King’s Scholar," receiving a £350 prize and a formal dinner in the King’s College Chapel. This tradition dates back to Henry VI and links the college to Eton, reinforcing a shared academic lineage.

Additionally, the **seven King's Schools**—Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester—were established by Henry VIII in 1541 to provide education for the elite. These institutions award scholarships based on academic or musical excellence, originally funding all pupils; today, scholarships are performance-based and often include fee discounts. In 1973, **Queen's Scholarships** were introduced by statute to honor Queen Elizabeth II and support co-education.

At **King's Ely**, 12 boys and girls are awarded annually based on GCSE grades; scholars are admitted in a choral service and participate in the "Hoop Trundle" tradition, enjoying privileges such as marriage and burial in Ely Cathedral.  

At **King's School, Canterbury**, scholars achieve exceptional GCSE results (typically over 9 A*), are admitted in a cathedral service, and hold rights to marry in the crypt and be buried in the cathedral.  

At **King's School, Worcester**, the highest academic or musical award is given; scholars in Lower Remove are always titled King's Scholars; those in Lower Sixth are King's (male) or Queen's (female) Scholars. Initiated in a cathedral service, the Senior Scholar holds ceremonial duties, including demanding a holiday from the judge ("Judge's Day") and rights to marriage and burial.

The **Marshall Scholarship**, founded in 1953 as a memorial to George C. Marshall, promotes U.S.-UK understanding by enabling intellectually distinguished American students to study in the UK, promoting academic excellence, cultural appreciation, and lifelong diplomatic engagement. Selection prioritizes academic achievement, leadership potential, and motivation, with a strong emphasis on character and intellectual capacity. Applicants must be endorsed by their universities and undergo regional interviews; around 3.5% of endorsed applicants are selected annually. Scholars receive full tuition coverage and a maintenance stipend for a two-year postgraduate study at any UK university (excluding pre-professional programs), with most attending top institutions like Oxford, Cambridge, and LSE. The program is highly selective, comparable to the Rhodes and Mitchell Scholarships, though slightly less so than the Gates Cambridge Scholarship. Marshall Scholars are more academically focused than Rhodes Scholars, with a higher proportion entering academia and research, while both programs contribute significantly to transatlantic relations.

The **Association of Marshall Scholars (AMS)**, founded in 1988, complements the Marshall Scholarship by conducting annual public opinion polls on U.S. perceptions of the UK and hosting the *Marshall Forum* annually to discuss key transatlantic issues. Held in different cities, the Forum features prominent American and British leaders and often coincides with anniversaries of the Marshall Plan or the Marshall Scholarship. Notable events include the 70th-anniversary Forum in 2017 on the Marshall Plan’s legacy, the 2018 Forum on scientific innovation, and the 2019 Forum on peace and prosperity. In 2024, the Forum will take place in Chicago on global markets, monetary policy, and innovation. The event has featured numerous Marshall Scholars and high-level officials, emphasizing transatlantic cooperation and shared challenges.

## **Edmund S. Muskie: A Profile in Academic and Political Excellence**

Edmund S. Muskie, born in 1914 to Polish immigrant parents in Rumford, Maine, grew up speaking Polish until age 4 and later became fluent in English. He excelled academically, graduating valedictorian from Stephens High School and earning honors at Bates College and Cornell Law School, where he secured financial aid through a donation from William Bingham II. He practiced law in Waterville, helped draft local zoning laws, and later entered politics, winning a seat in the Maine House of Representatives in 1946. Despite losing a mayoral bid in 1947, he remained active in local governance and politics, eventually serving as Minority Leader and helping revitalize the Maine Democratic Party. He later worked in federal economic policy before joining the Democratic National Committee.

**Expanded Political Career**: In 1953, Muskie overcame a serious injury that required hospitalization and rehabilitation. He entered politics as a Democrat, winning the 1954 gubernatorial election as a surprise upset, becoming Maine’s first Roman Catholic governor and ending Republican dominance. During his two terms (1955–1959), he advanced environmental policies, including the New England Interstate Water Pollution Control Compact, expanded state parks, promoted economic development, and reformed public education and infrastructure. His leadership significantly expanded the Democratic Party in Maine, shifting the political landscape. After resigning in 1959 to join the U.S. Senate, Muskie served from 1959 to 1980, winning three terms. His 1970 "election eve speech"—a powerful, bipartisan appeal for trust in citizens and opposition to fear-based politics—became a defining moment in his career and a notable political address.  

Muskie gained prominence through his environmental advocacy, legislative achievements, and evolving political stance. He championed key environmental legislation, including the Clean Air Act (1963) and Clean Water Act (1965, 1972, 1977), and was instrumental in advancing civil rights and anti-war efforts. A key figure in the Senate, he served on major committees, chaired the Subcommittee on Air and Water Pollution, and led efforts to limit nuclear arms development and curb executive overreach. Though initially combative with Lyndon B. Johnson, he developed a cooperative relationship, especially on domestic policy. Muskie ran for president in 1968 as Humphrey’s running mate and later in 1972, where he was a leading Democratic candidate. He narrowly lost in 1968 and withdrew from the 1972 race after a damaging forged "Canuck letter" and negative media coverage. In 1976, he was not selected as vice-presidential nominee despite strong support. Appointed U.S. Secretary of State in 1980 following Cyrus Vance’s resignation, Muskie worked to manage the Soviet-Afghan conflict, opposed nuclear arms buildup, and negotiated the Iran hostage crisis, which ended in a release after 444 days. He retired in 1981 and remained active in diplomacy and law, serving in academic leadership roles.

Known for his directness, integrity, and intellectual rigor, he was also a demanding and private figure. He died in 1996 at age 81 and is remembered as one of Maine’s most influential politicians and a transformative figure in American environmental and civil rights policy.
