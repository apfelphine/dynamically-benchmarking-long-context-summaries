The **Shuping Scholarship**, founded in 1939 by Koo Shuping in Shanghai, supports academically excellent students from poor families with full tuition, extracurricular opportunities, and holistic development, emphasizing education, filial piety, and social responsibility. Koo personally funded the scholarships until 1995, when he donated HK$10 million to establish the **Shuping Scholarship Foundation in Hong Kong**, which now awards over 700 scholarships annually to students across 37 secondary and 24 tertiary institutions in China, including expansions to Beijing, Huzhou, and performing arts schools. The foundation, overseen by a volunteer-led executive committee of family members and past scholars, revived in 1986 with government and alumni support and upholds the motto *"Return to the Society what you have gained from it."* It grew from 300 to over 1,100 students by 1949, with notable initiatives like the Refugee Children’s Home and Emergency Medical Fund. Koo remained deeply involved until his death in 1998, and his ashes were buried in Huzhou beside his parents.

The **Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes** (German Student Foundation), founded in 1948, supports outstanding undergraduate, graduate, and PhD students in Germany, the EU, and Switzerland, based on academic excellence, initiative, and social responsibility. Students receive a €300 monthly allowance, need-based grants up to €855/month, and additional support for families or research; PhD scholars receive €1,550–1,650 monthly (from 2025) plus a research allowance. Extensions beyond the sixth semester are granted based on performance. It operates internal and open programs, including partnerships with KAUST and the Max Weber Scholarship, and is well-represented at public universities like Heidelberg (657 scholars, 2.25% of students) and Freiburg (348 scholars, 1.44%), with higher participation at private institutions like Bucerius Law School (8.41%). Alumni include Nobel laureates, scientists, politicians, and leaders in arts and journalism.

The **Harkness Fellowship**, established in 1925 by the Commonwealth Fund, initially supported British graduates in the U.S. and later expanded to Commonwealth nations. Since 1997, it has focused exclusively on healthcare policy and practice, offering 12-month fellowships to international professionals for research and collaboration at leading U.S. institutions. Administered by the Commonwealth Fund with support from Germany, Canada, Switzerland, the UK, and the Netherlands, it fosters global dialogue through orientation, policy briefings, and reporting seminars. Alumni include prominent academics, scientists, artists, and public figures.

The **Thurgood Marshall College Fund (TMCF)**, established in 1987 and renamed in 2006, supports public historically black colleges and universities (HBCUs) through scholarships, capacity building, and programmatic support across **38 member institutions**, including **34 HBCUs, 2 Predominantly Black Institutions (PBIs), and 1 JUCO**. Key institutions include Howard University ($712.4M endowment), Tuskegee University ($129M), and North Carolina A&T ($178M). TMCF differentiates itself from the UNCF by focusing on public HBCUs and fostering entrepreneurship. It acquired the Opportunity Funding Corporation (OFC) in 2013 to expand talent identification and entrepreneurial support. Key leaders include Johnny C. Taylor Jr. (2010–2018), Dwayne Ashley (1999–2010), and Harry L. Williams (since 2018). Major donors include Apple, the Charles Koch Foundation, and Boeing.

The **Daughters of Africa** anthology, edited by Margaret Busby, is a landmark collection of over 200 women of African descent—including Maya Angelou, Toni Morrison, and Zora Neale Hurston—first published in 1992. It celebrated African women’s voices and inspired global recognition. Its 2019 companion, *New Daughters of Africa*, expanded the canon to over 200 writers from diverse backgrounds and generations, emphasizing black feminist literature and sisterhood. The anthology has been widely praised for its diversity, historical depth, and role in affirming African women’s contributions to literature. A **Margaret Busby New Daughters of Africa Award** was established in partnership with SOAS, University of London, to support African women students, with recipients announced in 2020 and 2023.

The **United Negro College Fund (UNCF)**, founded in 1944, supports African American education through scholarships to students of all ethnicities, with many notable alumni in politics, arts, and public service. Since 1980, the "Lou Rawls Parade of Stars" telethon has raised over $200 million. Major donations include $25 million from Koch Industries (2014), $50 million from the Lilly Endowment (2015) for the Career Pathways Initiative, $120 million from Reed Hastings (2020), and an additional $100 million from Lilly in 2024. MacKenzie Scott donated $70 million in 2025 to strengthen HBCUs. UNCF serves over 100 member HBCUs, including Spelman College ($390M endowment), Xavier University ($171M), and Morehouse College. Most are Christian-affiliated, with many participating in NCAA or NAIA divisions and regional conferences like SIAC, CIAA, and HBCUAC.

The **Rhodes Scholarship**, established in 1902 by Cecil Rhodes’s will, promotes unity among English-speaking peoples and strengthens diplomatic ties between Britain and the U.S., with global expansion. Administered by the Rhodes Trust in Oxford, it is one of the world’s most selective postgraduate scholarships (global acceptance rate: 0.7%). Selection emphasizes academic excellence, moral character, and leadership. Initially restricted to male students from specific schools and white applicants, women were admitted in 1977, and the program expanded globally, including to China and the UAE. Despite historical controversies over colonialism, racial exclusion, and disproportionate representation in finance and private law, it has produced influential leaders in government, science, and human rights—such as Bill Clinton, Bob Hawke, Howard Florey, and Pardis Sabeti. The **J. William Fulbright Prize for International Understanding** honors global leaders and cultural figures, including Nelson Mandela and Angela Merkel.

The **Bolashak Scholarship**, established in 1993 by Kazakhstan’s first president, Nursultan Nazarbayev, sends Kazakh youth abroad for education to instill Western democratic values. Over 12,800 scholarships have been awarded as of 2018, with many graduates in government and private sectors. Ethnic Kazakhs dominate due to language requirements, though the population has shifted since 2008. Recipients access leading universities in 32 countries, including top U.S., European, Asian, and Australian institutions.

The **British Council**, established in 1934, promotes English language and British culture abroad, operating in over 100 countries. Key initiatives include IELTS, "Peacekeeping English," Education UK, Erasmus+, and arts programs like ACCELERATE. It runs teacher training in North Korea and offers MOOCs. However, it faces significant controversies: outsourcing to India, pay disputes in Taiwan, failure to protect Afghan teachers post-2021, expense scandals, closures in Russia, Syria, and Europe, and criticism over its stance on Palestine and exclusion of dissident Chinese writers. Allegations include elitism in language courses, data breaches, intelligence involvement in Russia and Myanmar, and political influence in education policy. Critics also highlight unaddressed customer communications and ties to MPs.

The **National Merit Scholarship Program** identifies top high school juniors via the PSAT/NMSQT, awarding scholarships to Finalists and Semifinalists. Notable recipients include Bill Gates, Steve Ballmer, and Paul Krugman. Top universities like Harvard and the University of Florida enroll many scholars, with some offering guaranteed merit funding. Critics argue the program is unfair and lacks transparency due to state-specific score requirements and single-test reliance.

The **Fulbright Program**, established in 1946, promotes international peace through educational exchange, operating in over 160 countries. It includes student, scholar, teacher, and professional exchanges, managed by 49 bi-national commissions and organizations like IIE and CIES. Despite political interference under the Trump administration—leading to blocked awards and resignations of 11 of 12 board members—the program remains a cornerstone of global academic cooperation. The **J. William Fulbright Prize** honors global leaders and cultural figures, including Mandela, Desmond Tutu, and Angela Merkel. Fulbright alumni have achieved major accomplishments in government, academia, and industry, including Nobel and Pulitzer Prize winners.

The **Bright Futures Scholarship Program** in Florida offers merit-based scholarships to high school graduates, including levels such as Florida Academic Scholar (FAS), Florida Medallion Scholar (FMS), Florida Gold Seal Vocational (GSV), and Florida Gold Seal CAPE (GSC), with an added Academic Top Scholar (ATS) level. Awards are based on GPA, credit completion, and regional rankings. Scholarships are renewable for up to five years, with deferral options and military enrollment extensions. The program has faced criticism for racial bias, declining access, and rising SAT/ACT thresholds, especially affecting minority and low-income students. In 2025, it began accepting scores from the Classic Learning Test (CLT). Funding cuts have reduced coverage and aid amounts over time.

The **Miss America Pageant**, originating in 1919, evolved into a major beauty contest by 1921. It was racially segregated until 1950, with Bess Myerson (1945) as the first Jewish winner and Cheryl Browne (1971) as the first African-American contestant. Vanessa Williams (1984) resigned amid a scandal; Nina Davuluri (2014) faced xenophobic backlash. In 2018, under Gretchen Carlson, the pageant introduced "Miss America 2.0," removing swimsuit competitions and emphasizing personal qualities. However, changes were reversed in 2023 under Robin Fleming, who reinstated the swimsuit competition (replaced with fitness modeling), introduced the "HER Story" talent category, and raised the age limit to 28. A 2023 documentary, *Secrets of Miss America*, exposed historical issues like body-shaming and racism. In November 2024, the organization filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, later seeking dismissal. Recent titleholders include Cassie Donegan (2026), Abbie Stockard (2025), and Madison Marsh (2024).

The **Kennedy Scholarship**, established in 1964, supports outstanding American students to study at Harvard or MIT, with a permanent memorial site at Runnymede, England. Administered by the UK Kennedy Memorial Trust, it emphasizes intellectual ability, public service, and resilience. Over 600 scholars have been awarded, many of whom have held prominent roles in UK politics and public life. Notable alumni include economists Ros Altmann and Mervyn King, journalist Zanny Minton Beddoes, and academics Simon Schaffer and David Held.

The **Marshall Scholarship**, established in 1953 in honor of George C. Marshall, enables intellectually distinguished young Americans to study in the UK, promoting academic excellence and transatlantic understanding. With 50 scholars annually (as of 2023), it is highly selective—requiring a minimum GPA of 3.7, strong character, and clear academic plans. Scholars may study at any UK university, with a preference for Oxford, Cambridge, and LSE. The **Association of Marshall Scholars (AMS)** hosts the annual **Marshall Forum**, bringing together leaders to discuss transatlantic issues such as global health, innovation, and the rule of law. The **Marshall Medal** honors individuals who advance U.S.-British understanding. Notable recipients include Stephen Breyer, Ray Dolby, Roger Tsien (Nobel Prize in Chemistry), and Neil Gorsuch.

The **Norway Scholarship**, established in 1919 by Arthur Ivor Garland Jayne, enables Norwegian students to study at Wadham College, Oxford. Inspired by cultural exchange during World War I, it faced funding gaps during and after the 1940s and 1970s but was revived in the late 1970s. It supports scholars in law, literature, medicine, and sciences. Annual dinners in Oslo, often featuring Oxford guests including King Harald V, continue to strengthen ties between Norway and Oxford.

Additionally, the title **"King’s Scholar"** is used in several English institutions, symbolizing academic excellence and historical endowments:
- At **Westminster School**, King’s or Queen’s Scholars are selected through competitive exams, board at the oldest house (College), and wear gowns during Abbey services.
- At **King’s College Cambridge**, King’s Scholars receive a £350 prize upon achieving first-class degrees, attend a chapel ceremony, and dine free with their Directors of Studies (except in final year).
- The **seven King’s Schools** (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, Worcester), re-founded by Henry VIII in 1541, award scholarships based on academic or musical achievement, often with fee discounts.
  - **King’s Ely**: Awards 12 students annually (since 1973) based on GCSE grades; scholars are admitted in a choral evensong service, participate in the "Hoop Trundle" tradition, and enjoy lifelong privileges such as burial and marriage in Ely Cathedral.
  - **King’s School, Canterbury**: Scholars achieve exceptional GCSE results (often 9 A*); they attend a cathedral service, walk down the aisle first, and may wear scholar jumpers; former scholars can marry or be buried in the cathedral crypt.
  - **King’s School, Worcester**: Offers the highest scholarship; scholars are appointed in Year 9 or 12 based on exams or interviews. All Lower Remove scholars are titled King’s Scholars; female Upper Sixth scholars are titled Queen’s Scholars. Scholars are initiated into the cathedral foundation, wear white surplices, and enjoy privileges such as marriage, burial, and (theoretically) grazing animals on College Green. The Senior Scholar annually demands a holiday from the judge in Latin ("Judge’s Day").

These traditions reflect deep historical ties to monarchs and underscore the enduring significance of academic and religious heritage in English education.
