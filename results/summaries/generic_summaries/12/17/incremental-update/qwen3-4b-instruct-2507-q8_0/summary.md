**King's Scholars** originate from historical royal endowments at institutions such as Cambridge, Eton, Westminster, and the King's Schools. At **Eton**, scholars (or "Collegers") are selected through competitive exams, live in the central College building, wear black gowns, and participate in exclusive traditions; they are distinct from Oppidans (fee-paying students). At **Westminster**, scholars are named King's or Queen's Scholars based on the reigning monarch and live in the oldest house, College. At **King's College Cambridge**, first-class degree students receive a £350 prize and attend a formal ceremony. The **seven King's Schools** (e.g., King's Ely) award scholarships based on academic or musical merit, with girls now included since 1973. Scholars at these schools enjoy privileges such as cathedral services, special gowns, and traditions like the "Hoop Trundle."  

At **King's Ely, Canterbury, and Worcester**, scholars are selected based on academic or musical excellence and receive lifelong membership in the cathedral foundation. They enjoy privileges including marriage and burial in the cathedral, wearing white gowns or surplices during services, and special ceremonial rights. At Worcester, scholars are titled "King's" or "Queen's" Scholars by gender and year of appointment; the Senior Scholar leads annual traditions, including demanding a holiday from the judge (Judge's Day).  

**Edmund S. Muskie**, born in 1914 in Rumford, Maine, to Polish immigrant parents, was a student at Stephens High School and Bates College, where he excelled academically and in debate. He earned a partial scholarship to Cornell Law School, funded temporarily by William Bingham II, and graduated cum laude. He began a law practice in Waterville, contributed to local policy, and married Jane Gray in 1948, with five children. During WWII, he served in the U.S. Navy Reserve, training and serving as a diesel engineer and deck officer, ultimately escorting convoys and sinking a Japanese cargo ship before being discharged in 1945. He began his political career in the Maine House of Representatives in 1946, winning a surprise election amid a Republican-dominated legislature. He advocated for bipartisanship and later lost a mayoral bid in 1947, but remained active in local politics. In 1954, he ran a successful campaign against the Republican incumbent, becoming Maine’s first Roman Catholic governor and ending Republican dominance. His first term focused on environmental protection, including signing the New England Interstate Water Pollution Control Compact, expanding state parks, and promoting public investment. He expanded infrastructure, supported education and labor, and reformed state finances. Re-elected in 1956, he advanced economic development through initiatives like the Maine Guarantee Authority and highway construction. His tenure significantly expanded the Democratic Party in Maine and reshaped state politics, culminating in a constitutional amendment to move the state’s general election to November. Muskie resigned in 1959 to take a seat in the U.S. Senate.  

As a U.S. Senator from Maine (1959–1980), Muskie won four consecutive elections, becoming the first Democrat elected to the Senate in Maine. A leading advocate of environmentalism, he championed landmark legislation including the Clean Air Act (1963, 1970), the Clean Water Act (1965, 1972), and the Model Cities Bill, earning the nickname "Mr. Clean" and being hailed as the "father of the 1960s environmental movement." He played a key role in the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and helped establish a formal federal budget process. His 1970 "election eve speech," which criticized political fear and name-calling and was praised for its bipartisan tone, solidified his reputation as a unifying figure. A vocal anti-war advocate during the Vietnam War, he spoke at the 1968 Democratic Convention and urged President Johnson to end bombing campaigns. In later years, he helped pass the Clean Water Act after overcoming President Nixon’s veto through a bipartisan override. As Senate Budget Committee chairman, he oversaw the passage of the Clean Water Act (1977) and amendments to environmental laws. In 1972, his campaign was damaged by the forged "Canuck letter," which falsely accused him of racial insensitivity, though later proven a Nixon campaign smear. He ran in 1976 but did not secure the nomination, instead supporting Walter Mondale. In 1980, he was appointed U.S. Secretary of State by President Carter, following Cyrus Vance’s resignation after the failed Operation Eagle Claw. Though a "draft Muskie" movement emerged in the Democratic primaries, Muskie backed out to support Carter. During his tenure, he opposed Soviet recognition of Kabul in Afghanistan and engaged in diplomatic efforts with the USSR.  

After retiring in 1981, Muskie remained active in public service, serving on the Tower Commission investigating the Iran-Contra affair, and was honored with the Laetare Medal. He died in 1996, and his legacy endures as a transformative figure in American politics, diplomacy, and environmental policy, particularly as a strong advocate for nuclear arms control and a critic of Reagan’s rejection of the SALT II treaty.  

The **Edmund S. Muskie Internship Program**, established in 1992 by U.S. Congress and administered by organizations including Cultural Vistas since 2015, provides summer internships, career training, and financial support to Eurasian graduate students in the U.S., continuing his legacy of public service and international engagement. Muskie also received numerous honors, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom and honorary degrees from multiple universities, and is remembered for his integrity, intellect, and leadership. **Muskie Day** is celebrated annually in Maine in his honor.  

The **Norway Scholarship**, founded in 1920 by Arthur Ivor Garland Jayne, enables Norwegian students to study at Oxford University, with ongoing support from alumni and the Oxford University Society. It maintains a historical record from 1920 to 2025, documenting past scholars, their degree subjects (including law, literature, economics, medicine, history, philosophy, and sciences), and periods when awards were suspended—such as during WWII (1940–1945), 1961–1963, 1973–1975, 1977, 1985, 2004–2005, 2017–2018, and 2021–2022—reflecting both academic breadth and the impact of global events on educational access.  

The **Marshall Scholarship**, established in 1953 as a living memorial to U.S. Secretary of State George C. Marshall, fosters U.S.-UK relations by enabling intellectually distinguished American students to study in the UK. It emphasizes academic excellence, personal character, and the potential to contribute to society and strengthen bilateral understanding. Initially limited to 12 scholars, the program expanded to 50 places by 2023, with a record cohort of 51 selected in 2024. Selection involves university endorsements and regional interviews, focusing on leadership, motivation, and a broad range of interests. The scholarship is a prestigious U.S. government-funded postgraduate award for American citizens to study at UK universities, highly selective (approximately 3.6–3.7% of applicants), comparable in selectivity to the Rhodes and Mitchell Scholarships, though slightly less than the Gates Cambridge Scholarship. Scholars can pursue any full-time postgraduate course (master’s, research, or PhD) at UK institutions, with a two-year tenure, and receive full tuition coverage and a quarterly stipend. It offers greater flexibility than the Rhodes or Fulbright programs—allowing study at any UK university and the option to change institutions annually. Traditionally, Marshall Scholars study at institutions like Oxford, Cambridge, LSE, and Imperial College London. In 2015, 69 scholars were in residence, with a majority in arts and social sciences. The **Association of Marshall Scholars (AMS)**, founded in 1988, supports scholars through an annual public opinion poll and hosts the **Marshall Forum** annually, featuring high-level U.S.-British leaders on transatlantic issues. Past forums have addressed topics such as innovation, global health, peace, and the legacy of the Marshall Plan, often coinciding with anniversaries of the Marshall Plan. Notable speakers include former U.S. officials, Supreme Court justices, and other Marshall Scholars. Notable Marshall Scholarship recipients include U.S. Supreme Court Justice Stephen Breyer, Nobel laureate Roger Tsien, diplomat Anthony Quainton, and economist James K. Galbraith, reflecting the award’s profound influence in shaping leaders across law, science, politics, journalism, and academia from the 1950s to the 2010s.  

The **Kennedy Scholarship**, established in 1964 following President John F. Kennedy's assassination, is a British memorial initiative comprising a scholarship to attend Harvard or MIT and a permanent memorial at Runnymede, England. Created through consultation with UK and US leaders and the Kennedy family, the scholarship aims to honor Kennedy’s legacy by fostering leadership and public service. Selected through a rigorous national competition, scholars are evaluated on intellectual ability, character, and potential for public impact. Notable alumni include prominent UK politicians, diplomats, and public servants. The memorial is managed by a UK-appointed board of trustees, with current members including former scholars and representatives from Harvard, MIT, and the US government. The program is considered comparable in prestige to the Rhodes and Marshall Scholarships. **The Kennedy Scholarship recognizes distinguished individuals in economics, journalism, arts, academia, and law**, with notable scholars including economists Ros Altmann, Mervyn King, and Sylvia Hewlett; journalists Zanny Minton Beddoes and Simon Kuper; artists Hannah Sullivan; academics Andrew Blake and Peter Hennessy; and legal figures Mary Arden and Nicholas Hamblen.  

The **Miss America** pageant, founded in 1921, has a rich history marked by milestones in diversity, activism, and cultural change. Early editions featured notable firsts, including Jewish (Bess Myerson, 1945), Latina (Irma Nydia Vasquez, 1948), and Asian-American (Yun Tau Chee, 1948) contestants. The 1968 protest by the New York Radical Women and others criticized the pageant as sexist, racist, and pro-militarist, leading to symbolic acts like crowning a sheep and burning feminist products. In the 1970s, Cheryl Browne became the first Black contestant, and Lencola Sullivan the first Black woman to place in the top five. Vanessa Williams (1984) became the first Black woman to win, though her reign was marred by controversy and threats. Later, the pageant saw more diversity: Sharlene Wells Hawkes (first foreign-born, bilingual), Heather Whitestone (first deaf), and Angela Perez Baraquio (first Asian-American and teacher). The pageant faced criticism for its image and policies, including a brief rule change allowing divorced or abortion-affected contestants, which was later reversed. In 2015, Vanessa Williams returned as a judge, with an apology issued by the organization for past treatment.  

From 2004 to 2014, the pageant saw shifts in broadcast networks, venue changes, and notable firsts—such as **Nina Davuluri**, the first Indian-American and second Asian-American winner, and **Alexis Wineman**, the first autistic contestant. In 2017, a major scandal erupted over derogatory emails, leading to resignations of top officials. In 2018, under new leadership, **Gretchen Carlson** launched the **"Miss America 2.0" era**, emphasizing inclusivity, removing appearance-based judging, and raising the titleholder age limit to 25. Tensions persisted, with former Miss Americas criticizing leadership, culminating in a petition for Carlson and CEO Regina Hopper’s resignations ahead of the 2019 pageant.  

**From 2018 to 2022**, the Miss America Organization (MAO) faced financial and leadership challenges, including a legal dispute with Fox Rothschild LLP, license terminations, and a lawsuit over a "bad-faith takeover." In 2019, Shantel Krebs became board chairwoman, and the pageant moved from Atlantic City to Connecticut, shifting to NBC and live streaming. The 2020 competition was postponed due to the pandemic, with Miss America 2022 held in December 2021. In 2023, under new CEO Robin Fleming, the organization reversed some 2018 reforms—restoring the term "contestant," reintroducing fitness-based judging, adding a "HER Story" talent category, and raising the age limit to 28. A 2023 A&E documentary, *Secrets of Miss America*, highlighted historical issues like body-shaming and racism. In November 2024, MAO filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, later seeking to dismiss it. Recent titleholders include Cassie Donegan (2026, NY), Abbie Stockard (2025, AL), and Madison Marsh (2024, CO).  

**Bright Futures Scholarship Program (Florida):**  
Provides merit-based scholarships in Florida based on academic performance and standardized test scores. Awards include:  
- **FAS (Academic Scholar):** Full tuition at public institutions; 100% at private institutions.  
- **FMS (Florida Merit Scholar):** 100% for associate degrees at public colleges; 75% elsewhere.  
- **GSV & GSC:** Per-credit hourly payments for technical and career programs; no summer use.  
- **ATS (Academic Top Scholar):** Additional credit-based payment on top of FAS.  
All students must maintain minimum GPAs (3.00 for FAS, 2.75 for others), complete required credit hours, and meet renewal conditions. Scholarships are renewable up to five years or for specified hours. Deferral up to 5 years is allowed; military enlistment extends deferral. FAS/FMS students may earn graduate study funding after completing a bachelor’s in ≤7 semesters.  

**Fulbright Program:**  
A U.S.-led international educational exchange program founded in 1946 to promote peace and mutual understanding through academic exchange. Operates in bilateral agreements with over 160 countries. Funds students and scholars to study, teach, or conduct research abroad. Key programs include the U.S. Student Program (for U.S. citizens) and the Fulbright Degree Program (for international students). In 2025, the program faced political interference and funding freezes, leading to resignations of 11 board members over ideological screening and political bias, raising concerns about its integrity and global credibility. Administered by the U.S. Department of State’s Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs and guided by the Fulbright Foreign Scholarship Board, the program promotes international educational exchange through student, scholar, teacher, and professional grants. Key components include the Fulbright Foreign Student Program (for overseas students studying in the U.S.), Fulbright U.S. Scholar Program (for American scholars abroad), Fulbright Teacher Exchange Program, and Fulbright–Hays Program (focusing on research and training in non-Western languages and area studies). The program is supported by 49 bilateral commissions, U.S. embassies, and partner organizations, including World Learning, American Councils, and the Academy for Educational Development. Independent organizations like the Fulbright Association and Fulbright Academy promote alumni engagement and professional development. The **J. William Fulbright Prize for International Understanding** honors individuals and organizations that advance cross-cultural understanding, with past recipients including Nelson Mandela, Kofi Annan, and Angela Merkel.  

**Fulbright Alumni Achievements:** The Fulbright Program has produced over 325,000 alumni who have achieved significant accomplishments in government, academia, and industry. Notable achievements include 89 Pulitzer Prize winners, 78 MacArthur Fellows, 62 Nobel laureates, 40 heads of state or government, 10 U.S. Congress members, and one United Nations Secretary-General. Prominent recipients such as Harold Bloom, Linus Pauling, Juan Manuel Santos, and Maria Ressa reflect the program’s profound global impact.  

The **National Merit Scholarship Program** identifies high-achieving students through the PSAT/NMSQT, with scores determining eligibility for Commended, Semifinalist, and Finalist status. Finalists are selected based on academic records, test scores, and essays, and approximately 8,000 receive Merit Scholarships, while 2,500 receive $2,500 awards based on state representation and performance. Corporate and college sponsors provide additional scholarships. The program highlights academic excellence with public recognition. Notable scholars include Bill Gates, Paul Krugman, and Jeff Bezos. Many universities offer preferential funding to Merit Scholars.  

The **National Achievement Scholarship Program**, established in 1964 to support Black American students, ended in 2015 and transitioned to the United Negro College Fund.  

The **Rhodes Scholarship**, established by Cecil Rhodes, is a prestigious postgraduate scholarship awarded to students from around the world to study at the University of Oxford. Originally limited to male students from specified schools, including four all-male South African institutions, it expanded globally in the 21st century, including to China and the UAE, with women admitted in 1977 following the Sex Discrimination Act of 1975. The scholarship emphasizes academic excellence, moral character, and leadership, with a global acceptance rate of 0.7%, making it one of the most competitive. It includes provisions for university fees, maintenance stipends, and access to Oxford’s residential colleges and Rhodes House. Despite its prestige, the scholarship faces ongoing criticism for its colonialist and white supremacist legacy, leading to campaigns for renaming, curriculum reform, and removal of statues. Concerns also persist over the disproportionate number of scholars entering business and law rather than public service, undermining its original mission to promote public service and unity among English-speaking peoples. The program remains active, with scholars selected from over 64 countries.  

**Rhodes Scholarship Alumni Impact:** The Rhodes Scholarship has produced a wide range of influential alumni across education, law, medicine, science, human rights, and the arts. While few scholars have "changed the world," most have built respected, impactful careers. Notable achievements include leadership roles in government (e.g., Bill Clinton, Bob Hawke), breakthroughs in science (e.g., penicillin, genome research), and advocacy for civil rights and social justice. Alumni like Bram Fischer, Nick Kristof, and Pardis Sabeti have advanced human rights and public health, while figures such as Atul Gawande and Brian Greene have contributed to medicine and cosmology. The scholarship's model has inspired similar programs globally, with close parallels to the Marshall and Gates Cambridge Scholarships, though distinctions in focus and eligibility remain historically notable.  

**United Negro College Fund (UNCF)** was founded in 1944 to address educational inequities for African Americans, uniting college leaders to raise funds for historically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs). While its scholarships are open to all ethnicities, most recipients are African American. UNCF has supported numerous prominent alumni in business, politics, health care, and the arts. It has raised significant funds through donations (e.g., John F. Kennedy, Walter Annenberg, Reed Hastings, Lilly Endowment) and events like the "Lou Rawls Parade of Stars," which raised over $200 million. The organization’s motto, *"A mind is a terrible thing to waste,"* is widely recognized, and it also promotes the message *"not asking for a handout, just a hand."* UNCF serves as a key supporter of HBCUs, with member institutions across the U.S. In 2025, MacKenzie Scott donated $70 million to enhance HBCU financial stability and long-term sustainability.  

**Member HBCUs:** The United Negro College Fund supports a network of Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) across the United States, including institutions such as Spelman College (Atlanta, GA; $390 million endowment), Xavier University of Louisiana (New Orleans, LA; $171 million endowment), and others. Most HBCUs are affiliated with Christian denominations, though some have no specific religious affiliation. Athletic programs at these institutions are primarily affiliated with NCAA, NAIA, or regional conferences, reflecting the diverse academic and extracurricular landscapes of the HBCU system.  

**Daughters of Africa**, edited by Margaret Busby, is a groundbreaking anthology featuring over 200 women of African descent, including renowned authors like Maya Angelou, Toni Morrison, Zora Neale Hurston, and Audre Lorde. Widely acclaimed for its comprehensive and diverse representation of black women’s writing across continents and centuries, it challenges misconceptions about the historical presence of black women in literature. It is praised for restoring marginalized voices to the literary spotlight, celebrating linguistic and cultural richness, and serving as an essential reference for students, scholars, and readers. Recognized as a landmark achievement, it has been included in numerous essential reading lists and remains a definitive guide to the work of African-descended women writers.  

**Margaret Busby’s *Daughters of Africa* (1992)** compiled writings by women of African descent and became a transformative influence on African and Black feminist literature, inspiring global editions and critical acclaim. In 2019, Busby published *New Daughters of Africa*, an expanded anthology featuring 200 writers, highlighting generational progress and diverse voices in Black women’s literary expression. The works have been celebrated for reasserting women’s roles in African narratives, empowering young Black women, and affirming a rich, global literary canon.  

**New Daughters of Africa** (2019), edited by Margaret Busby, is an international anthology of writing by women of African descent featuring over 200 contributors. Launched in London, it has been published in the UK, US, South Africa, and Germany, receiving widespread critical acclaim for its diverse, generational, and culturally rich content. The anthology was paired with the **Margaret Busby New Daughters of Africa Award**, established to support African women students, with recipients announced in 2020 and 2023. It was nominated for a 2020 NAACP Image Award and donated to schools and libraries to promote Black literature and education.  

The **Thurgood Marshall College Fund (TMCF)**, established in 1987, supports public HBCUs and PBIs through scholarships, capacity building, and entrepreneurship programs. It merged with the Opportunity Funding Corporation in 2013 and has raised over $500 million, supporting 55 institutions, including 42 HBCUs, 3 PBIs, and community colleges. TMCF differs from the UNCF by focusing specifically on public HBCUs and has received major donations from Apple, Koch Foundation, and Boeing.  
**The Thurgood Marshall College Fund currently lists 38 member institutions, primarily Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs), with a few Public Benefit Institutions (PBIs) and Historically Black Graduate Institutions (HBGI). Key institutions include Howard University, Tuskegee University, and North Carolina A&T State University. These institutions vary widely in endowment size—ranging from $1.47 million to over $178 million—and student populations from 1,241 to over 13,000. Many offer specialized law schools, such as the Thurgood Marshall School of Law and Howard University School of Law, reflecting a strong academic and athletic presence across the U.S.**  

**Harkness Fellowships**, established in 1925 by Edward Stephen Harkness and later renamed in 1961, are a prestigious international program supporting professionals in health care policy and practice. Since 1997, the program has focused exclusively on health care, offering one-year fellowships to individuals from Australia, Canada, France, Germany, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Switzerland, and the UK. Fellows conduct research at leading U.S. institutions and participate in seminars on healthcare policy, international policy, and U.S. government engagement. Administered by the Commonwealth Fund of New York City with support from international partners, the program fosters global collaboration in health policy. Notable alumni include prominent scholars, scientists, journalists, and public officials across diverse fields.  

**Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes** is a German foundation founded in 1948 in Cologne, reestablishing a scholarship program after being dissolved under National Socialism in 1934. It offers scholarships to undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students based on excellence, initiative, and responsibility, with no citizenship or age requirements. Admission occurs through nomination by schools, professors, examination boards, competitions, self-application via an assessment test, alumni, or artistic institutions. Scholarship holders receive financial support (e.g., €300 monthly allowance, need-based grants up to €855, child-related supplements, and travel support), academic support (summer schools, workshops, mentoring), and opportunities for research and events. A doctoral scholarship is available globally. In 2023, 2,179 undergraduate/graduate and 301 doctoral scholarships were awarded, with 55.4% of scholars being women. The scholarship share of German students remained stable at approximately 0.48% from 2013 onward.  

**Studienstiftung supports students across German universities, with a notable presence at public institutions like Heidelberg (657 scholars, 2.25% of students) and Freiburg (348 scholars, 1.44%), and at private institutions like Bucerius Law School (8.41% share). Key alumni include Nobel laureates in physics and chemistry, Fields Medalists, and prominent figures in politics, business, arts, and journalism.**  

The **Shuping Scholarship**, founded in 1939 by Koo Shuping, is a long-standing charitable initiative in China supporting students through financial aid, educational programs, and community services. Guided by values of filial piety, honesty, and tolerance, Koo personally funded the scholarship for decades. In 1995, he donated HK$10 million to the Shanghai Education Development Foundation and established the Shuping Scholarship Foundation in Hong Kong to ensure its long-term sustainability. Key milestones include the expansion to 33 secondary schools and 24 tertiary institutions by 1998, the formation of scholar-led associations, and the establishment of support services like medical aid and vocational training. After his death in 1998, George Koo succeeded him as chairman. The foundation continues to honor his legacy through scholarships, exhibitions, and support for disadvantaged students.
