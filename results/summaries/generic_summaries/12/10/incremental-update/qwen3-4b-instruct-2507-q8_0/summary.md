## Thurgood Marshall College Fund (TMCF) and United Negro College Fund (UNCF)

The Thurgood Marshall College Fund (TMCF), established in 1987 and renamed in 2006, supports public historically black colleges and universities (HBCUs), having raised over $500 million for scholarships, programmatic support, and capacity building. It serves 48 member institutions—42 HBCUs, 3 PBIs, and 3 HBGI schools—distinct from the UNCF, which focuses on scholarships for African American students at HBCUs. TMCF expanded in 2013 with the acquisition of the Opportunity Funding Corporation (OFC), enhancing talent identification and entrepreneurship initiatives. Key leaders include Harry L. Williams (since 2018), Johnny C. Taylor Jr. (2010–2018), and Dwayne Ashley (1999–2010), founder of the Leadership Institute. Major donors include Apple, the Charles Koch Foundation, and Boeing. Notable institutions include Howard University (largest endowment: $712.4M), Tuskegee University (largest student body: 2,877), and North Carolina A&T (largest enrollment: 13,322). Prominent law schools include Howard University School of Law, Southern University Law Center, and Thurgood Marshall School of Law. Most HBCUs compete in NCAA or NAIA athletics, primarily in conferences such as SWAC, MEAC, SIAC, and CIAA. Religious affiliations are largely with the United Methodist Church or United Church of Christ.

The United Negro College Fund (UNCF), founded in 1944, provides scholarships to African American students at HBCUs, with a majority of recipients being Black. It has raised significant funds through major donations—including from John F. Kennedy, Walter Annenberg, Reed Hastings ($120M), and the Lilly Endowment ($100M in 2024)—and through events like the "Lou Rawls Parade of Stars," which raised over $200M. Its motto, “A mind is a terrible thing to waste,” and message of “not asking for a handout, just a hand” remain influential. In 2025, MacKenzie Scott donated $70M to support HBCU financial stability and sustainability. Member institutions span the U.S., including in Alabama, Georgia, and Texas.

## International Scholarships and Academic Exchange Programs

The *Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes* provides German scholarships for undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students based on excellence, initiative, and responsibility, with no citizenship restrictions. Scholars receive financial support (€300 monthly allowance, up to €855 need-based, €1,550–1,650 monthly stipend for PhDs), academic support (summer schools, mentoring), and research opportunities. In 2023, 2,179 undergraduates and 301 PhDs were awarded, with 93.5% of funding requests approved. Notable alumni include Fields Medalists Peter Scholze and Gerd Faltings. The foundation has strong ties to institutions like Heidelberg (657 scholars, 2.25% of students) and Bucerius Law School (8.41% share).

The *Bolashak* scholarship, established in 1993 by Kazakhstan’s President Nazarbayev, supports Kazakh students studying abroad. Over 5,950 students have been selected since 2006, with 93.6% being of Kazakh ethnicity. As of 2023, eligible universities span 32 countries, including top institutions in the U.S., UK, Germany, and Japan. Around 2,000 students are employed in government or national enterprises, and 800 returned to Kazakhstan in 2009, reinforcing national development.

The *Shuping Scholarship*, founded in 1939 by Koo Shuping, supports students in Shanghai, Beijing, and Huzhou. Guided by filial piety, honesty, and tolerance, it provides full tuition, extracurriculars (including a student-run library, free medical care), and volunteer service. Over 6,000 scholars have been supported by 2000, with over 700 awarded annually across 37 secondary and 24 tertiary institutions. The Shuping Scholarship Foundation (established in 1995) ensures long-term sustainability through alumni governance.

The **National Merit Scholarship Program** identifies approximately 8,000 finalists annually, with 2,500 receiving $2,500 awards based on academic performance, extracurriculars, and college potential. Awards are funded by the National Merit Scholarship Corporation (NMSC) and corporate sponsors, with some tied to employee families or geographic regions. Notable scholars include Bill Gates, Paul Krugman, and Jeff Bezos. The program faced criticism in Florida for racial and economic disparities, leading to funding cuts and reforms in 2014–2018. The National Achievement Scholarship Program (1964–2015) supported Black American youth and transitioned to UNCF.

The **Bright Futures Scholarship Program (Florida)** offers four levels—FAS, FMS, GSV, GSC—with varying award amounts and academic requirements. It was initially modeled after Georgia’s HOPE Scholarship, providing merit-based support to over 39% of graduates. After criticism over equity, funding was reduced in 2011, and score thresholds were raised. A 2018 overhaul restored full tuition coverage for top scholars and introduced vocational pathways. As of 2025, it remains merit-based with strict academic, test, and service-hour requirements.

The **Margaret Busby New Daughters of Africa Award** (2020) supports African women students, linked to the international anthology *New Daughters of Africa*, which features over 200 writings by women of African descent. The anthology received critical acclaim and was nominated for a 2020 NAACP Image Award. It has empowered young Black women and influenced major writers like Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie and Candice Carty-Williams.

The **Kennedy Scholarship** selects around 600 British postgraduate students annually to study at Harvard or MIT, emphasizing public service and intellectual attainment. Notable recipients include Lady Arden and Lord Hamblen in law.

The **Rhodes Scholarship**, founded in 1902, promotes global diplomacy and academic excellence with a global acceptance rate of 0.7%. It expanded globally, including to China and the UAE, and now includes over 20 constituencies across 64 countries. Women now constitute about 35% of U.S. scholars. It has faced criticism over its colonial legacy and racial exclusion, particularly in South Africa, where Black scholars were only selected in 1991. Despite this, it has produced influential leaders in politics (e.g., Bill Clinton, Bob Hawke), medicine (e.g., Howard Florey), law (e.g., Bram Fischer), and human rights (e.g., Edwin Cameron). It has inspired similar programs like the Marshall, Gates Cambridge, and Schwarzman Scholarships.

The **Norway Scholarship** enables Norwegian students to study at Wadham College, Oxford. Inspired by World War I, it was revived in 1979 and has supported diverse disciplines. Notable scholars include Alf Bøe, Andor Birkeland, and King Harald V.

Historical **King’s Scholar** traditions exist in UK institutions: Eton (70 scholars annually, notable alumni include Orwell, Keynes, Boris Johnson), Westminster (selected via "The Challenge"), King’s College Cambridge (first-class degree recipients), and the seven King’s Schools (e.g., King’s Ely), where scholars enjoy lifelong cathedral membership, marriage, and burial rights. Scholar titles are gender-specific: male scholars are King’s Scholars; female scholars are Queen’s Scholars.

The **Fulbright Program**, founded in 1946, promotes international understanding through academic and cultural exchange. Administered via 49 bilateral commissions in over 160 countries, it supports students, scholars, teachers, and professionals. In 2024–2025, it faced disruption due to U.S. funding freezes and political interference allegations, leading to 11 of 12 Fulbright Board members resigning. Despite challenges, over 325,000 alumni have contributed to global peace and public service. The **J. William Fulbright Prize for International Understanding** honors individuals like Nelson Mandela, Kofi Annan, and Angela Merkel.

The **Edmund S. Muskie Internship Program** (1992) supports Eurasian graduate students and scholars with internships and career training in the U.S., continuing Muskie’s legacy of international engagement and public service.

The **Miss America Pageant**, founded in 1921, evolved from a sexist and exclusionary institution to a more inclusive platform. Key milestones include Bess Myerson (first Jewish-American winner), Vanessa Williams (first Black woman winner), and Nina Davuluri (first Indian-American winner). From 1968 to 2022, it faced protests, scandals, and governance issues. In 2018, under Gretchen Carlson, it rebranded as "Miss America 2.0," eliminating appearance-based judging, raising the age limit to 25, and introducing the "HER Story" category. A 2023 A&E documentary exposed historical issues like body-shaming and racism. The pageant filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2024 and later sought dismissal.

## Marshall Scholarships (Updated)

The Marshall Scholarship, established in 1953 as a memorial to George C. Marshall, enables outstanding U.S. citizens to pursue full-time postgraduate studies (master’s, research, or PhD) at any UK university—excluding pre-professional programs—over a two-year period. It covers tuition and provides a quarterly stipend. The scholarship is highly selective (3.6–3.7% of applicants), more flexible than the Rhodes Scholarship (allowing study at any UK institution and annual university changes), and emphasizes academic excellence and leadership. Recipients are typically from top U.S. universities such as Harvard, Princeton, and Stanford. Notable recipients include U.S. Supreme Court Justices Stephen Breyer and Neil Gorsuch, Nobel laureate John M. Jumper, influential economists, scientists, and public servants. The scholarship has produced leaders across law, politics, science, journalism, and the arts. The **Association of Marshall Scholars (AMS)**, founded in 1988, hosts an annual Marshall Forum on transatlantic issues, featuring prominent leaders and scholars, and conducts public opinion polls on UK perceptions. The program continues to strengthen U.S.-UK academic and diplomatic ties through leadership development and international exchange.
