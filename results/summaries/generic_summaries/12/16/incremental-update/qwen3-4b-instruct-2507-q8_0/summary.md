- The Norway Scholarship, established in 1919 by Arthur Ivor Garland Jayne, enables Norwegian students to study at Wadham College, Oxford, fostering cultural ties between Norway and Britain through academic exchange. Initially funded with NOK 60,000, funding was later revived in the late 1970s with NOK 1.17 million. Managed by figures such as Alf Bøe and Bjørn Blindheim, it supports students in diverse disciplines—including law, literature, medicine, and international relations—and is annually supported by an Oxford guest, often King Harald V.

- King’s Scholars is a prestigious academic designation with roots in 1317 at Cambridge and expanded through Eton (1440) and the seven King’s Schools. Selected through competitive exams, scholars receive fee waivers and participate in exclusive traditions. At Eton, about 70 scholars are admitted annually; they live in the central building, wear black gowns, and engage in ceremonies. Notable alumni include George Orwell, John Maynard Keynes, and Boris Johnson. At King’s College Cambridge, first-class degree holders receive the title and a £350 prize. The seven King’s Schools—Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester—were re-founded by Henry VIII, now awarding scholarships based on academic or musical excellence (with girls admitted since 1973). Scholars at Ely and Worcester gain lifelong cathedral membership, including the right to marry or be buried there, and participate in traditions such as the "Hoop Trundle" and red gowns during services.

- The Kennedy Scholarship, established in 1964 following JFK’s assassination, offers UK students a place at Harvard or MIT. Funded by a memorial at Runnymede, it evaluates intellectual ability, public service potential, and resilience. Overseen by trustees including former scholars, it has produced notable recipients in economics, journalism, arts, and law—such as Ros Altmann and Lord Eatwell. The Edmund S. Muskie Internship Program (established in 1992) provides insight into Muskie’s life, including his immigrant background, academic achievements, early public service, WWII Navy Reserve service, and marriage to Jane Gray. Muskie, a U.S. Senator from Maine (1959–1980), was a leading architect of U.S. environmental policy, co-authoring the Clean Air Act (1970) and Clean Water Acts (1972, 1977), and championed New Federalism and the Congressional Budget Office. A vice-presidential candidate in 1968 and 1972, he faced political setbacks including the forged "Canuck letter" and Watergate attacks. As U.S. Secretary of State (1980–1981), he opposed Soviet expansion, advocated for arms control, and led diplomatic efforts during the Iran hostage crisis. He remains remembered as a pivotal figure in environmental policy, civil rights, and diplomacy, with numerous institutions—such as Muskie Day in Maine, the Muskie School of Public Service, and the Muskie Pro Bono Award—bearing his name.

- The Bright Futures Scholarship in Florida offers four levels—FAS, FMS, GSV, GSC—and an Academic Top Scholar (ATS) award. FAS covers 100% of tuition and a $300 book stipend (until 2021), while FMS covers 75%. In 2021, the stipend was removed and SAT/ACT thresholds raised (later reduced), with CLT scores now accepted (95 for FAS, 82 for FMS) from 2025. Renewal rules allow movement between levels based on GPA, with one-year deferrals for illness or emergency. Awards range from $39–$49 per credit (semester) for GSV/GSC and additional $44–$29 per credit for ATS. Benefits include up to five-year deferral, military service deferral, and eligibility for graduate study after a bachelor’s in ≤7 semesters.

- The Marshall Scholarship, founded in 1953 as a memorial to George C. Marshall, enables intellectually distinguished U.S. students to study in the UK. It expanded to 51 scholars in 2024 (largest cohort), with a selection rate of 3.4–3.66% of endorsed applicants. Emphasis is on academic excellence (minimum GPA 3.7), leadership, and potential to contribute to UK university life. Scholars study at any UK university (excluding pre-professional programs), receive a maintenance stipend, and are more likely to pursue academic or research careers. Alumni include Supreme Court Justice Stephen Breyer, Nobel laureate Roger Tsien, and diplomat Kim Campbell. The Association of Marshall Scholars hosts the Marshall Forum on transatlantic issues, with notable participants like Madeleine Albright and Reid Hoffman.

- The National Merit Scholarship Program identifies top U.S. high school students via the PSAT/NMSQT, awarding scholarships based on academic performance, extracurriculars, and recommendations. About 8,000 Finalists receive up to $2,500. Critics argue the program is unfair and lacks transparency due to its reliance on PSAT scores and exclusion of 99% of test-takers. The National Achievement Scholarship Program (for Black American students) ended in 2015 and transitioned to UNCF. Notable alumni include Bill Gates, Paul Krugman, and Steve Ballmer.

- The Miss America Pageant, founded in 1920, evolved into a major cultural event with milestones in diversity: first African-American contestant in 1970 (Cheryl Browne), first Black winner in 1984 (Vanessa Williams), first Indian-American in 2013 (Nina Davuluri), and first deaf (Heather Whitestone), first with diabetes (Nicole Johnson), and first Asian-American teacher (Angela Perez Baraquio). In 2018, "Miss America 2.0" was launched, removing swimsuit and evening gown competitions, emphasizing personal style and scholarship, and raising the age limit to 25. The organization faced criticism over scholarship claims, misrepresentation of funds (exposed by John Oliver in 2014), and internal conflicts, including a 2018 petition by Cara Mund, CEO resignations (Sam Haskell, 2015), and a 2023 documentary exposing body-shaming and racism. In 2024, it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, later seeking dismissal. Recent titleholders include Cassie Donegan (2026), Abbie Stockard (2025), and Madison Marsh (2024).

- The Bolashak Scholarship, established in 1993 by Kazakhstan’s Nursultan Nazarbayev, sends Kazakh youth abroad for education to promote Western democratic values. Over 12,800 have been awarded, with many in government and private sectors. Ethnic distribution is heavily Kazakh (93.6%) due to language requirements. Recipients study at 630 leading universities across 32 countries.

- The Fulbright Program, founded in 1946, promotes mutual understanding through educational exchange between the U.S. and other countries. Administered by the U.S. Department of State and Education, it includes U.S. and foreign student, scholar, and research programs. Despite political interference under the Trump administration (2024–2025), including funding freezes and ideological screening, its mission of global cultural exchange remains central. The J. William Fulbright Prize for International Understanding, first awarded to Nelson Mandela, honors global leaders and scholars. Notable alumni include Nobel and Pulitzer laureates, and leaders in science, arts, and diplomacy.

- The United Negro College Fund (UNCF), founded in 1944, supports historically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs), focusing on educational equity. It has raised over $200 million through major donations (e.g., Reed Hastings, Lilly Endowment) and events like the "Lou Rawls Parade of Stars." In 2025, MacKenzie Scott donated $70 million to strengthen HBCU sustainability. Key HBCUs include Spelman College (largest endowment: $390 million) and Xavier University of Louisiana ($171 million). Most are Christian-affiliated, with athletic programs in NCAA, NAIA, and regional conferences.

- The Thurgood Marshall College Fund (TMCF), founded in 1987, supports 55 public HBCUs and PBIs with scholarships and capacity-building. It raised over $500 million, including $50M from Apple, $25.6M from Koch Foundation, and $6M from Boeing. It supports 48 institutions across 23 U.S. states, including Howard and Thurgood Marshall Law Schools. Endowments range from $1.4M to $178M (North Carolina A&T), with enrollment from 1,241 to 13,322. Most compete in NCAA Division I or II, with affiliations in SWAC, MEAC, SIAC, CIAA, and MIAA.

- The Rhodes Scholarship, established by Cecil Rhodes, originally limited to male students from Commonwealth countries. Opened to women in 1977 and expanded globally, including to China and UAE. Criticized for its colonialist legacy and exclusion of Black Africans, especially in South Africa. Despite these concerns, it remains highly selective (0.7% acceptance rate) and has produced influential figures such as Bill Clinton, Bob Hawke, Malcolm Turnbull, Howard Florey, and Pardis Sabeti. Recent recipients include the first transgender woman, Hera Jay Brown (2019), and non-binary scholars (2020). The program continues to support public service, human rights, and social justice.

- The Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes, founded in 1948 in Cologne, provides scholarships for undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students in Germany, EU, or Switzerland based on excellence, initiative, and responsibility. Applicants can be nominated by schools or professors. Support includes a €300 monthly allowance, need-based scholarships (up to €855/month), and research funds. It awards approximately 0.48% of Germany’s university students, with notable representation at institutions like Heidelberg (657 scholars) and Bucerius Law School (8.41%). Alumni include Nobel laureates and leaders in science, social sciences, business, politics, and arts. The *Daughters of Africa* (Margaret Busby) and *New Daughters of Africa* (2019) are landmark anthologies celebrating African women’s writing, dismantling myths of literary absence and affirming global black feminist heritage.

- The Harkness Fellowship, initiated in 1925 by Edward Stephen Harkness, enables British and Commonwealth graduates to study in the U.S., initially in business and civil service, and since 1997 has focused exclusively on health care. Fellows conduct 12-month research at leading U.S. institutions and attend seminars on healthcare policy. Funded by the Commonwealth Fund and supported by international partners, it is a prestigious program in health policy, with notable alumni in academia, science, journalism, and public service.
