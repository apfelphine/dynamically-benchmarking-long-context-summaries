### **The Miss America Pageant and Elite Scholarships: Historical Inequities, Modern Reforms, and Cultural Representation**

The **Miss America Pageant**, founded in **1919**, has long been a symbol of systemic inequities in **race, gender, and cultural representation**, despite early progress like **Bess Myerson (1945)** and **Yun Tau Chee (1948)**. Feminist movements in the **1960s–70s** exposed persistent discrimination—**Cheryl Browne’s (1970) resignation**, **Vanessa Williams’s (2014) controversies**, and **Nina Davuluri’s (2014) online racism**—while **Erin O’Flaherty’s (2016) status as the first openly lesbian winner** highlighted ongoing barriers. Inclusivity efforts expanded with bilingual winners (e.g., **Sharlene Wells Hawkes, 1984**) and categories like **"HER Story"**, though scholarship disparities remain. Recent reforms under **Miss America 2.0 (2017–2022)** and **Robin Fleming’s 2023–2024 iteration** introduced **talent-based judging, expanded scholarships, and raised the age limit to 28**, replacing swimsuit judging with fitness modeling.

### **Elite Scholarships: Colonial Legacy, Global Expansion, and Interdisciplinary Impact**
Scholarships tied to the **Miss America Pageant** contrast with **elite programs like the Rhodes Scholarship (1902)**, which, despite modern inclusivity (e.g., **transgender and non-binary scholars since 2018**), retains **colonial-era biases**—originally excluding Black Africans until the **1990s** and maintaining a **~0.7% acceptance rate**. Meanwhile, the **Shuping Scholarship (1939–1986)**, founded by **Koo Soh Bing**, offered **holistic support** (tuition, housing, vocational training) to **1,100+ students** during WWII, revived in **1986** with **~700 annual awards** and **HK$10 million in funding** (1995), supported by the **Shuping Scholarship Foundation**. Other programs, such as the **Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes (1925/1948)**, support **exceptional German students** through **financial aid and PhD funding**, with **55.4% female scholars (2020)**.

The **Harkness Fellowship (1925)**, originally supporting **British and Commonwealth students**, evolved into **12-month research fellowships in U.S. healthcare institutions**, fostering **transatlantic collaboration** in policy and global research. Funded by the **Commonwealth Fund** and international foundations, it has produced **healthcare leaders, scientists, and politicians** (e.g., **Sir Ronald Calne, Dame Rosalyn Higgins**). While rooted in **Royal Society and British Academy ties**, its scope has expanded to include **diverse academic fields**, with alumni like **Sir Graeme Catto, Derek Parfit, and Jonathan Wolff** shaping interdisciplinary scholarship.

### **Literary and Scholarship Initiatives: Empowering Global Voices**
Margaret Busby’s *Daughters of Africa* (1983/1992) remains a **landmark anthology** of **200+ African women writers**, dismantling literary erasure and inspiring **global solidarity**. Its **feminist, multilingual, and historically rich** contributions expanded into *Töchter Afrikas* (1994) and *New Daughters of Africa* (2019), a **200-writer expansion** (19th–21st centuries) funding **UK/Ghana school distributions (2020–2021)** via *The Black Curriculum*. Recognized for its impact, it was **nominated for the NAACP Image Award (2020)** and translated into **German (2023)**. Busby’s **New Daughters of Africa Award** supports **African women students**, while the **Shuping Scholarship** exemplifies **holistic philanthropy**, aligning with Busby’s emphasis on **literary and educational empowerment**. Together, these initiatives reflect a **cultural shift toward inclusive global scholarship**, though systemic barriers persist in programs like the **Rhodes Scholarship**. The **Harkness Fellowship’s legacy** bridges **healthcare policy and transatlantic research**, underscoring its role in **interdisciplinary and global exchange**.
