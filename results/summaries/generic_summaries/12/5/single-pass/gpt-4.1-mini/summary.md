# Rhodes Scholarship
Founded in 1902 by Cecil Rhodes’ will, the Rhodes Scholarship was established to promote unity within the British Empire and foster Anglo-American diplomatic ties, awarding postgraduate scholarships primarily at Oxford University. Despite Rhodes’ controversial beliefs, the scholarship does not discriminate based on race or religion and is administered by the Rhodes Trust in Oxford. Over the 20th and 21st centuries, the program expanded globally, introduced gender inclusivity in 1977, and recently opened to postgraduate applicants worldwide. It remains highly selective, with rigorous criteria including academic excellence, leadership, and moral character.

The scholarship offers full university fees and stipends for two years of study in any full-time postgraduate course at Oxford. Its distribution has broadened geographically and demographically, though it faced historical exclusions, such as barring German and Black African applicants. Controversies include Rhodes’s colonial legacy, initial exclusion of women and Black Africans, and criticisms of alumni career paths diverging from public service ideals. Notable scholars have contributed significantly in politics, human rights, medicine, science, arts, and social justice.

# Margaret Busby’s *Daughters of Africa* Anthology and Award
*Daughters of Africa* (1992), edited by Margaret Busby, is a landmark international anthology compiling writings by over 200 women of African descent spanning centuries. Highly praised for its breadth and literary quality, it has been influential in highlighting black women’s literary contributions globally. The anthology inspired a 2019 sequel, *New Daughters of Africa*, extending the literary canon with contemporary voices. The companion Margaret Busby New Daughters of Africa Award supports African women students, with donations from Busby, contributors, and partners like SOAS, University of London. Both anthologies are noted for fostering black feminist literary traditions and education.

# British Council
Established in 1934 to promote British culture and education globally, the British Council operates in over 100 countries as a UK government-sponsored charity. It offers English teaching, administers exams like IELTS, facilitates international educational exchanges, and supports cultural programs including arts leadership and LGBT initiatives. Historically, it faced challenges including office closures in Russia and Syria, attacks in conflict regions, and controversy over political neutrality, outsourcing, and staff treatment. The British Council works extensively through partnerships, organizes mobility and school programs, and contributes to global cultural diplomacy despite ongoing scrutiny regarding financial management, political influence, and operational focus.

# Harkness Fellowship
Founded in 1925 by the Commonwealth Fund to reciprocate Rhodes Scholarships, the Harkness Fellowships support mid-career international professionals—now focused on health care policy—to study or conduct research in the United States for up to 21 months. The program offers seminars and placements at prestigious American institutions and is funded primarily by the Commonwealth Fund with additional support from various foundations worldwide. Notable alumni span diverse fields including academia, government, science, and the arts.

# Fulbright Program
Established in 1946 by Senator J. William Fulbright to promote international peace and understanding through educational exchange, the Fulbright Program is the largest global scholarship and exchange initiative. It funds study, research, teaching, and training across nearly all academic disciplines between the U.S. and partner countries. The program operates bi-nationally with commissions managing exchanges, supported by the U.S. Department of State and cooperating organizations. Despite interruptions due to geopolitical conflicts, notably Russian sanctions and U.S. funding freezes, the program remains prestigious, producing numerous Nobel laureates, Pulitzer Prize winners, and heads of state. Its scholarships cover students, scholars, teachers, and professionals in a variety of specialized categories.

# Shuping Scholarship
Founded in 1939 in Shanghai by businessman Koo Soh Bing in memory of his father, the Shuping Scholarship supports academically talented youth from poor families, providing tuition, extracurricular activities, medical care, and a community center. Despite challenges during WWII and socio-political upheavals, it awarded over 1,100 scholarships in its first decade and resumed operations nationwide in 1986 after China’s reforms. The scholarship promotes moral education and social responsibility, managed by alumni associations and sustained through Koo’s philanthropic foundation and endowments.

# Miss America
The Miss America Pageant began officially in 1921 in Atlantic City as a beauty contest aiming to boost tourism. Over time, it evolved to include talent and scholarship components and faced historical racial and ethnic exclusions, with the first African-American contestant participating in 1970 and the first African-American winner in 1983. The pageant has undergone numerous social and organizational challenges, including feminist protests, racial controversies, and leadership scandals, particularly during the 2017–2018 email controversy. Recent reforms under "Miss America 2.0" removed judging by physical appearance; however, these changes have partially reversed since 2023. The pageant continues to award scholarships, emphasizing talent and personal achievements alongside changes in format and inclusivity.

# Studienstiftung (German Academic Scholarship Foundation)
Established initially in 1925 and re-founded in 1948, the Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes is Germany’s largest and oldest scholarship foundation focused on academic excellence, initiative, and social responsibility. Admission is competitive via nomination from schools, professors, competitions, or self-assessment tests. It supports undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students with financial aid and extensive academic enrichment programs like summer schools and mentoring. Its scholars represent a small, highly talented fraction of German students and include many prominent scientists, economists, politicians, artists, and journalists.

# Marshall Scholarship
Created in 1953 as a living memorial to George C. Marshall, the Marshall Scholarship supports American postgraduate study in the UK to strengthen US-UK ties and prepare future leaders. Unlike the Rhodes, it admits women and married men, offers flexibility to study at any UK university, and consists of rigorous selection emphasizing academic merit and leadership potential. Annually, about 40–50 scholars are chosen from over 900 endorsed applicants. Scholars typically study at prestigious UK institutions, especially Oxford and Cambridge. The Marshall Scholarship is comparable in selectivity and prestige to Rhodes and Fulbright scholarships and features a robust alumni association fostering transatlantic dialogue.

# Thurgood Marshall College Fund (TMCF)
Founded in 1987 to support publicly funded Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs), the TMCF has grown into a nonprofit championing higher education and scholarships for 55 member institutions, including HBCUs, predominantly Black institutions, and technical colleges. Differing from the United Negro College Fund, TMCF focuses on public institutions and has raised over $500 million for programming and scholarships. It has received significant philanthropic donations from major corporations and has expanded through acquisitions like the Opportunity Funding Corporation to support entrepreneurship and talent development within the HBCU community.

# Edmund S. Muskie Internship Program
Named after Edmund Muskie (1914–1996), a prominent American statesman, governor, senator, U.S. Secretary of State, and vice-presidential candidate of Polish descent, this program is a U.S. Department of State–supported exchange offering summer internships and career training to Eurasian scholars and graduate students studying in the U.S. Muskie’s legacy includes environmental legislation (Clean Air and Water Acts), civil rights advocacy, and diplomatic service. The program reflects his commitment to education, public service, and international cooperation.

# UNCF (United Negro College Fund)
Founded in 1944 to address educational inequities for African Americans, the UNCF is a nonprofit coalition supporting Black students and Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs). It provides scholarships widely accessible to all ethnicities but predominantly awarded to African Americans. The UNCF has a history of high-profile fundraising campaigns, including the Lou Rawls telethon, and has received substantial donations from individuals and corporations. Its motto, "A mind is a terrible thing to waste," is widely recognized. The fund continues to support numerous HBCUs and provides pathways to success for Black professionals across diverse sectors.

# King's Scholar
Originating in 1317 at Cambridge and formalized at Eton College in 1440 by Henry VI, King's Scholars are students awarded prestigious scholarships marked by academic distinction. At Eton, King's Scholars live in the historic College house, don unique gowns, and partake in traditional privileges and education. Similar scholarship traditions exist at Westminster School, King’s College Cambridge, and several historic King's Schools in the UK, including King's Ely and King's School Canterbury. These scholarships recognize high academic or musical merit and confer lifelong affiliations and ceremonial honors.

# Bolashak Scholarship (Kazakhstan)
Established in 1993 by Kazakhstan’s first president Nursultan Nazarbayev, the Bolashak Scholarship funds Kazakh students to study abroad in leading universities worldwide to acquire Western education and values. It has granted over 12,800 scholarships, mainly to ethnic Kazakhs proficient in the Kazakh language, despite the country’s diverse ethnic composition. The program emphasizes training future specialists who often assume significant roles in Kazakhstan’s public and private sectors. Eligible universities span Australia, Europe, North America, and Asia.

# Norway Scholarship
Initiated in 1919 to enable Norwegian students to study at Oxford University’s Wadham College, the Norway Scholarship promotes cultural exchange and closer ties between Norway and the UK. Though financial limitations affected its continuity, renewed efforts in the late 20th century restored the scholarship’s funding and prestige. The scholarship supports varied academic disciplines, and annual gatherings and lectures foster community among Norwegian recipients, including notable alumni and the participation of Norway’s royal family.

# National Merit Scholarship Program
A U.S. academic competition using the PSAT/NMSQT as an initial qualifier for recognition and scholarships, the National Merit Scholarship Corporation annually awards scholarships to high-achieving high school juniors based on standardized test scores, academic records, and leadership. Finalists and Semifinalists receive scholarships varying from $2,500 to college-specific awards. The program maintains a significant presence in college recruiting but has faced criticism for over-reliance on test scores and lack of transparency. The National Achievement Scholarship Program specifically supported Black American students until 2015, when its funds transitioned to graduate awards.

# Kennedy Scholarship
Established in 1964 as a British memorial to U.S. President John F. Kennedy, the Kennedy Scholarship funds British postgraduate study at Harvard or MIT. Selection emphasizes intellectual attainment, communication skills, public service potential, and originality, resembling Rhodes and Marshall Scholarship standards. Trustees include notable academics and diplomats, with strong Kennedy family support. Scholars have been influential in politics, academia, economics, journalism, and the arts.

# Bright Futures Scholarship Program (Florida, USA)
Modeled after Georgia’s HOPE scholarship, Florida’s Bright Futures Program awards merit-based scholarships to Florida high school graduates meeting academic and service criteria. Since its 1997 inception, it has undergone funding cuts and eligibility changes that reduced coverage and increased qualification thresholds, disproportionately impacting minority and low-income students. The program supports four scholarship tiers applicable to academic or vocational studies, renewable with GPA and credit requirements. Awards cover tuition and fees with varying percentages, and recent reforms include acceptance of alternative standardized tests like the CLT.
