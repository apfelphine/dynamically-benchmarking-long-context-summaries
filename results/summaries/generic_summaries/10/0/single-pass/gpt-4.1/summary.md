# Education Systems: Concise Summary

---

## Sweden

**Structure:** Education is organized into preschool (Förskola), compulsory comprehensive schooling (Grundskola, ages 6–16), upper secondary (Gymnasieskola, elective, 99% enrollment, ages 16–19), and higher education (universities/university colleges, Bologna-compatible degree cycles). There are also independent schools (friskolor) publicly funded but privately run.

**Recent History & Developments:** Major expansions occurred post-1947, including universalization of the gymnasium and strong emphasis on education for economic growth. Primary education has evolved with successive reforms affecting curriculum and grading. Upper secondary now comprises preparatory and vocational tracks, all qualifying for higher education.

**Grading & Assessments:** Compulsory school grades range from A–F, with national tests administered in grades 3, 6, and 9; recent years have seen challenges with cheating and inconsistent test results.

**Higher Education:** Split into bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral cycles. Admissions require proficiency in Swedish and English and, for some, the Swedish Scholastic Aptitude Test. Student aid is available, and tuition is free for EU/EEA/Swiss citizens.

**Independent Schools:** Friskolor have grown to serve 15% of compulsory and 30% of upper secondary students, but profit incentives and grade inflation have raised controversy.

**Performance:** Sweden improved its PISA scores in 2015 after years of decline; immigrant influx impacts results but does not fully explain previous score drops.

## Hong Kong

**Structure:** Children attend non-free (voucher-subsidized) preschool, followed by six years of free compulsory primary education and six years of secondary. Secondary is split into junior (general curriculum) and senior (HKDSE/IBDP preparatory). Medium of instruction varies: Cantonese, Putonghua, and English.

**Tertiary & Higher Education:** Highly competitive admission through the JUPAS system; universities primarily use English. There are eight main UGC-funded universities, alongside numerous self-financing colleges.

**International & Special Education:** Over 100 international schools serve mostly local students; integration and induction programs target immigrant and non-Cantonese speakers. Special education is moving toward integration, except for severe disabilities.

**Recent Trends:** Since 2020, increased emigration led to student and staff departures. Political developments have influenced curriculum, emphasizing national security and patriotism. AI tools and Greater Bay Area educational collaborations are expanding. Class sizes decreasing owing to lower birth rates; “spoon-fed” culture and heavy exam focus are commonly cited.

## Norway

**Structure:** Education consists of compulsory elementary (barneskole, ages 6–12), lower secondary (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16), and elective upper secondary (videregående skole, ages 16–19). Most schooling is public and free.

**Grading & Examinations:** No official grades in elementary; grading starts in lower secondary. National exams are administered in upper secondary, determining eligibility for higher education.

**Higher Education:** State-run universities and university colleges, with bachelor’s, master’s, and PhD degrees. Admission largely depends on GPA and subject electives; vocational and academic tracks merged since 1994. About 73% upper secondary graduation rate.

**Special Education:** Inclusive education is mandated, with special education mainly integrated into ordinary schools.

**Academic Calendar:** Two-semester system, mid-August to June, with multiple vacation periods.

## Ghana

**Structure:** Consists of free and compulsory basic education (4–15 years, kindergarten through junior high), secondary (senior high, academic/vocational/technical), and tertiary (universities, polytechnics, colleges).

**History & Policy:** Mission schools dominated until independence in 1957, followed by rapid expansion and several reforms. Recent decades feature increased access but persistent quality and equity challenges.

**Grading:** Numeric for basic/secondary; WASSCE letter grades for senior high; tertiary institutions utilize GPA systems.

**Challenges & Governance:** Teacher shortages, financing struggles, and equity gaps (gender, geography, wealth) persist. Multiple agencies oversee policy and examinations; private-public partnerships fill gaps.

**Technical/Vocational Education:** Still largely informal; formal TVET faces completion and reputation issues.

**ICT & Literacy:** ICT integration and literacy campaigns ongoing; literacy rates improved but disparities remain.

## Armenia

**Structure:** Universal, free, compulsory primary and secondary (grades 1–12); preschools; high enrollment rates. Higher education includes state and private universities, many fields of study, and international ties.

**Reforms:** Post-Soviet transition to Armenian-centric curriculum, increased foreign language learning (English, Chinese), licensing for private schools.

**Assessment:** State exams after secondary determine higher education eligibility; drop-out and absenteeism linked to child labor and education quality.

**International Links:** Member of the Bologna Process and EU higher education networks; participates in Erasmus and other European programs.

## England

**Structure:** Legally compulsory education from ages 5–18; primary (Key Stages 1–2), secondary (Key Stages 3–4), post-16 options (Key Stage 5: A-levels, vocational “T-levels”), and higher education via universities/colleges.

**School Types:** State-funded (93%), including academies, free schools, comprehensives, grammar schools, university technical colleges, faith schools; private sector accounts for 7%.

**Curriculum & Exams:** National curriculum mandates core subjects and broad knowledge; GCSE exams at 16; A-levels, IB, NVQs post-16.

**Higher Education:** Competitive entry; bachelor’s, master’s, doctorate cycles; tuition fees exist but support is available. Wide variety of vocational and adult learning routes.

**Performance:** High international rankings (PISA, PIRLS, UN Education Index); funding expanded since 2018.

## Indonesia

**Structure:** PAUD (pre-school), six years primary (SD), three years junior high (SMP), three years senior high (SMA, academic or SMK/vocational). Education is compulsory and mostly free through junior secondary.

**History:** Colonial legacy, Islamic schooling traditions, and post-independence reforms focusing on inclusivity and secular curriculum; recent decentralization increased local control.

**Grading & Curriculum:** Emphasis on rote learning; revised curriculums stress sciences, languages, and religious study (varies by school type).

**Higher Education:** Academic (universities), vocational, and professional tracks. Admission increasingly competitive.

**Islamic Schools:** Three main types, from traditional pesantren to modern madrasah and sekolah Islam; significant religious/secular overlap.

## France

**Structure:** Preschool (maternelle, ages 3–6), elementary (école élémentaire, ages 6–11), collège (middle school, ages 11–15), lycée (high school, ages 15–18), leading to baccalauréat. Higher education: “grandes écoles,” public universities, and vocational technical institutes.

**Governance & Curriculum:** Highly centralized, secular (laïque); Ministry of National Education oversees unified curriculum. Private education mainly mirrors state programs (“sous contrat”) or offers alternative curricula.

**Assessment:** Bac exams critical for higher education access; competitive preparatory classes (“prépas”) route to elite schools.

**Tuition:** Public higher education is low cost or free for citizens; private sector much pricier.

**International Education:** Numerous international schools regulated by AEFE.

## Egypt

**Structure:** Highly centralized; basic education (two years kindergarten, six years primary, three years preparatory, ages 4–14) is compulsory and free. Secondary education is split into general and technical tracks; tertiary system is extensive, mainly publicly funded.

**History:** Rich legacy from ancient to Islamic times; modernization under Muhammad Ali; education suffered under British and flourished post-independence. Recent reforms focus on access, quality, and teacher development.

**Challenges:** Low education quality, reliance on private tutoring, teacher shortages, and high youth unemployment. Tuition fees in public schools plus prevalent private costs hinder equity.

**Other Systems:** Al-Azhar religious school system operates in parallel, and agricultural/vocational schools expand educational scope.

## Turkey

**Structure:** Compulsory primary (8 years), secondary (3–4 years, general or vocational), and increasing access to pre-primary. Recent reforms extend compulsory education to 12 years (“4+4+4” system).

**Higher Education:** Expansive public and private universities, technical and research strengths, increasing autonomy, and international collaborations; competitive university entrance.

**Curriculum & Religion:** History and Turkish nationalism central in curriculum; religious education now mandatory from grade 4, with content control by the Ministry of Education.

**Challenges:** Frequent policy changes, teacher shortages, textbook quality concerns, and controversy over historical narratives.

## Sri Lanka

**Structure:** Free, universal education; government-funded primary (grades 1–5), junior secondary (grades 6–9), senior secondary (grades 10–11), collegiate (grades 12–13), and tertiary (university level). Vocational and technical options available.

**History:** Buddhist, Hindu, Christian traditions; major reforms brought universal access and public schooling.

**School Types:** National, provincial, pirivenas (for Buddhist clergy), private, international, and madrasas; international schools mostly unregulated.

**Challenges:** Extremely competitive exams (O/L, A/L), limited university access, calls for private universities blocked, tuition class market rising.

**Vocational Education:** Progressing under the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, offering structured qualifications.

---

## Iran

**Structure:** Centralized, compulsory elementary (6 years), lower secondary (3 years), non-compulsory upper secondary (3 years), technical/vocational options, and highly competitive university entrance (Konkour).

**History & Reform:** Shaped by pre-Islamic, Islamic, and modern reforms; post-revolution era focused on Islamization and segregated schooling. Current reforms stress global knowledge and labor market alignment.

**Challenges:** Teacher shortages, rising privatization, rising costs, disparities in quality, and shortage of counselors.

**Higher Education:** Large, state-run system; bachelor’s, master's, PhD cycles; prominent universities, high female participation; rigorous admissions.

**Special & Gifted Education:** NODET and other selective schools exist for talented students; special education integrated.

**Languages:** Persian primary, Arabic and English (now French as option) at secondary; private language schools widespread.

## Israel

**Structure:** Universal primary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), and high school (10–12); compulsory through grade 12. Tracks: state-secular, state-religious, Haredi/independent, Arab, with some integration schools.

**Grading & Exams:** Bagrut (matriculation) exams determine university eligibility and employment prospects.

**Higher Education:** Highly valued; universities are subsidized, competitive admissions (bagrut, psychometric test), diverse programs. Army service often precedes or intertwines with higher studies.

**Challenges:** Inequality between Jewish and Arab sectors, gender gaps, teacher salary concerns, and persistent strikes.

**Performance:** High tertiary attainment; strong global university rankings, though international test scores show achievement gaps.

## Taiwan

**Structure:** Compulsory education spans 12 years—six elementary, three junior high, three senior high (regular or skill-based/vocational). Tertiary includes colleges, universities, graduate and technical programs.

**History:** Shift from suppressing local influence to expanding private schooling and broader access. Mandated by national law.

**Curriculum:** Academic achievement strongly emphasized; pressure and cram school industry prevalent. Corporal punishment officially banned but still reported.

**Higher Education:** Highly competitive entry; many public and private universities, strong in engineering and sciences. Parallel systems for vocational degrees.

**International & Private Education:** English immersion kindergartens and supplemental “buxiban” cram schools widely attended.

## Republic of Ireland

**Structure:** Compulsory from age 6–16 (or completion of three years secondary); primary school (infants to sixth class), secondary (junior cycle, transition year, senior cycle), and tertiary.

**School Types:** Majority of schools under Catholic patronage, but growing multi-denominational and Irish-language sectors.

**Curriculum & Exams:** Irish, English core subjects; Junior Certificate and Leaving Certificate for secondary cycles. Mock exams often precede state assessments.

**Special Needs & Equity:** Specialized resources, SNAs, targeted supports, and the Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools (DEIS) program. Reforms ongoing to address religious admissions bias.

**International Education:** Ireland’s universities are highly ranked; the country is a popular destination for international students.

---

## Nigeria

**Structure:** Universal Basic Education: 6 years primary, 3 years junior secondary; 3 years senior secondary or technical college; tertiary options include universities, polytechnics, colleges.

**Exams:** Promotional exams culminate in the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE), administered by WAEC/NECO. International A-level programs (IJMB, JUPEB) enable direct university entry.

**School Types:** Federal, state, private, religious, and international schools, with large disparities in funding, quality, and teacher qualifications.

**Challenges:** Low literacy (about 69%); regional, gender, and infrastructural disparities; high out-of-school children especially in the North due to poverty and insecurity; female education initiatives ongoing.

**Vocational & Informal Education:** National Board oversees vocational education; apprenticeship routes common; informal literacy and education programs vital but underfunded.

**Tertiary Education:** Over 150 universities (public/private); admission is highly competitive. Recent years have seen an increase in private and state university establishment.
