# Education in Sweden
Sweden’s education system spans from preschool to postgraduate levels, emphasizing compulsory comprehensive schooling (grades 1–9) for ages 6–16. Preschool is publicly accessible and partly subsidized, focusing on social values and early learning through play. Primary education has evolved from 19th-century folkskola to a unified Grundskola since 1962, with a broad curriculum including 16 compulsory subjects and graded assessments starting in year 6. Upper secondary education (gymnasieskola) is elective, with high enrollment, offering 18 national programmes divided into preparatory (academic) and vocational tracks. Admission depends on compulsory school grades, with alternative introductory programmes for students without standard eligibility. Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, offering bachelor's, master's, and doctoral cycles, with students required to demonstrate Swedish and English proficiency. Independent “free schools” (friskolor) coexist with public schools, funded by municipalities but debated for quality and profit motives. Sweden's PISA and PIRLS results have fluctuated, partly due to demographic changes and assessment challenges.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education traces to early Chinese village schools and missionary efforts in the 19th century, evolving under British rule with a bilingual (Chinese-English) approach. Post-handover reforms infused national curriculum elements, patriotism, and security education. Preschool is subsidized but not free; primary and secondary education is compulsory from age 6 to 18, culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) or International Baccalaureate (IB). Medium of instruction is primarily Chinese or English (EMI schools), with increasing promotion of Putonghua. Higher education features eight publicly funded universities renowned globally and several private providers, with English as the main instruction language. International schools are numerous and diverse, serving expatriates and affluent local families. Challenges include declining enrollment due to political climate, curriculum adjustments reflecting national security concerns, and efforts to integrate AI in learning.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education system is divided into elementary (grades 1–7), lower secondary (grades 8–10), and upper secondary school (grades 11–13), with compulsory education from ages 6 to 16. The system emphasizes inclusivity, with reforms merging vocational and general secondary education paths to enhance mobility between tracks. Assessment begins in lower secondary, with elective languages and subjects. Upper secondary education combines school-based learning and apprenticeships, culminating in graduation by roughly 73% of students. Educator roles vary by qualification, from preschool teachers to university lecturers. Higher education is provided mainly by state-run universities and colleges, overseen by quality agencies. Norway has a tradition of special education integration and a grading scale from 1 to 6. The academic calendar includes distinct semesters and multiple holiday breaks.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education evolved from informal apprenticeship systems and castle schools during the Portuguese era to formal missionary and colonial schools in the 19th century. Post-independence reforms introduced universal basic education, reorganizing the system into kindergarten, primary, junior high, senior high, and tertiary levels. Basic education is compulsory and free, and senior high offers academic or vocational tracks with a major exam (WASSCE) determining tertiary access. Tertiary education includes public and private universities and polytechnics. Despite steady enrollment increases, vocational education faces challenges like low prestige and completion rates. The government works with private and community partners to address funding and infrastructure, and significant focus is placed on female education, literacy, and ICT integration. Private-public partnerships play a vital role in Ghana’s education delivery.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia boasts near 100% literacy, with a compulsory and free 12-year primary and secondary education system divided into primary (grades 1–4), middle (grades 5–9), and high school (grades 10–12) levels, including general and specialized streams. State public schools dominate, following a national curriculum, while private schools offering specialized programs coexist, subject to government licensing and quality oversight. Higher education includes numerous public and private universities, with a focus on fields such as science, medicine, engineering, and Armenian studies. Armenia actively participates in the Bologna Process and European educational collaborations, with reforms in quality assurance and international programs. Challenges include rising dropout rates linked to child labor and a push for expanded non-traditional education opportunities such as technology centers.

# Education in England
England mandates education or training for children aged 5 to 18, structured into early years, primary, secondary, and post-16 education stages. The National Curriculum governs state schools across subjects with standardized assessments (e.g., GCSEs, A-levels). School types include comprehensive, grammar, academy, free, and private schools, many with faith or specialist statuses. Post-16 education offers diverse pathways via sixth-form colleges, further education, apprenticeships, and T-levels. Higher education is extensive, consisting of universities offering bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees, with fees introduced in 1998 and capped since 2012. Quality assurance is overseen by Ofsted and QAA. Adult education and lifelong learning are widely promoted. England maintains multiple qualification frameworks aligned with European standards and has shown overall high international student performance with ongoing funding reforms.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education system combines formal secular schools and Islamic institutions (pesantren, madrasah), shaped by Dutch colonial legacy and post-independence reform. Formal schooling covers 12 years compulsory education across kindergarten, primary, junior, and senior secondary levels, with academic and vocational streams. Public and private religious schools operate under different ministries. Islamic education coexists within formal and informal systems. Teacher qualification standards have been rising, but challenges remain in rural teacher shortages and infrastructure. The post-colonial national curriculum integrates Pancasila’s principles. Higher education includes universities, institutes, and polytechnics, aligned under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework. Vocational education is promoted with diploma programs. The system recently adopted a “4+4+4” structure, with emphasis on accommodating religious education (Imam Hatip schools) and vocational training.

# Education in France
French education, shaped since the Third Republic by secular, free, and compulsory schooling laws, features standardized curricula set by the Ministry of National Education across preschool, primary, secondary (collège and lycée), and higher education levels. Primary school starts around age 3 with maternelle, followed by elementary school. Secondary education divides into collège (11–15 years) and lycée (15–18 years), culminating in the baccalauréat exam which channels students to universities or professional routes. Private schools include those “under contract” with the state and independent religious schools. Higher education includes universities and prestigious grandes écoles specialized in sciences, humanities, business, and administration; admission involves highly selective preparatory classes (CPGEs). France follows the Bologna system with Licence, Master, and Doctorate degrees. Education has a strong emphasis on secularism and national culture, with ongoing reforms to promote diversity and modernization.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education has ancient roots, evolving from classical centers like al-Azhar to French-influenced colonial reforms and modern nation-building efforts. The system is centralized and free basic education is compulsory for ages 4–14, followed by secondary education split into general and technical tracks. Higher education includes public and private universities, with increasing enrollment but ongoing quality challenges. Parallel systems include the Al-Azhar Islamic educational track emphasizing religious studies. Challenges include disparities in urban-rural access, teacher shortages, reliance on rote learning, heavy private tutoring dependence, and limited resources. Recent reforms address curriculum, pedagogy, and ICT integration. International schools and agricultural secondary schools add diversity. Quality, funding, and infrastructure remain key issues, with youth unemployment linked to inadequate TVET. The pandemic spurred distance learning. Egypt hosts many international schools offering varied foreign curricula.

# Education in Turkey
Following secularization and modernization reforms post-Ottoman era, Turkey’s education system is highly centralized under the Ministry of National Education. Compulsory education lasts 12 years, segmented into primary (8 years) and secondary (4 years), with general, vocational, and religious tracks. The “4+4+4” system introduced in 2012 restructured schooling, reintroducing religious (Imam Hatip) schools. Higher education comprises universities and vocational schools, admitting students through centralized exams. Private schools and minority language education comply with regulations. Religious education is integrated but controversial, with curriculum debates, including censorship of evolution and nationalist indoctrination. Foreign languages, mainly English, are taught starting early, with expansion of Kurdish and Arabic instruction in recent years. The system faces criticism for political control, curriculum bias, textbook quality, and frequent structural changes.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education system, with roots in religious monastic schools and colonial missionary institutions, is governed by multiple ministries. Education is free and compulsory from primary through junior secondary levels, progressing to senior secondary and collegiate stages, culminating in GCE Ordinary and Advanced Level exams for university access. The system accommodates multiple religions through national, Muslim, Hindu, and international schools. Universities are state-funded with limited private sector involvement. Vocational education and training is coordinated under the Ministry of Skills Development. Tuition classes are prevalent due to high competition. Education faces criticism for rigidity and lack of private university development. Special education, adult education, and leadership training have programs in place. Recent policies focus on addressing inequality through increased resources to disadvantaged schools (DEIS).

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education reflects ancient traditions, modernization under the Pahlavi dynasty, and Islamization after the 1979 revolution. The Ministry of Education centrally administers schooling from pre-primary to university level, offering free elementary and secondary education. Upper secondary education is not compulsory but widespread, with options in general, technical-vocational, and manual skills tracks. Higher education is extensive, including prestigious universities and a large private Islamic Azad University system. Admission is exam-based (Konkur). Women are a majority in university enrollment. Education reforms emphasize improving quality and integrating Islamic values. Challenges include teacher shortages, privatization, political control of curricula, and restrictions on protest within education. Foreign languages are taught variably; English is common, alongside Arabic and recently added French. Notable are selective gifted schools (NODET) and distance learning institutions.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system consists of primary, middle, and high school tiers with compulsory education from kindergarten through grade 12. Schools are divided mainly into secular Jewish, state-religious, Haredi religious, and Arab sectors, each with distinct curricula and cultural emphasis. The state's education focuses on academic rigor, with a high proportion of post-secondary education attainment. Arab and Haredi communities face educational disparities and underfunding. Matriculation (Bagrut) and Psychometric Entrance Tests regulate university admission. Israel boasts high university standards and international research reputation but contends with inequalities and teacher union strikes. Education is highly valued culturally, linked to socioeconomic mobility, and supported by significant government investment.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan offers a 12-year compulsory education system covering preschool through senior high school, with national exams controlling high school and university access. The curriculum emphasizes Mandarin, English, math, science, social studies, arts, and physical education, with vocational and skill-based streams available. Higher education includes a variety of public and private universities and junior colleges offering associate to doctoral degrees. International programs and private education (including cram schools) are common. Education is competitive with high university entrance rates leading to some graduate unemployment. Special education, teacher training, and language instruction are actively supported. The academic calendar runs from September to June with multiple breaks. The government invests in expanding early childhood education and vocational education.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland’s education system spans from pre-school to tertiary levels, with compulsory education from age 6 to 16 (or three years of secondary school). The system is predominantly state-funded but heavily influenced by religious bodies, particularly the Catholic Church, though multi-denominational and secular schools are increasing. Primary and secondary education are organized into clear stages culminating in Junior and Leaving Certificate exams. Transition Year provides an optional maturity period. Higher education includes universities and colleges with diverse programs. Special needs education and disadvantaged school support (DEIS) are integral. Ireland ranks highly in literacy and science internationally, with policies addressing school closures and funding. Historical tensions exist over religious influence in schools.


# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education begins with six years of primary education followed by three years of junior and three years of senior secondary schooling, culminating in national exams for tertiary admission. The system includes federal, state, and private schools with varying resources and quality. Vocational education is promoted but challenged by outdated curricula and low completion rates. Literacy rates are improving but regional disparities and socio-cultural factors affect access, particularly for girls. Informal education, apprenticeships, and literacy programs supplement formal education. Female education is targeted by initiatives addressing barriers like early marriage and militancy. Tertiary education includes public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges with growing offerings but funding and quality concerns persist.

# Overall Summary
The global education systems reviewed share common structures of compulsory primary and secondary education, often followed by diverse higher education pathways encompassing vocational, technical, and academic tracks. Most countries face challenges of equitable access, quality assurance, resource allocation, teacher training, and curriculum relevance, with reforms aiming to increase inclusivity, international compatibility, and adaption to changing socio-political landscapes. Religious and private schooling coexist in many regions, affecting admission and curriculum content. International assessments reveal fluctuating student performance, while technology integration, adult education, and lifelong learning receive growing emphasis. Cultural values deeply influence education goals, with many systems evolving to balance tradition and modernity.
