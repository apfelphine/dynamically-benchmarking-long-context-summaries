### **Education in Sweden**
Sweden’s education system is structured into pre-primary, primary, upper secondary, and higher education, with a strong emphasis on equality and access. Compulsory education spans from age 6 to 16, with a nine-year comprehensive school (grundskola) at the core. The system includes various types of schools, such as universities, university colleges, and vocational institutions. Higher education follows the Bologna Process, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. The grading system is criteria-referenced, with multiple systems in use. Preschool is free for children aged 3–6, and the system promotes equal value of all people through inclusive pedagogy. The government ensures free education for citizens, EU/EEA, and Swiss nationals, with student aid provided by CSN. Independent schools (friskolor) are publicly funded and offer alternative pedagogies, though they face criticism over grading practices and inequality. The system is evaluated through national tests, with a focus on equity and student development.

### **Education in Hong Kong**
Hong Kong’s education system is divided into pre-primary, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary education is compulsory from age 6 to 14, with a focus on bilingual instruction (English and Chinese). Secondary education is split into junior and senior levels, with students choosing between academic and vocational tracks. The Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) is a key examination for tertiary admissions. The system emphasizes English as the medium of instruction, with a growing number of EMI (English Medium Instruction) schools. Vocational education is limited, with a focus on practical skills. Pre-school education is not free, though government subsidies are available. The system faces challenges related to equity, including disparities in access and outcomes for students from different backgrounds. The government has introduced reforms to promote inclusivity, with a growing emphasis on national identity and civic education. The influx of immigrants has impacted student performance, leading to concerns over curriculum relevance.

### **Education in Norway**

Higher education is accessible through a range of public and private institutions, with a strong emphasis on research and quality assurance. ### **Education in Ghana**
Ghana’s education system is structured into early years, basic, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary education is compulsory and free, with a focus on foundational skills in literacy, numeracy, and problem-solving. Secondary education offers academic and vocational pathways, with students preparing for national exams such as the West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE). Tertiary education is growing, with both public and private institutions offering degrees. The system faces challenges related to teacher shortages, quality, and access, especially in rural areas. The government has implemented reforms to improve equity, including the Free, Compulsory and Universal Basic Education (FCUBE) policy. Private schools play a significant role in providing alternative education, though access remains unequal. The system emphasizes national identity and cultural values, with a growing focus on vocational training and adult education. Challenges include low teacher qualifications, limited infrastructure, and gender disparities in access.
