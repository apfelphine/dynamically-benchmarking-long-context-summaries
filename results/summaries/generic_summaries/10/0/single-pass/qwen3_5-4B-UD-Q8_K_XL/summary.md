# Global Education Systems Summary

## Sweden
Sweden's education system is characterized by a comprehensive, state-funded structure with a strong emphasis on equality and high quality. The system is divided into preschool, compulsory primary education (grundskola), upper secondary education (gymnasieskola), and higher education. Compulsory education covers ages 6–16, with a focus on core subjects like English, mathematics, and Swedish. Grading begins in sixth grade using a letter system (A–E), while national tests in grades 3, 6, and 9 ensure standardized assessment. The system offers diverse pathways, including vocational training and special education for specific needs. Higher education is Bologna-compliant, featuring universities and university colleges that offer bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. While the system is highly regarded, recent challenges include declining performance in international assessments like PISA and issues regarding the independent school sector (*friskolor*), which face scrutiny over grading standards and profit models.

## Hong Kong
Hong Kong's education system underwent significant transformation following the 1997 handover from British to Chinese rule. Historically rooted in missionary and colonial influences, the system now blends Chinese and Western curricula. Compulsory education is mandatory for six years of primary and three years of secondary school. The system is stratified into public sector schools (government, aided, and private) and international schools. A notable distinction exists between Chinese-medium instruction (CMI) and English-medium instruction (EMI), with EMI schools often commanding higher university admission rates. Recent political developments, including the National Security Law, have impacted enrollment as some families opt for international education abroad. The curriculum has shifted to emphasize patriotism, national security, and civic development, replacing Liberal Studies with Citizenship and Social Development.

## Norway
Norway's education system is unified under the *grunnskole* (foundation school) for primary and lower secondary education, which is free and compulsory for ages 6–16. The system emphasizes equity, with a single curriculum across the country, though students often change schools upon entering upper secondary school (*videregående skole*). Upper secondary education lasts three years and offers two main tracks: general studies (*studieforberedelse*) for university preparation and vocational studies (*yrkesfag*), often following a "2+2" model with an apprenticeship. The grading system is unique, using a 1–6 scale where higher numbers indicate better performance. Teachers are highly trained, with distinct roles for adjuncts, lecturers, and researchers. The system faces criticism regarding the pace of learning and the need to support students who struggle with basic knowledge.

## Ghana
Ghana's education system prioritizes free and compulsory basic education for nine years (ages 4–14), comprising kindergarten, primary, and junior high school. Secondary education (senior high school) is also free but follows a three-year academic curriculum leading to the West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE). The system is heavily decentralized, with local district assemblies managing many schools. Despite high enrollment rates, challenges persist regarding teacher quality, infrastructure, and the low prestige of vocational education. A significant portion of the population relies on private tutoring to supplement the public curriculum. Higher education is expanding rapidly, though there is a noted disparity in access between wealthy urban students and poorer rural populations.

## Armenia
Armenia's education system is free and compulsory for 12 years, structured into primary, middle, and high school levels. Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the system shifted from a centralized Russian-influenced model to one emphasizing the Armenian language and culture, though Russian remains widely used. Public schools dominate the landscape, while private institutions offer specialized programs. The system faces challenges with high dropout rates, particularly among children engaged in child labor, and disparities in enrollment between urban and rural areas. Higher education is centralized, with Yerevan State University as the leading institution, but the country is increasingly participating in European higher education frameworks like the Bologna Process.

## England
England's education system is highly centralized yet diverse, with compulsory education lasting until age 16, followed by optional study until 18. It is divided into five Key Stages, with the National Curriculum mandating core subjects in state-funded schools. The system includes a wide range of school types: community schools, academies, free schools, and voluntary aided/controlled schools. Selective grammar schools compete for students via the 11-plus exam, while comprehensive schools are non-selective. Post-16 education includes sixth forms, further education colleges, and apprenticeships. Higher education is competitive, with the Russell Group universities leading in prestige. Funding is nationalized, and the system faces ongoing debates regarding social mobility and the impact of academy trusts.

## Indonesia
Indonesia's education system is compulsory for 12 years, covering elementary (SD), junior high (SMP), and senior high (SMA). It is the fourth-largest in the world but faces significant challenges with equity, funding, and teacher distribution, particularly in outer islands. Public schools are government-operated, while Islamic schools (*pesantren* and *madrasah*) play a crucial role, often providing religious and modern education. The curriculum emphasizes rote learning and the national ideology, Pancasila. Higher education is expanding but remains accessible primarily to those from urban areas. Vocational education exists but often suffers from low prestige and a lack of industry alignment.

## France
France's education system is highly centralized, with a secular (*laïque*) curriculum enforced by the Ministry of National Education. Compulsory education lasts until age 16. The system is known for its rigor, particularly in the *grandes écoles* and *classes préparatoires aux grandes écoles* (CPGE), which are highly selective pathways to elite engineering and business schools. Public universities offer degrees in three cycles (Licence, Master, Doctorat) and are generally free for EU citizens. The system emphasizes a unified national culture through history and literature education. Challenges include the impact of immigration on social cohesion and the strict enforcement of secularism in schools.

## Egypt
Egypt's education system is highly centralized and free for basic education (ages 4–14), though disparities exist between urban and rural areas. The system includes a three-year secondary stage leading to university entrance. Despite high enrollment rates, the quality of education is often criticized for relying on rote memorization. A parallel market for private tutoring has become widespread, with families spending a significant portion of income on it to improve academic outcomes. Challenges include teacher shortages, low salaries, and infrastructure deficits. Higher education is expanding but faces issues with overcrowding and the need for better governance and relevance.

## Turkey
Turkey's education system is secular and state-controlled, with compulsory education lasting 12 years. Historically influenced by Atatürk's reforms, it mandates Turkish nationalism and secularism, though religious education has been reintroduced in recent years. The system includes primary, secondary, and high school levels, with high school students choosing tracks (science, social sciences, etc.) that influence university admissions. Recent controversies have arisen regarding curriculum changes, such as the removal of evolution theory and the inclusion of creationism in some areas. Higher education is competitive, with public universities being tuition-free but facing challenges in quality and infrastructure.

## Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's education system is divided into five parts: primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate, and tertiary. It is free and compulsory up to grade 9. The system is characterized by high competition, particularly in the G.C.E. Ordinary and Advanced Level examinations, which determine university admission. State universities are free but extremely competitive, often resulting in high dropout rates among graduates due to a mismatch between skills and job market needs. The system includes a diverse range of schools, from government schools to international institutions and religious schools, though quality varies significantly.

## Iran
Iran's education system is highly centralized and heavily influenced by Islamic values and laws. Compulsory education covers primary and lower-secondary levels. Higher education is based on the competitive *Konkur* exam. The system has undergone significant changes since the 1979 Revolution, including the Islamization of curricula and segregation by sex (though this is being relaxed). Iran produces a large number of engineers and STEM graduates, particularly women, who often face challenges in the job market. The system faces issues with teacher shortages, privatization pressures, and the need for reform to align with global standards.

## Israel
Israel's education system is unique in its diversity, serving a multi-religious and multi-ethnic population. It consists of state-secular, state-religious, Haredi (ultra-Orthodox), and Arab tracks. Compulsory education lasts until age 18. The Haredi sector, focused on Torah study, often results in lower academic achievement and workforce participation. The Arab sector faces significant resource disparities compared to the Jewish sector. The system is highly competitive, with the *Bagrut* (matriculation) exam being crucial for university admission and military conscription. Israel ranks highly in PISA scores but faces challenges with inequality and teacher morale.

## Taiwan
Taiwan's education system is highly competitive, with a strong emphasis on academic achievement and preparation for university entrance exams. Compulsory education spans 12 years, from elementary to senior high school. The system is known for strict discipline, high class sizes, and the use of "cram schools" (*buxiban*) for supplementary tutoring. Higher education is accessible but intense, with universities often being large and research-focused. The system has seen reforms to increase vocational education and reduce pressure, but the culture of academic excellence remains dominant.

## Republic of Ireland
Ireland's education system is free and compulsory from ages 6 to 16. It is structured into primary, junior cycle (ending with the Junior Certificate), transition year, and senior cycle (ending with the Leaving Certificate). The system is diverse, with a significant portion of schools under Catholic patronage, though reforms are increasing multi-denominational options. Ireland consistently performs well in international assessments like PISA. Higher education is accessible, with free undergraduate tuition for EU citizens. Challenges include addressing educational disadvantage through the Deis scheme and improving special needs support.

## Nigeria
Nigeria's education system is characterized by a "6-3-3-4" structure (primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, and tertiary). Universal Basic Education (UBE) provides free primary and junior secondary education. The system faces severe challenges, including poor infrastructure, inadequate teacher training, and regional disparities. Literacy rates remain low, particularly in the North. Higher education is expanding with many private universities, but quality varies. Vocational education is often informal, and the system struggles with funding and political instability.
