## Global Education System Overviews

### Sweden
Swedish education covers various levels, from Preschool class (age 6-7) to Doctoral level. Compulsory education is a nine-year comprehensive school ($\text{grundskola}$) for ages 6–15. Students take 16 compulsory subjects, and grading begins in year 6. Upper secondary education ($\text{gymnasieskola}$), usually three years, is elective and divided into preparatory and vocational national programmes. Higher education involves university ($\text{Universitet}$) and research-entitled university colleges ($\text{Högskola med forskningstillstånd}$), structured around the Bologna Process cycles (Bachelor's, Master's, Doctoral). Preschool is available for children from age one.

### Hong Kong
Hong Kong's education mandates primary schooling after age 6 and secondary schooling before age 18. The system includes Kindergarten, Primary school (P.1–P.6), Junior secondary (S.1–S.3), and Senior secondary (S.4–S.6), culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE). While public primary education is free, pre-school education requires payment, although vouchers are available for residents. The system has seen shifts in curriculum emphasis, with recent reforms emphasizing national security and patriotic themes.

### Norway
The Norwegian system is compulsory from age 6 to 16, divided into elementary ($\text{barneskole}$, ages 6–12), lower secondary ($\text{ungdomsskole}$, ages 13–16), and upper secondary ($\text{videregående skole}$, ages 16–19). Elementary and lower secondary schools do not formally grade, but grades begin in lower secondary school. The upper secondary level features general studies ($\text{studieforberedelse}$) or vocational studies ($\text{yrkesfag}$), often using a 2+2 model. Higher education is state-run, with universities focusing on theory and university colleges offering a wide range of degrees, all overseen by NOKUT.

### Ghana
Ghana’s education system is divided into basic (12 years, ages 4–15), secondary, and tertiary education. Basic education is free and compulsory, covering Kindergarten, Primary school (6 years), and Junior High School (3 years), concluding with the BECE. Secondary education branches into academic or vocational programmes, ending with the WASSCE. Tertiary education is growing rapidly, with a mix of public and private institutions. The system faces challenges related to funding, quality control, and high graduate unemployment, though recent enrollment rates are high.

### Armenia
Armenian education is largely free and compulsory, spanning 12 years, structured into primary (grades 1–4), middle school (grades 5–9), and high school (grades 10–12). Public schools form 97% of all institutions and adhere to the national curriculum, blending global trends with local culture. While public schools are state-funded, private schools exist, operating under specific licensing rules. Higher education adheres to the Bologna Process, with a strong focus on state-run universities.

### England
In England, education is compulsory from age 5 to 18. The system includes Early Years, four Key Stages (KS1-KS4), and post-16 education. State-funded schools are predominantly comprehensive, although selective grammar schools exist. Schools are subject to inspection by Ofsted. Higher education is provided by universities, with significant fee structures, though state support helps mitigate costs for low-income students. The National Curriculum sets standards across thirteen core subjects for state schools.

### Indonesia
Indonesia requires twelve years of schooling. The system includes compulsory primary and middle school, followed by senior high school (SMA) or vocational high schools (SMK). Historically, the Dutch colonial system segregated education by social status. Today, the system balances public, nonsectarian schools with private and religious (Islamic) schools. The curriculum emphasizes national principles ($\text{Pancasila}$), but pedagogical styles often rely on rote learning. Tertiary education is diverse, spanning academic, vocational, and professional streams, organized under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI).

### France
Education in France is highly centralized under the Ministry of National Education and Youth. Primary education (maternelle and école élémentaire) is mandatory, free, and secular, emphasizing a "common culture" for national integration. Secondary education includes $\text{collège}$ (middle school) and $\text{lycée}$ (high school), leading to the $\text{baccalauréat}$. Higher education operates under the Bologna Process, distinguishing between public universities and elite $\text{grandes écoles}$. State funding is substantial, keeping tuition low for EU citizens, though private institutions exist.

## Comparative and Regional Systems

### Turkey
Turkey’s education system has undergone significant secularization since the Republic's foundation in 1924. The Ministry of National Education oversees curricula and standards. Compulsory education was extended to eight years, and in 2012, it was prolonged to 12 years under the "4+4+4" model. While the system aims for secularism, religious education courses are mandated throughout schooling. Higher education is state-funded, though private universities exist; technical universities are globally recognized for engineering.

### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's system is overseen by several ministries, leading to administrative complexities. Education historically evolved from Buddhist temple systems to British colonial models. The structure includes primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, and collegiate levels. The system emphasizes national languages ($\text{Sinhala}$ or $\text{Tamil}$) in primary education. Higher education is primarily state-run, but the high-stakes nature of exams drives a parallel, lucrative private tutoring industry.

### Israel
Israeli education is compulsory from kindergarten through 12th grade and is highly diverse, operating in four tracks: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious ($\text{Haredi}$), and Arab. Society highly values higher education as a path to socioeconomic mobility. The Bagrut matriculation exam determines progression. While the system is praised for high academic attainment, disparities exist, particularly between sectors and in resource allocation to the Arab sector.

### Taiwan
Taiwan’s public education system is compulsory for 12 years, with Mandarin as the language of instruction. The system is characterized by a tight discipline and reliance on national exams for university entrance. Junior high school focuses on preparation for senior high, while senior high allows tracks in liberal arts or science. Tertiary education is highly competitive, with high enrollment rates in universities, and private cram schools ($\text{buxiban}$) are a major, profitable element supporting academic achievement.

### Republic of Ireland
Ireland features a state-funded system where English is the primary medium, though $\text{Gaelscoileanna}$ use Irish. Compulsory education runs from age 6 to 16, culminating in the Junior Certificate and the Leaving Certificate. The system balances breadth and depth, with secondary schools offering different types (voluntary, community, comprehensive). The country ranks high internationally in student literacy, though religious patronage remains a dominant factor in primary school control.

### Nigeria
Nigeria's education is structured around a 9-3-4 system (Universal Basic Education), which is intended to be free and compulsory. The system is segmented into federal, state-sponsored, and expensive private/religious schools. While vocational training is emphasized due to high unemployment, challenges persist due to poor infrastructure, teacher shortages, and political instability affecting policy implementation. Literacy rates remain low, particularly in northern regions.

### Egypt
Egypt’s system is highly centralized, with compulsory basic education lasting nine years (ages 4–14). Education is provided free in government schools, but the poor quality of public education fuels a large, near-compulsory private tutoring market. The system is divided into general and technical secondary tracks. While access has expanded, low standardized test scores highlight systemic quality issues, exacerbated by a wealth gap in attainment.

### Iran
Iranian education is centralized and features a strong Islamization trend since the 1979 revolution, with mandatory religious instruction. The system includes three types of secondary education: theoretical, technical-vocational, and manual skills. Higher education admission relies heavily on the Konkur exam. Despite high government spending on education, privatization is rising rapidly, and teacher shortages persist.

## Emerging Trends and Global Comparisons

### Internationalization and Private Sector Influence
Several countries showcase increasing private sector involvement. In **Sweden**, private options exist; in **Hong Kong**, independent schools are numerous; and in **Ireland**, preparatory schools are independent. **Egypt** relies heavily on private tutoring due to public sector shortcomings. **Israel** has diverse private options, while **Nigeria** has expensive private schools contrasting with subsidized federal schools. In **Taiwan**, the lucrative cram school industry is a major feature.

### Curriculum and Pedagogy
Curricular focus varies widely. **France** prioritizes transmitting a "common culture" and secular identity. **Egypt** has traditionally relied on rote memorization. **Norway** emphasizes play and social structure in early grades. Conversely, **England** and **Israel** emphasize rigorous, high-stakes exams (GCSE/Bagrut) to determine progression, while **Iran** has recently shifted toward a research/facilitator model alongside traditional memorization.

### Policy and Governance
Governance models differ: **Sweden** is a decentralized system for post-secondary education; **France** is highly centralized under the Ministry of National Education. **Ghana** and **Nigeria** struggle with administrative decentralization versus central control. **Turkey** actively promotes secularism, though religious content is mandated in schools. **China** (via Taiwan's historical context) demonstrates high state control aimed at assimilation.

### Equity and Access
Disparities are a common theme. **Israel** shows sector-based inequalities; **Nigeria** exhibits deep regional and socioeconomic disparities. **Ghana** and **Egypt** demonstrate a widening gap between affluent and poor students. **Ireland** has seen shifts in religious school control. **Sweden** and **Norway** maintain strong public systems with relatively high equity benchmarks.
