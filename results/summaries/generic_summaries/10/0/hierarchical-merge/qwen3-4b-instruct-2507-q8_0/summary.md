# **Merged Summary: Global Education Systems (Sweden, Norway, Hong Kong, Ghana, Armenia, Egypt, Turkey, Sri Lanka, Iran, Israel, Taiwan, Ireland, Nigeria, England, France, Indonesia)**

## **Scandinavian and East Asian Systems: Equity, Holistic Development, and National Standards**

Sweden, Norway, and Hong Kong represent education systems rooted in equity, national standards, and holistic student development. Sweden’s nine-year *grundskola* (ages 6–16) emphasizes inclusion, student agency, and access, with modernized grading and national assessments in core subjects. Upper secondary education (*gymnasieskola*) offers academic and vocational pathways, supported by *komvux* and *friskolor* for diverse learners. Despite strong performance, concerns persist over rising non-qualifiers—particularly among boys and students of foreign origin—and declining PISA results, linked to immigration and methodological factors. Norway prioritizes emotional, social, and cultural development, with no formal grading in primary school and full integration of special education into mainstream settings. The *Kunnskapsløftet* reform (2006) promotes student choice between academic and vocational tracks, combining classroom learning with apprenticeships. PISA results remain strong, though math scores lag behind Shanghai. Hong Kong’s historically exam-driven system has evolved to balance academic rigor with national values, including mandatory Citizenship and Social Development and field trips to mainland China. The HKDSE remains central to university admission, with English proficiency and global competitiveness emphasized. However, the national security law has led to an exodus of educators and students, and curriculum neutrality remains a contentious issue.

## **Sub-Saharan, South Asian, and Middle Eastern Systems: Access, Inequity, and Structural Challenges**

Ghana, Armenia, Egypt, Sri Lanka, Turkey, Iran, and Nigeria reflect systems shaped by historical legacies, socioeconomic disparities, and political influence. Ghana offers free, compulsory 12-year basic education with rising enrollment but persistent challenges in technical education and teacher quality; private schools dominate, and gender/rural disparities remain. Armenia provides free, compulsory education from age 6 to 18, with high primary and secondary enrollment, though high school participation lags OECD standards. Student absenteeism and dropout rates have increased due to child labor and declining quality, though innovation hubs like Tumo and SMART support youth development. Sri Lanka maintains a rigid, competitive structure with GCE O/L and A/L exams as university gateways—only 16% of qualified students gain admission, fueling widespread private tutoring and study-abroad trends. A controversial period of military-style leadership training was scrapped due to public backlash. Egypt’s system, rooted in ancient and Islamic institutions, suffers from poor quality, high student-to-teacher ratios, and rote learning, ranking 118th globally in education quality (WEF), with youth unemployment at 36.1% and private tutoring deepening inequality. Turkey blends secular nationalism with mandatory religious education from grade 4, including a controversial denial of the Armenian genocide; state textbooks are criticized for poor quality, and minority education has been suppressed. Iran’s centralized, Islamicly influenced system is free and compulsory up to grade 9, with optional upper-secondary tracks and a competitive *Konkur* exam for university admission. Despite progress in STEM (70% of engineering graduates are women), challenges include high dropout rates, teacher shortages, mandatory prayer times, and limited English instruction since 2018. Nigeria follows a 6-3-3-4 structure with free, compulsory 9-year basic education under the UBE policy, monitored by UBEC. Students take the CEE and SSCE (WAEC/GCE) for university entry, with growing access via IJMB and JUPEB pathways. However, rural schools face poor infrastructure, teacher shortages, insecurity, and gender-based barriers. Vocational education is underfunded and misaligned with labor markets, while informal education remains decentralized and under-resourced.

## **Global Patterns and Common Challenges**

Across all regions, education systems are shaped by cultural values, historical legacies, and political priorities. England, France, and Indonesia feature nationally regulated systems—England through Ofsted and the National Curriculum, France via *laïcité* and elite *grande écoles*, and Indonesia through Pancasila and *pesantren*—emphasizing national identity, academic standards, and access. In contrast, systems in Egypt, Sri Lanka, Turkey, Nigeria, and Iran face persistent issues of low quality, inequitable access, and misalignment between education and labor markets. Common challenges include poor teacher training, unequal funding, overreliance on high-stakes exams, and political interference in curriculum and policy. While some countries—such as Armenia (Bologna Process), Taiwan (rigorous national exams and high tertiary participation), and Ireland (strong PISA performance and inclusive special education)—have made strides in modernization and equity, structural inequalities remain significant. A shared global imperative is to strengthen teacher quality, promote inclusive and culturally responsive curricula, and ensure education serves both academic excellence and social equity. All systems were impacted by the 2020–2021 pandemic, with nationwide school closures disrupting continuity of learning. Taiwan and Ireland have robust policies for special education and international engagement, while Nigeria continues to struggle with underfunded vocational education and rural disparities. Despite improvements in literacy and access, regional, gender, and socioeconomic inequalities persist as enduring barriers to equitable education worldwide.
