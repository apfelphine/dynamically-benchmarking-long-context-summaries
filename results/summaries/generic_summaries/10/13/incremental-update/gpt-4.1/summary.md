## Summary

### Indonesia
Indonesia’s education system evolved from religious and colonial foundations to a compulsory, modern framework, offering academic and vocational tracks under the 2013 curriculum. While literacy and access have improved, significant disparities persist in teacher quality, resources, and early childhood education, particularly in remote areas. The system supports special needs students, and higher education follows the KKNI framework.

### Armenia
Armenia’s public education progressed due to government and European reforms, emphasizing increased funding, STEM and medical program quality, and improved rural access.

### Egypt
Egypt’s education blends traditional, religious, and specialized pathways, but is challenged by school quality, exam integrity, tutoring prevalence, and graduate unemployment.

### Norway
Norway mandates schooling ages 6–16, offers academic/vocational tracks, focuses on teacher training, and integrates technology. High graduation rates exist but regional disparities remain.

### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka provides free, compulsory education up to age 14, managed by multiple ministries and bolstered by private tuition and vocational options. Higher education is highly competitive and limited.

### Sweden
Sweden utilizes a merit-based system from preschool through secondary levels, with academic/vocational tracks and ongoing curriculum reforms. Issues include equity, immigration challenges, and provision of free higher education for domestic/EU students.

### Israel
Israel’s diverse system covers secular, religious, Haredi, Arab, and private institutions, marked by funding and achievement gaps and challenges for minorities. Ongoing reforms address teacher pay, strikes, admissions exams, and equity, especially in technology and mobility.

### Hong Kong
Hong Kong provides voluntary kindergarten, compulsory primary/junior secondary, and selective senior secondary and tertiary options, including international schools. The bilingual, exam-focused system faces socio-political and equity challenges, discipline and inclusion issues, and complexities in cross-border education.

### Republic of Ireland
Ireland mandates schooling ages 6–16, offers free preschool, various secondary options (primarily religious-run), and supports disadvantaged groups. The system is modernizing, recovering post-pandemic, and features top-ranked higher education.

### Ghana
Ghana’s multi-agency education system spans basic to tertiary levels, with privatization, support for women and rural areas, vocational/adult programs, and scholarship/equity reforms. Challenges include low vocational uptake, digital divides, and competitive tertiary entry.

### Nigeria
Nigeria offers nine years of compulsory education, but faces issues in teacher quality, infrastructure, exam outcomes, and female access barriers. Entry to tertiary and vocational tracks is exam-based, with growing digital literacy initiatives.

### England
England mandates schooling until 16 (with education to 18), features a mix of state, private, and home education, and charges university fees up to £9,535/year, with support schemes. Academic performance is strong, and Academy schools benefit from targeted funding.

### Taiwan
Taiwan provides 12 years of compulsory education through academic and technical tracks, supported by widespread cram schools. Higher education mirrors the U.S. system, with expanding adult and overseas options and high transition rates.

### France
France’s secular, compulsory education covers primary through lycée under strict Ministry oversight for both public and private providers. The system features selective grandes écoles, low EU tuition, renowned research agencies, and continuing reforms in teaching recruitment, secularism, and youth engagement.

### Iran
Iran’s centralized system managed by the Ministry and OERP oversees curriculum and assessment, emphasizing Islamization and gender segregation since 1979. The country boasts high female university and STEM participation, Persian as the main instruction language, regional disparities, and digital learning expansion, alongside high investment and specialized elite schools.

### Turkey
Turkey implements a secular, unified 4+4+4 structure (eight years mandatory), offering general and religious (Imam Hatip) secondary tracks, with most universities public, low fees, international accreditation in technical universities, and exam-based admissions. The education system has seen marked growth in enrollment and the teaching workforce, robust support for scientific research (e.g., TÜBİTAK), and expanded language instruction beginning in elementary school, with electives in English, Arabic, German, and French. Foreign languages and minority language options have increased since 2012, although minority and religious education remain restricted by government regulation and policies linked to the Treaty of Lausanne. The Ministry of Education tightly controls curricula, emphasizing Turkish nationalism and Atatürk’s secular-democratic ideals. However, recent curriculum changes have reduced emphasis on Atatürk and sciences while increasing Islamic and nationalistic content, including school rituals and history lessons that promote official narratives, sometimes denying topics such as the Armenian genocide. These changes are controversial and accompanied by critique of low-quality state textbooks, leading families to purchase expensive alternatives.
