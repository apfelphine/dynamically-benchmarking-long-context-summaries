# Summary of Education in Indonesia, Armenia, Egypt, Norway, Sri Lanka, Sweden, Israel, Hong Kong, the Republic of Ireland, Ghana, Nigeria, England, Taiwan, France, Iran, and Turkey

## Education in Indonesia  
Rooted in Islamic Pondok Pesantren and Hindu-Buddhist traditions, Indonesian education was shaped by Dutch colonial segregation. Post-independence reforms established a secular system grounded in Pancasila ideology, featuring compulsory 12-year academic and vocational tracks. Public secular and private/religious schools coexist amid challenges like teacher shortages and funding gaps. Higher education is expansive but faces management inefficiencies. The academic calendar divides into two semesters.

## Education in Armenia  
Armenia’s predominantly public system blends Armenian heritage with global trends, featuring high preschool participation and strong higher education in sciences and IT. It aligns with Lisbon and Bologna frameworks and addresses absenteeism and dropout linked to child labor. Digital centers such as Tumo develop 21st-century skills.

## Education in Egypt  
Drawing from ancient and Islamic roots, Egypt’s compulsory centralized schooling includes Al-Azhar Muslim and international schools. Reforms target quality and teacher development, with COVID-19-induced distance learning. Persistent challenges include reliance on private tutoring, low teacher pay, corporal punishment, and youth unemployment.

## Education in Norway  
Mandatory schooling from elementary through upper secondary features academic and vocational tracks emphasizing social skills and inclusivity. Higher education is standardized via NOKUT with expanding specialized programs. The academic year employs semesters and national holidays.

## Education in Sri Lanka  
With Buddhist monastic and colonial legacies, Sri Lanka mandates schooling to Grade 9 with competitive university entry. The system includes private, international, and vocational institutions. Critiques highlight intense competition and rigidity; military training was phased out.

## Education in Sweden  
Sweden provides preschool, compulsory schooling, and upper secondary Gymnasium with preparatory and vocational paths facilitating university access. The Bologna Process and ECTS support higher education. Independent “Friskolor” coexist with public schools. Variable PISA scores reflect immigration and assessment challenges.

## Education in Israel  
Compulsory education spans kindergarten to 12th grade through multiple sectors: state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, Arab, private, and integrated. Integration challenges stem from growing Haredi and Arab populations. High STEM results contrast with variable matriculation outcomes. Post-secondary military service is common. Affirmative policies and funding target Arab education gaps amid gender disparities and teacher strikes. PISA scores show moderate overall performance with disparities.

## Education in Hong Kong  
Originating from missionary and village schools, Hong Kong’s bilingual/bicultural system emphasizes English Medium Instruction. Subsidized schools expanded post-1970s. Recent reforms stress patriotism and national security. Education progresses from kindergarten to senior secondary with HKDSE exams; IB programs and vocational education coexist. Eight UGC-funded English-language universities operate. Special education trends toward inclusion with immigrant support. International schools offer diverse curricula despite rote-learning critiques.

## Education in the Republic of Ireland  
Ireland offers free upper secondary education with compulsory schooling from ages 6–16. Primary and secondary education is English-medium with growing non-denominational options supplanting religious schools. Key exams include Junior and Leaving Certificates, supporting above-average OECD results. Special Needs Education and disadvantaged-targeted programs receive centralized support. Ireland hosts high international student enrollment; COVID-19 closures affected schooling.

## Education in Ghana  
Transitioning from informal to formal systems, Ghana mandates free 12-year basic education ending with the BECE. Senior high offers academic and vocational tracks culminating in the WASSCE. Tertiary admissions require this exam; graduates undertake compulsory National Service. Private schools outnumber public, with partnerships augmenting resources. Decentralization proceeds under the Ministry of Education. TVET remains largely informal and poorly regarded. Scholarships promote tertiary equity; COVID-19 exposed digital divides. The academic year runs August–May with English as the medium.

## Education in Nigeria  
Nigeria’s 9-3-4 system includes nine years free Universal Basic Education, three years senior secondary, and four years tertiary. Education begins at age five with core and indigenous languages. Secondary offers academic and vocational tracks with variable teacher qualifications. Private and religious schools often have better resources but higher fees. The SSCE is mandatory for tertiary entry. Tertiary education includes 153 universities with a post-1993 private university boom. Vocational education faces shortages, outdated curricula, and political challenges. Informal and NGO literacy efforts are underfunded. Female education is emphasized amid sociocultural barriers. Rural education suffers infrastructural and security deficits; adult literacy remains low in northern regions. Digital literacy targets 95% by 2030 with UNESCO aid.

## Education in England  
Evolving from religious origins, England mandates education ages 5–18 via Early Years and Key Stages (KS1–KS5). The National Curriculum mandates core and foundation subjects, with compulsory arts, humanities, or languages at KS4. Post-16 education offers sixth forms, further education colleges, technical qualifications (A-levels, BTEC, NVQs, T Levels), and apprenticeships. School types include state academies, community, free, foundation, voluntary aided/controlled, selective, and private independent schools (~7% attendance). Home education is legal but unfunded. State schools provide supports and are inspected by Ofsted. Higher education features 3-year degrees, capped fees (~£9,535 from 2024–25), loans, bursaries, and Russell Group universities. Adult education encompasses vocational, access, distance (Open University), and recreational courses. The UK ranks well internationally; funding boosts support for SEND and deprived areas.

## Education in Taiwan  
Taiwan integrates Dutch, Chinese, and Japanese influences centralized post-1949. Twelve years of compulsory schooling span primary through senior high, semesterized. Corporal punishment is banned but occurs occasionally. Curricula cover literature, math, sciences, social studies, and arts. Senior high offers scientific, liberal arts, and skill-based tracks with military-supervised discipline. Higher education includes universities, junior colleges, and institutes of technology offering degrees up to doctoral level. The Teaching Excellence Universities Award recognizes top institutions. Significant private education expands preschool enrollment. The "3+3" system allows private bypass of junior high entrance exams. Cram schools emphasize English, math, and physics amid debate. Special education supports ages 2–22. Taiwan attracts many mainland Chinese students.

## Education in France  
France mandates free, secular education ages 3 to 16, with a centrally governed system emphasizing national identity. The school year runs September to July with standardized breaks. Preschool fosters early literacy; elementary school features all-subject teaching. Secondary includes collège and lycée preparing for the baccalauréat or vocational CAP, leading to higher education or employment. Private schools exist, mostly religious. France hosts numerous international schools regulated by AEFE.

Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, featuring Licence, Master, Doctorate cycles, selective grandes écoles for leaders, large public universities, and University Technical Institutes offering vocational degrees. Post-1968 decentralization created smaller universities with collaborative graduate programs. Public higher education has low tuition for EU students; higher fees exist for others. Public schools are strictly secular with a 2004 law banning visible religious symbols, generating controversy. Teachers are highly qualified and differentiated. France’s school population is large with low violence.

## Education in Iran  
Iran’s ancient system is centrally governed, with optional pre-primary (~50% enrollment), six years free elementary (not yet universal), compulsory lower secondary, and mostly optional upper secondary split into vocational and theoretical tracks (~80% enrollment). Post-1979 Islamization introduced gender segregation, Islamic curricula, and religious observances. Persian is the main instructional language; Arabic and English are taught. The Education Organization for Research and Planning manages curriculum and assessment.

Teacher education is centralized under Farhangian University but faces shortages (~60,000 vacancies). Digital infrastructure supports hybrid learning and distance education. Higher education enrolls about 4.5 million students, with admission via national exam, and includes a major private Islamic Azad University. Women make up over 70% of university students and engineering graduates. Gifted education through selective NODET (SAMPAD) schools is competitive. Education expenditure is roughly 5% of GDP. Near-universal primary enrollment and rising secondary rates are noted. Reforms emphasize Islamic values, research, and family involvement. Controversies include political control and recent education protests. Despite privatization concerns, the state invests strongly.

## Education in Turkey  
Turkey centralized and secularized education following Republic reforms abolishing Islamic medrese schools and unifying diverse channels. Compulsory education expanded in stages to 12 years ("4+4+4" system) covering primary, middle (general or Imam Hatip religious vocational), and secondary education starting at age 6, compulsory until 14. Pre-primary caters to ages 3–6. Primary education is free in public schools, emphasizing Turkish, math, science, social studies, and foreign languages, mainly English.

Secondary education offers general, vocational, and technical tracks, with national exams for university admission; international programs include the IB since 1994. Vocational education blends formal schooling and apprenticeships. Higher education encompasses about 167 universities enrolling over one million students; admission is exam-based. Public universities are affordable; private ones costly. Technical universities hold some ABET accreditations. University governance involves multiple state bodies. Turkey participates in Erasmus programs and hosts many international students, supported by government scholarships.

Research oversight by TÜBİTAK and TÜBA focuses on key fields like agriculture, health, biotech, nuclear, IT, and defense. Private schools include Turkish, minority, foreign, and international institutions. "Dershane" prep centers converted to academic high schools by 2015.

Religious education, removed in 1927, was reintroduced from 1949 through 1980 and is now taught from 4th grade through high school, covering Islam, other religions, secularism, and ethics, using approved flexible materials. Creationism was controversially added in 1985. English is taught from 2nd grade, with a second foreign language introduced in high school; private schools generally offer stronger English. Efforts to improve English include hiring foreign assistants. Minority languages (Kurdish, Abkhaz, Adyghe, Georgian, Laz, Albanian, Bosnian) have been optional courses since 2012.

Since 2016–17, Arabic was added as an elective second language from second grade alongside German, French, and English, with progressive skill development.

The system emphasizes Turkish nationalism rooted in Atatürk’s principles, promoting laique and democratic values. Nationalistic content includes denial of the Armenian genocide and patriotic symbols in classrooms. Frequent curriculum and exam changes spark controversy, including reductions in Atatürk content and increased religious material. State textbooks face criticism for low quality, prompting unofficial private book use despite bans. History education is biased, reflecting state-backed denial of the Armenian genocide and negative portrayals of Armenians.

The Treaty of Lausanne protects minority education languages like Hebrew but excludes others such as Judaeo-Spanish and French, leading to diminished minority-language schooling. Religious instruction was outlawed by 1928, impacting Jewish minority schools that adapted to mainstream curricula.
