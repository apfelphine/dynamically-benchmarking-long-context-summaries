### **Expanded Comparative Analysis: France, Iran, and Turkey**

**France: Secularism, Hierarchy, and Diversity**
Rooted in Napoleonic principles and Jules Ferry's laws, France enforces strict secularism (*laïcité*), including bans on conspicuous religious symbols in public schools. The system features a rigid teacher hierarchy: primary teachers hold master's degrees and work ~28 hours/week, while secondary *Collège* teachers pass the CAPES exam and *Lycée* teachers (*Agrégés*) undergo competitive exams for higher pay and fewer hours. Higher education offers diverse pathways from accessible public universities to elite *grandes écoles* and IUTs. With 18 million pupils (25% of the population), the system historically evolved through milestones like the establishment of 36 secondary colleges for women by 1896 and the fragmentation of universities post-May 1968. Tuition is low for EU citizens but higher for internationals, supported by significant scholarships.

**Iran: Centralization, Ideology, and Challenges**
Iran's education system is defined by historical evolution and strong centralization under the Ministry of Education, with curriculum, assessment, and textbook production managed by the Organization for Educational Research and Planning (OERP). Teacher training is centralized at Farhangian University, and the curriculum emphasizes Persian, Arabic, and English. While pre-Islamic roots included the Academy of Gondishapur and the Pahlavi dynasty's Literacy Corps (1969–1979) improved literacy, the 1979 Revolution Islamized the system, enforcing sex segregation and integrating Islamic values. Despite high government spending (20% of the budget, 5% of GDP), significant gaps persist: millions of children drop out of K-12, and enrollment varies by region. The structure moves from optional pre-primary to compulsory lower-secondary, with competitive higher education based on the *Konkour* exam. Higher education, comprising over 90 institutions, is heavily influenced by Islamic supervision, with admission to public universities contingent on the national exam. Top institutions like Tehran University, Tarbiat Modares, and Sharif University are renowned for specific fields including theology, post-graduate studies, and engineering. Recent challenges include a teacher shortage (~60,000), lack of counselors in half of schools, and regional disparities. Recent developments include a 2023 mandate for half-hour daily Islamic prayers, a post-pandemic hybrid learning program, and 2024 laws restricting educational protests without referendums. A unique feature remains the "University Service," requiring graduates to serve the government or pay fees. Despite ideological constraints, Iran boasts high female enrollment in universities, high literacy rates (85% overall), and a growing focus on entrepreneurship, producing a significant number of STEM graduates. Unique competitive government-run schools like SAMPAD and Nemoone Dolati provide specialized education, while Internet access and distance learning have expanded significantly, with Payame Noor University leading in online education. Notable international schools include Mehr-e-Taban and Tehran International School.

**Turkey: Secularization, Modernization, and Structural Framework**
Following the 1923 establishment of the Republic, Turkey reorganized its education system to secularize it, merging religious and secular institutions under the Ministry of National Education. Key milestones include the adoption of the Latin alphabet in 1928, the closure of all *medreses* (Islamic schools) in 1924, and the expansion of compulsory education from five to 12 years. The system serves approximately 14.6 million students across pre-primary through vocational levels, though the structure underwent a major reform in March 2012, implementing the "4+4+4" system: four years of primary, four years of middle school, and four years of secondary education. Middle school offers a choice between general education or religious vocational (*Imam Hatip*) schools.

*   **Primary Education:** Consists of eight uninterrupted years (ages 6–14), divided into "First School" (4 years) and "Second Level" (4 years), covering Turkish, Mathematics, Science/Social Studies, and a foreign language.
*   **Secondary Education:** Lasts four years with tracks in General High Schools (Turkish-Mathematics, Science, Social Sciences, or Foreign Languages) and Vocational/Technical Schools. Students must pass a finishing exam to sit for the University Entrance Exam (YÖS), with admission scores weighted by track.
*   **Vocational Education:** Prepares individuals for employment or higher education, including schools for various trades and special education, plus a separate apprenticeship system for those leaving formal education.
*   **International Education:** The International Baccalaureate (IB) has been available since 1994, with 53 schools currently offering programs.

**Foreign Languages and Curriculum Evolution**
Language policy in Turkey emphasizes English, which is taught from 2nd grade in public schools, though standards often lead students to take intensive "prep years" at university. Private schools offer more hours and native speakers. Optional languages include Kurdish, Albanian, Bosnian, and various Caucasian languages (2013–2017). Recently, Arabic was introduced as an elective in elementary schools starting in 2016, alongside German, French, and English. Religious instruction was removed in 1927, re-introduced in 1949 for primary students, and expanded to secondary schools in 1956 following multi-party democracy. It became constitutionally secured in 1980 after a military coup and is currently taught from 4th grade through high school (8 continuous years), covering Islam, secularism, ethics, and other world religions. Controversies have arisen regarding curriculum content; notably, the 2017 changes removed evolution theory and added Islamic concepts, leading to Turkey ranking last in evolution knowledge among industrialized nations. Furthermore, historical instruction often promotes denialism regarding the Armenian genocide, attributing blame to external actors or Armenians themselves.

**Higher Education and Private Sector**
Governed by the Higher Education Council, the system includes 167 universities (public and private) and over 820 higher education institutions with over 1 million students. Historically, the 1930s saw the hiring of over 1,000 exiled German academics to strengthen science and medicine. Entry requires passing the national university entrance exam (YÖS). Public universities charge low fees, while private foundation universities can cost up to $30,000 annually. Public university rectors are jointly appointed by the faculty, the Council, and the President. Technical universities are internationally recognized and accredited by ABET. Turkey acts as a hub for foreign students (approx. 796,000 in 2016), hosting over 500,000 under a government scholarship plan. Private schools are categorized into four groups: private Turkish schools, schools for minorities (Greek, Armenian, Jewish), private foreign schools (French, German, etc.), and international education institutions. Additionally, traditional private high schools (*dershanes*) were legally required to transform into academic high schools in 2015.

**Systemic Criticism and Minority Rights**
The Turkish system faces frequent criticism regarding curriculum changes, entrance exams, and textbook quality. Many families are compelled to purchase private textbooks to supplement state materials, which are sometimes criticized for low quality or political bias. While the Treaty of Lausanne guaranteed educational rights for minorities, subsequent laws banning religious instruction and shifting curricula led to the decline of Jewish schools, forcing them to adopt mainstream Turkish education models. This systemic instability and ideological pressure contrast with the more stable, albeit hierarchical, structures seen in France and Iran.
