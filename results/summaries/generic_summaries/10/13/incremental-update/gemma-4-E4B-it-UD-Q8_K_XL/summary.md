Global education systems display significant international variation in structure, centralization, and cultural emphasis.

**National System Highlights:**
*   **Taiwan** mandates 12 years of schooling, supporting high university attendance through skill-based exams and cram schools.
*   **England** operates under a National Curriculum (ages 5-18) with varied post-16 routes (Sixth Form, FE colleges, apprenticeships), enhanced by the Academies program.
*   **France** adheres to its tradition of free, secular public instruction (Jules Ferry Laws), aiming for a "common culture" culminating in the *baccalauréat*; elite entry into *grandes écoles* relies on intensive preparatory classes ($\text{CPGE}$s).
*   **Iran** features a highly centralized system reflecting the 1979 revolution, integrating Islamic values, achieving high literacy despite teacher shortages, and utilizing gender segregation under OERP.

**Turkey's System:**
Turkey employs a "4+4+4" model (primary and secondary education), offering choices between general and religious vocational tracks (Imam Hatip). Higher education involves about 820 institutions accessed via a national entrance exam, with public universities offering low fees while private ones are costly; technical universities are often internationally competitive. The system emphasizes Turkish nationalism derived from Atatürk's ideology while promoting *laïcité*. Education requires mandatory religion from the 4th grade onward, with English being the primary foreign language, though Arabic is now available as an elective.

**Challenges and Criticisms:**
A key point of discussion is the Turkish system's instability due to frequent curriculum and examination changes. Furthermore, state-provided textbooks face criticism regarding quality and ideological bias, notably concerning history texts that allegedly deny the Armenian genocide or shift blame. Historically, the guarantees for religious and minority education made by the Treaty of Lausanne have been curtailed, affecting Jewish minority schools.
