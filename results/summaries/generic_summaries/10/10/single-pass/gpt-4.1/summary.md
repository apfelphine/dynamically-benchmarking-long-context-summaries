# Education Systems Summary

---

## Israel

**Structure and Schools:**  
Israel’s education system is tiered into primary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), and high school (10–12), with compulsory education up to grade 12. Schools are divided into four tracks—state-secular, state-religious, independent Haredi (ultra-Orthodox Jewish), and Arab—plus private and integrated schools, with growing Haredi and Arab pupil populations.

**Society and Outcomes:**  
Education is highly valued and strongly linked to socioeconomic status; about 46% of Israeli Jews have post-secondary degrees. Matriculation exams (bagrut) are critical for university admission and employment, but only 46% achieve the full certificate. Higher education is subsidized, admissions are based on bagrut and psychometric tests, and Israeli universities have strong global rankings in science and engineering.

**Challenges:**  
There are disparities between Jewish and Arab sectors in funding and results. Teacher salaries are low, causing morale issues, and there are frequent strikes. Efforts exist to close gaps, especially for Arab and Haredi sectors, and gender disparities persist, notably in STEM fields.

## Sri Lanka

**Governance and History:**  
Education is administered by several ministries; the system has a history dating back 2300 years with significant British colonial influence. Free, formal education was established in 1938. Schools are state-run, religious (Buddhist, Hindu, Muslim, Christian), and private.

**Structure:**  
Education comprises primary (grades 1–5), junior secondary (6–9), senior secondary (10–11 for O/Ls), collegiate (12–13 for A/Ls), and tertiary levels. Government education is compulsory to grade 9.

**Higher and Vocational Education:**  
State university admissions are highly competitive—only 16% of applicants enter. There is a growing private tertiary sector and international schools. Vocational education is overseen by governmental commissions and technical colleges.

**Issues:**  
Criticism focuses on high competitiveness and rigidity. Private universities face resistance. There are also concerns regarding the negative impact of excessive tutoring, and a now-discontinued compulsory military-style training for university entrants.

## Republic of Ireland

**Structure:**  
Mandatory schooling runs from ages 6 to 16, with primary (eight years) and secondary (Junior and Senior Cycles, plus optional Transition Year). Schools are mainly state-funded, often operated under religious patronage, though diversity in ethos is growing.

**Higher Education:**  
Ireland has internationally ranked universities; higher education is largely state-funded, and the country is attractive to international students. Transition rates to tertiary education are high.

**Issues and Equity:**  
Class sizes, teacher wages, and performance gaps persist; education is increasingly viewed critically for disparities. DEIS programs target disadvantaged schools.

## Egypt

**Historical Development:**  
Education is deeply historic, with roots in ancient temples and medieval Islamic institutions (notably Al-Azhar). Modern reforms began under Muhammad Ali, but British colonial neglect restricted broad access.

**System Today:**  
Highly centralized, divided into basic education (nine years), secondary (general, technical, and applied technology), and extensive higher education. Public education is free, but private and international schools are prevalent.

**Challenges:**  
Quality concerns include reliance on rote learning, infrastructure shortages, and teacher underqualification. Private tutoring is widespread, and wealth gaps affect attainment. Vocational education is being expanded, but youth unemployment remains high.

## Nigeria

**Structure:**  
The 6-3-3-4 system covers six years of primary, three of junior secondary, three of senior secondary, and four in higher education. Universal Basic Education aims to ensure education for all, with promotion and graduation through standardized exams (SSCE, WAEC, NECO).

**Types of Schools and Higher Education:**  
Federal, state, private, and international schools coexist, with Federal schools holding higher reputations. Universities include federal, state, and private institutions, and entry is competitive. Vocational and informal education have increasing policy attention.

**Issues:**  
Persistent gender gaps, regional disparities, teacher shortages, low literacy rates, and challenges for rural education remain, although improvements are ongoing.

## Indonesia

**History and Structure:**  
Indonesia’s education evolved from Islamic and colonial Dutch systems to a national framework post-independence. Compulsory education spans twelve years, with high enrollment in primary but lower rates in upper secondary and tertiary education.

**School Types:**  
Public, private, and Islamic schools (pesantren, madrasah, sekolah Islam) operate, with vocational tracks available. Higher education is provided by universities, institutes, polytechnics, and academies.

**Challenges:**  
Quality and equity issues persist, particularly in remote areas, and teacher certification and resources vary widely.

## Taiwan

**Structure:**  
A twelve-year compulsory system covers primary, junior, and senior high, with national curricula and competitive exams for advancement. Vocational and skill-based tracks exist alongside traditional academics.

**Higher Education:**  
Numerous universities and colleges, both public and private, are present. Entrance is highly competitive. Vocational institutions offer associate to doctoral degrees.

**Trends:**  
Cram schools (buxiban) are common, reflecting a culture of high academic pressure. Special and adult education provisions are expanding. Language immersion (especially English) is growing in popularity.

## Armenia

**Structure:**  
Compulsory, free twelve-year education includes preschool, primary, middle (basic), and high school. Literacy is near-universal, with major reforms post-Soviet era emphasizing Armenian history and culture.

**Higher Education:**  
The country has a robust tertiary sector, with numerous universities offering courses in a variety of fields. International agreements (e.g., Bologna Process, Erasmus+) support modern standards and mobility.

**Issues:**  
Student absenteeism and drop-out rates are linked to child labor and educational quality. There is also a range of after-school and technology-focused learning centers.

## Iran

**History and Structure:**  
Education has ancient roots and was heavily reformed after the 1979 revolution to emphasize Islamic values. The system is highly centralized, with compulsory primary and lower secondary education, and competitive entry to upper secondary and university.

**Higher Education:**  
Admission is based chiefly on national exams. Universities are state or private (notably Azad University), with strong STEM graduation rates. Women dominate enrollment in higher education.

**Issues:**  
Privatization has led to disparities in quality and access. Teacher shortages, political constraints, and syllabus changes are ongoing concerns. Efforts are being made to modernize methods (hybrid learning) and expand foreign language offerings.

## Norway

**Structure:**  
Divided into elementary (barneskole), lower (ungdomsskole), and upper secondary (videregående), with compulsory education covering ages 6–16. Secondary education takes either general or vocational paths, and IT is integrated across curricula.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities, university colleges, and private colleges offer post-secondary education, with admission typically requiring a general university admissions certificate. Quality assurance is managed by NOKUT.

**Special Education:**  
Inclusive policies ensure local schooling for students with special needs. Grading varies from numeric and letter-based systems to ECTS compatibility in higher education.

## Turkey

**History and Structure:**  
A secular, centralized system since reforms in the early 20th century, with twelve years of compulsory schooling (4+4+4). Education covers pre-primary, primary (8 years), and secondary (high school) and is free in public institutions.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities, faculties, and vocational schools comprise the tertiary sector, admission is by entrance exams. The private sector is increasingly present, and Turkey is a major international student destination.

**Issues:**  
Frequent curriculum changes and politicization (e.g., religious courses, nationalism) are sources of debate. Inequality and textbook quality also present challenges.

## Sweden

**Structure:**  
Mandatory nine-year comprehensive school (grundskola) is followed by optional three-year upper secondary (gymnasieskola) with vocational and preparatory programs. Grading has shifted to a letter system, and national standardized tests are administered.

**Higher Education:**  
Degrees conform to Bologna Process cycles—bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral levels offered at universities and university colleges. Independent/friskolor schools receive public funding; their impact and oversight are subjects of debate.

**Trends:**  
Sweden scores at or above international averages in student assessments (PISA, PIRLS). Reforms focus on improving standards amid rising educational inequality.

## France

**Structure:**  
Highly centralized and secular, compulsory education spans ages 6–16, with pre-primary, primary, collège (middle school), and lycée (high school). The curriculum is national, and large numbers of students transition into higher education with university or grande école options.

**Higher Education:**  
Three-tiered system (licence/master/doctorat) compatible with Bologna standards; low tuition is underwritten by the state. Prestigious grandes écoles and highly selective preparatory classes (CPGE) train elites for specialized subjects.

**Issues:**  
Laïcité (secularism) guides curriculum, and selective recruitment for teachers is standard. Diversity in private and international schools supplements the public system.

## Hong Kong

**Structure:**  
Education is compulsory from age 6 to 18, with pre-school, primary, junior secondary and senior secondary culminating in the HKDSE. The system features government, aided, private, DSS, and international schools.

**Higher Education:**  
Several internationally ranked universities exist. Undergraduate programmes shifted from three to four years in 2012. English is the medium of instruction.

**Issues:**  
Recent years saw moves toward "patriotic" curricula and increased censorship. International and private schools are growing, and there is an increasing trend of students and teachers leaving the territory. Cram schools are popular, and small class sizes have become more common due to declining birth rates.

## England

**Structure:**  
Compulsory education spans ages 5–18, with primary, secondary, and post-16 (sixth-form, college, or apprenticeship) options. Schools include state-funded (comprehensive, grammar, academy, free, faith, technical) and private/independent sectors.

**National Curriculum and Exams:**  
Defined Key Stages guide subjects and standards; GCSEs cap age 16, followed by A-levels or vocational alternatives. Inspection and standards are managed by Ofsted.

**Higher Education:**  
Degrees are typically 3–4 years, with postgraduate options. Tuition fees are high but offset by loans and bursaries, with increased outreach for disadvantaged students.

**Issues:**  
There is ongoing debate about funding, access, standards, private/public divides, and educational equity. England performs above average in international assessments.

## Ghana

**History and Structure:**  
From informal, indigenous education to mission and colonial schools, Ghana today mandates 12 years of basic education (kindergarten, primary, junior high), followed by secondary (senior high, vocational), and tertiary (universities, polytechnics).

**Challenges:**  
Efforts have increased enrollment and literacy, but vocational education struggles with poor reputation and employment outcomes. Tertiary admission is competitive and reflects socioeconomic gaps. Private and international schools are influential; ICT integration is ongoing.

**Governance:**  
Education is overseen by the Ministry of Education, with decentralization and substantial donor support shaping policy. Literacy and gender equity remain key targets, and recent reforms address system efficiency and universal access.
