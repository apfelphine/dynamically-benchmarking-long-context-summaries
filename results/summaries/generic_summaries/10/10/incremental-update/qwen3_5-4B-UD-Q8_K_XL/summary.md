### Education in Ghana: Structure, Progress, and Challenges

**Historical Context and Performance**
Ghana's educational landscape has evolved from colonial foundations to a universal system prioritized by the state, historically allocating ~25% of its budget. Key milestones include the rise of literacy from 54% (1997) to 79.04% (2018) and a high primary net enrollment rate of 84%. Despite these gains, the system faces persistent hurdles, including a gender gap, declining vocational prestige, and economic barriers.

**System Structure and Curriculum**
The curriculum is divided into three sectors:
*   **Basic Education:** A 12-year cycle (Kindergarten to Junior High) requiring the Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE).
*   **Secondary Education:** Three years of Senior High School (SHS) concluding with the WASSCE. Students select streams including Agriculture, Arts/Science, Business, and Vocational/Technical. While 90% of vocational training remains informal, Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) exists within the formal system, though it suffers from low completion rates, poor infrastructure, and a lack of official certification, damaging its reputation.
*   **Tertiary Education:** Comprises 6 public universities, 49 private institutions, 10 polytechnics (offering 3-year HNDs), and Colleges of Education. Admission typically requires a maximum of 24 WASSCE points.

**Admissions, Financing, and Governance**
Admissions require a PIN/serial number fee; tertiary schools primarily use GPA, while SHS relies on WASSCE scores. Financing is a shared responsibility: the government covers teacher salaries, free textbooks (P1–6), and infrastructure, whereas parents pay user fees, furniture, food, and transport. Donor funding has significantly declined from 8.5% to 2.5% of total expenditure (2005–2012). Governance is centralized under the Ministry of Education but decentralized for implementation, supported by agencies like the Ghana Education Service (GES) and National Council for Curriculum and Assessment (NCCA).

**Equity, Technology, and Current Challenges**
Equity remains a critical issue in tertiary education, characterized by high competition and disparities based on gender, socioeconomic status, and geography. Although initiatives like the "Girls Education unit" and online scholarships aim to reduce these inequalities, wealthier males remain disproportionately represented. Furthermore, the integration of ICT is hampered by limited infrastructure, high costs, and a gap between policy and practice. The system continues to face disruptions, such as those during the pandemic, and adult literacy programs like the National Functional Literacy Programme (FNLP) target rural and female populations to meet historical elimination goals.
