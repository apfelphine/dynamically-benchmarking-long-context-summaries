# Summary: Education Systems in Israel, Sri Lanka, the Republic of Ireland, Egypt, Nigeria, Indonesia, Taiwan, Armenia, Iran, Norway, Turkey, Sweden, France, Hong Kong, England, and Ghana

## Israel  
Education spans primary to grade 12 across diverse sectors—state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab—with vocational training addressing gaps. Efforts promote Jewish-Arab integration and anti-discrimination. Universities require matriculation and psychometric tests; nine public institutions focus on STEM, supported by military incentives. Challenges include large classes, moderate international scores, Arab underfunding, and STEM gender gaps.

## Sri Lanka  
Compulsory education extends to grade 9 in government, Buddhist monastic, private, international, madrasa, and semi-governmental schools. Exam pressure fuels private tutoring and online lessons. Higher education is free but selective amid quality debates.

## Republic of Ireland  
Mandatory education (ages 6–16) mainly in English, with Irish promoted through Gaelscoileanna and reforms reducing Catholic dominance. Schools include Catholic national, Gaelscoileanna, multidenominational, and minority-faith types. Secondary culminates in the Leaving Certificate. Support exists for special needs and disadvantaged pupils.

## Egypt  
Compulsory education (ages 4–14) is centralized but challenged by rote teaching, teacher shortages, underfunding, unofficial fees, and socioeconomic disparities. Vocational education is state-backed; higher education enrols ~30% facing quality and resource issues. Private English-language schools use international curricula.

## Nigeria  
Universal Basic Education mandates primary and junior secondary schooling. University admission uses WAEC, GCE, and JAMB exams. Universities vary in quality; federal predominate. Vocational education and apprenticeships are under-resourced but vital. Female education improves despite cultural barriers; rural infrastructure lags; literacy and digital skills grow.

## Indonesia  
Education blends Islamic and Dutch colonial legacies promoting secular nationalism centered on Pancasila. Twelve years compulsory include secular, private, and religious schools blending religious and national content amid persistent rote learning. Challenges include teacher shortages and remote area disparities. Islamic schools include Pesantren, Madrasas, and Sekolah Islam. Higher education follows a national qualifications framework with academic and vocational paths.

## Taiwan  
Compulsory 12-year education with national exams governs transitions. Secondary divides into academic and vocational tracks; over 95% enter university, modeled on the US system. Vocational education, cram schools, special education, adult learning, and expanding kindergarten are notable. Taiwan is a regional Chinese language study hub.

## Armenia  
Post-Soviet reforms emphasize Armenian language and culture, with growing English and Chinese. Public schools dominate alongside variable private institutions. Secondary education is free and compulsory, offering general and specialized tracks. Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process. Non-formal technical education addresses rural youth skills and child labor. European partnerships support reforms.

## Iran  
Education reshaped after the 1979 Islamic Revolution features compulsory elementary and lower secondary education and Konkour exams. Significant spending prioritizes secondary and tertiary levels. Reforms enhance quality, vocational training, and critical thinking, merging Islamic and global knowledge. Hybrid/distance learning expanded post-pandemic. Persian is official; English primary teaching banned in 2018, increasing private demand. Public universities dominate; STEM emphasized with high global rankings and over 70% female enrollment. Gifted education thrives via selective schools. Political/religious discrimination persists; entrepreneurship grows. International Baccalaureate schools operate.

## Norway  
Education traces back millennia, formalized in compulsory folkeskole since 1889, divided into elementary, lower secondary, and upper secondary. Reforms merged tracks into flexible study paths and mandated IT and apprenticeships. 2017 upper secondary graduation reached 73%. Educators require degrees plus pedagogy. Higher education includes research universities, colleges, and private institutions (~10% enrollment). Special education emphasizes inclusion and tailored support.

## Turkey  
From fragmented religious/foreign schools post-1923 to centralized secular education with ~59,000 schools (2001) and 16 million students. Compulsory free 12-year education (4+4+4) includes expanding pre-primary. Primary focuses on Turkish, math, science, and foreign languages; secondary offers general, vocational, and technical tracks with choice at grade 11. Vocational education includes apprenticeships with legal protections. Private schools include Turkish, minority, foreign, and international types. Seventy-six universities enroll over 1 million students; technical universities hold ABET accreditation. Governance involves faculty councils and the Higher Education Council. Turkey participates in Erasmus and hosts many international students. Religious education was removed then reintroduced emphasizing Islamic ethics except in some private schools. Nationalist biases persist. Frequent reforms and low-quality textbooks lead to unofficial materials. Minority-language education is legally protected but limited in practice.

## Sweden  
Compulsory education (ages 6/7–15/16) follows a national curriculum focusing on literacy, math, and religion. Preschool accessible free part-time from age 3 promotes equality. Secondary offers national preparatory and vocational tracks; some attend introductory programs if not immediately qualified. Admission is grade- and exam-based. Higher education aligns with Bologna, offering academic, professional, and higher vocational degrees. Education is free for Swedish/EU/EEA/Swiss citizens. Universities ensure nondiscrimination. Independent “friskolor” (publicly funded, privately run) enroll ~10% with scrutiny over profit and grade inflation. Sweden ranks highly in early childhood education. PISA and PIRLS scores showed declines and modest recoveries; testing irregularities sparked debates.

## France  
Modern French education arose from 19th-century Jules Ferry laws establishing free, secular, compulsory elementary education fostering national identity. Centrally governed by the Ministry of National Education and Youth, compulsory since 2021 with uniform curriculum. Secular public schools except in Alsace/Moselle. A 2004 ban on conspicuous religious symbols sparked religious freedom debates. Population is ~67 million with 18 million students; stages include preschool, elementary, collège, and lycée leading to baccalauréat or vocational certificates. Private schools may be state-contracted or independent. Over 100 AEFE-regulated international schools offer English curricula. Higher education comprises public universities and elite grandes écoles with specialized tracks. Faculty combine research and teaching; CNRS supports research. Enrollment (2021) at 1.65 million mainly bachelor’s and master’s. EU/EAA students pay low tuition; private institutions charge more. Bologna system and ECTS apply. University Technical Institutes offer applied vocational degrees. Teachers include master’s-trained primary teachers, certified middle school teachers, and agrégés for high school and CPGE with stringent requirements.

## Hong Kong  
Evolved from traditional Chinese village schools to Western-style missionary education under British rule, blending bilingual studies. Post-1997 reforms introduced active learning; recent policies emphasize patriotism, national security, and mainland integration causing tensions. Compulsory education covers optional kindergarten (3–6), six years primary, three years junior secondary, and selective three-year senior secondary leading to HKDSE exams. Shifted from half- to full-day classes; declining birth rates reduced class sizes. Strict discipline per Education Bureau. Exam-focused culture combines rote learning and group project reforms. School types include Government, Aided, Direct Subsidy Scheme, Caput, Private, Private International, and English Schools Foundation. International schools (~175) offer UK and IB curricula; rising fees push some students to government schools. Higher education includes eight UGC-funded universities plus specialized and self-financing institutions. Undergraduate degrees extended to 4 years. English is main instruction language. Adult and special education emphasize inclusion. Hong Kong fosters international collaborations, including MIT innovation.

## England  
Education stems from medieval ecclesiastical origins expanded through charity and 19th–20th-century state acts establishing compulsory schooling. Currently compulsory ages 5–18: attendance mandatory until 16, then full-time education or training until 18 via study, apprenticeships, or volunteering. Pre-school (3–5) offers optional state-funded 600 hours/year. Stages include Foundation Stage and Key Stages 1–4 with a National Curriculum covering core and additional subjects; compulsory schooling ends at 16 with continued education or training to 18. Post-16 options include sixth form, further education colleges, apprenticeships, traineeships, and technical courses (e.g., A-levels, BTECs, NVQs, T Levels). Universities and colleges provide higher education with degrees from certificates to doctorates. Undergraduate fees capped at £9,535/year (from 2024–25) with loans and bursaries. Adult education includes vocational qualifications, access courses, distance learning, and community courses. National Qualifications Frameworks (RQF, FHEQ) structure certifications aligned with European frameworks. School types span state-funded (academies, community, free, foundation, voluntary aided/controlled, University Technical Colleges, maths schools, City Technology Colleges, and some boarding) and private schools (~7%). Home education is legal and parent-led. State schools publish governance and curriculum info; uniforms and free meals support inclusivity. Ofsted inspects schools; many secondary schools specialize and select pupils. The UK ranks 7th globally on the UN Education Index; British 15-year-olds ranked 13th globally in literacy, math, and science (PISA), with primary students 4th in reading (2021). City academies since 1997 improved school ratings to 88% good/outstanding by 2015 (from 68% in 2010). Schools funded via a national formula since 2018 with budget increases from 2020–23, including specific support for Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND).

## Ghana  
Ghana’s education reflects postcolonial expansion from early missionary and colonial roots to universal basic education and tertiary growth amid ongoing quality and vocational challenges. Government allocates about 25% of its budget to education; donor funding declined but still influences reforms. The formal system includes basic education (kindergarten to junior high, numerically graded; Basic Education Certificate Examination), secondary education (Senior High School academic and vocational tracks; WASSCE grading A1 to F9; bachelor’s entry requires ≤24 points), and tertiary education (universities, polytechnics; online applications with PIN fee; GPA varies by institution). Private schools, now outnumbering public ones in some districts, complement provision via public-private partnerships with government funding teachers/infrastructure and communities paying fees. Governance is decentralized across 16 regions and 230 districts under the Ministry of Education and agencies like Ghana Education Service and National Schools Inspectorate Authority. Teacher education includes 46 public Colleges of Education awarding diplomas, universities for secondary/tertiary teaching, and distance education addressing rural shortages. Adult literacy campaigns since the 1940s, intensified post-independence, focus on women and rural areas via NFED and FLSP programs. Literacy improved markedly (54% in 1997 to 71.5% in 2010), especially for females (36% to 65%), though retention and gender gaps remain.

Adult and youth literacy rates rose to over 89% youth literacy by 2015. NFED provides life-skills, occupational training, and civil awareness for illiterate and unemployed adults. Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) remains predominantly informal, reliant on apprenticeships lacking formal certification, facing low completion, insufficiently trained instructors, inadequate infrastructure, and weak stakeholder reputation. Since 2006, COTVET coordinates policies, introduces qualifications frameworks, and supports apprenticeships, though impact is limited by fragmentation and data gaps.  

In tertiary education, increased secondary enrollment heightens university competition, reducing acceptance and exposing equity gaps by gender, location, and socioeconomic status. Women and rural students remain underrepresented; financial barriers and social factors like early marriage contribute. Government initiatives including scholarships, lowered entry requirements, and a Girls Education unit raised female tertiary representation from 25% to 32% between 1999 and 2005, yet access favors wealthier males. ICT policies mandate integration across all education levels, with many schools equipped with computer labs; however, actual pedagogical use lags, focusing more on operational skills than subject learning enhancement. In 2020, the Ghana Scholarship Secretariat launched an online application system to improve scholarship access and efficiency.

## Cross-Cutting Themes  
Education systems reflect diverse historical, cultural, linguistic, and religious legacies with varied governance and mixed public-private provision. Common challenges include equitable access, quality enhancement, teacher training, gender disparities, and labor market alignment through vocational and higher education reforms. Many engage international frameworks, scholarships, and partnerships. Taiwan and Norway model advanced vocational and higher education; Turkey balances secularism and nationalism amid expansion. Sweden emphasizes early childhood education and publicly funded independent schools. France upholds secular identity via centralized schooling, elite grandes écoles, and tiered teaching careers. Hong Kong blends bilingual bicultural traditions but faces politicization and mainland integration. England evolved from medieval roots to comprehensive, inclusive, state-funded systems with diverse school types, extended compulsory education to 18, and historic advances in women’s higher education. Ghana reflects postcolonial expansion with growing private sector participation, decentralized governance, increased literacy, ongoing vocational education challenges, and expanding ICT integration.
