## Summary

Education systems worldwide—including those in Israel, Sri Lanka, Ireland, Egypt, Nigeria, Indonesia, Taiwan, Armenia, Iran, Norway, Turkey, Sweden, France, Hong Kong, England, and Ghana—reflect unique historical, cultural, and policy contexts but converge on challenges and priorities related to reform, equity, curriculum relevance, workforce preparation, exam orientation, inclusion, and adaptation to contemporary needs.

### Country Comparisons

- **Israel:** Multi-track compulsory education serves secular, religious, Haredi, and Arab groups, addressing disparities, STEM gaps, and inclusion challenges.
- **Sri Lanka:** Highly exam-oriented, with competitive university access pushing many graduates toward vocational and private sectors.
- **Ireland:** Inclusive curriculum spans denominational, language, and secular schools, emphasizing special education and ongoing secularization.
- **Egypt:** Centralized, underfunded system faces shortages, discipline concerns, youth unemployment, and continuous reform efforts.
- **Nigeria:** Nine years compulsory education, challenged by regional/gender disparities, curriculum gaps, and growing private sector involvement.
- **Indonesia:** Twelve-year compulsory system integrates state and religious schools, focusing on equity and teacher improvement.
- **Taiwan:** Competitive system offering academic/vocational tracks, digital learning, disciplinary reforms, and evolving university access.
- **Armenia:** State-funded with focus on literacy, heritage, modernization, and absenteeism concerns.
- **Iran:** Centralized, Islamized curriculum yields high literacy and substantial female university participation, with NODET schools, privatization, and persistent inequality.
- **Norway:** Ten-year compulsory, skills-oriented education offers vocational/general tracks and inclusive higher education.
- **Turkey:** Unified system includes general, Imam Hatip, and minority schools; politicized curriculum, compulsory religion, minority rights debates, and vocational/international expansion.
- **Sweden:** Universal preschool, nine-year inclusive grundskola, national assessments, low violence, and free EU/EEA higher education.
- **France:** Centralized, secular system with national curriculum, selective academic/vocational pathways, and highly qualified staff.

### England

England provides a broad curriculum in state and private schools, diverse qualifications (GCSEs, A-levels, BTECs, T Levels, IB), and compulsory education until 16, training until 18. Reforms expanded academies, improving rankings and funding, especially for special needs. Higher education, starting at 18, offers financial aid for disadvantaged students. Historically, a shift from elitist, church-led models to mass, inclusive schooling marks England’s educational evolution.

### Hong Kong

Hong Kong’s education includes public, aided, DSS, private, international, and ESF schools, adhering to a 3-6-3-3 structure culminating in the HKDSE exam. While exam-oriented and disciplined, recent reforms support group learning and curriculum change. Instruction is mainly in Cantonese, with increasing English/Mandarin options. Recent policy emphasizes patriotism, national security, preschool subsidies, AI integration, and special needs support.

### Ghana

Ghana dedicates about 25% of its budget to education, governed by the Ministry of Education and various agencies. The system covers basic, secondary, and expanding tertiary levels, taught mainly in English, with decentralized policy implementation. Public-private partnerships address government funding gaps, and international organizations provide additional support. Teacher training is mainly through colleges/universities, with distance options for rural educators. Enrollment rates are relatively high, basic education is free and compulsory, but formal vocational education participation is declining. National Service is required for graduates, and COVID-19 prompted virtual learning amid notable challenges.

Recent improvements in adult and youth literacy stem from government and non-formal initiatives, such as NFED’s life-skills and occupational programs. Technical and vocational education (TVET) faces persistent difficulties: low completion rates, limited infrastructure, and reputational issues, even as COTVET and micro-credit schemes aim to strengthen the sector. Tertiary education access remains inequitable; women, rural, and poor students are underrepresented, with ongoing efforts—like scholarships, relaxed entry requirements, and girls’ education initiatives—yet socioeconomic and cultural barriers persist. ICT integration is a national priority, but gaps continue between policy intent and classroom practice.

### Cross-Regional Themes

Globally, education systems strive to balance inclusive and selective pathways, align curricula with workforce demands, address exam-centered cultures, improve funding and equity, enhance special needs support, expand lifelong learning, and adapt to socioeconomic and technological change. Decentralization, public-private partnerships, targeted literacy programs, and increased ICT integration are key strategies to address persistent educational inequities and challenges.
