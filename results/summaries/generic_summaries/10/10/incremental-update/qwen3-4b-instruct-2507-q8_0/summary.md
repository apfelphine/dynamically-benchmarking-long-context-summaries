# **Final Working Summary (Incorporating All Chunks 1–45)**

Israel’s education system is structured into three tiers—primary, middle, and high school—with compulsory education from kindergarten to 12th grade. It features four distinct tracks: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab, each with unique curricula and cultural emphases. Central to the system is the *Bagrut* matriculation exam, a critical gateway to university admission, military service (*atuda* programs), and employment. High matriculation rates are observed in urban centers, with women outperforming men in exam outcomes (62% vs. 51%) and dominating university enrollment (56.7%) and graduate studies (59.3% in Master’s, 52.4% in doctorates). Despite strong tertiary attainment—ranking second in OECD countries at 46%—Israel spends less per student than Norway and Mexico, and performance in PISA reveals gaps, particularly in math and science. Arab students face systemic underfunding, higher dropout rates, unequal access to resources, and limited higher education opportunities, though targeted efforts—including affirmative action and a five-year development plan—aim to improve equity. Nonprofit programs like Aluma’s Ruad support Arab, Druze, and Circassian students, while textbook content has been criticized for promoting negative Arab images. Teacher wages remain low, and quality is often prioritized over funding, with reforms blocked by unions. Education is widely valued as a path to socioeconomic mobility in Israel’s high-tech economy, where nearly half of Israeli Jews hold post-secondary degrees and average 11.6 years of schooling. However, about 15% of *Bagrut* certificates fail to meet university standards, highlighting persistent quality and equity challenges.

France’s higher education system is highly selective, centered on preparatory classes (CPGEs) that serve as gateways to elite institutions (*grandes écoles*). Only 5% of applicants gain entry, with students categorized by discipline: scientific (e.g., MPSI, PCSI), business (ECS/ECE), or humanities (Hypokhâgne/Khâgne). Oral exams (*colles*) are a defining feature, emphasizing critical thinking and preparation. Teacher recruitment follows a hierarchical structure: INSPE (primary), CAPES (middle), and *agrégés* (highly competitive, well-paid) in secondary schools. University teaching is led by "teachers-researchers" with doctorates and HDRs. France maintains secular public schools (*laïcité*), banning religious instruction except in Alsace and Moselle, and a 2004 law restricts conspicuous religious symbols to prevent proselytism. With a declining youth population (32% under 25), the system faces demographic pressures, though only 39 of 75,000 state schools were deemed "seriously violent," indicating relative stability.

Hong Kong’s education system is compulsory from age 6 to 18, with free public schooling. It features a three-tier structure (primary, junior, senior secondary) leading to the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE), though some schools offer the IB or international curricula. The commerce stream is vocational, and EMI schools are prestigious with higher university acceptance. The government promotes Putonghua, though Cantonese remains dominant. Since 2012, undergraduate programs have standardized to four years, with English as the primary medium. Eight UGC-funded universities—HKU, CUHK, HKUST, PolyU, CityU, HKBU, Lingnan, and EdUHK—are key institutions, with HKU, CUHK, and HKUST globally ranked among the top 100. Admission is primarily via JUPAS. Textbook revisions post-2020 downplay Hong Kong’s colonial status and emphasize mainland China in the Citizenship and Social Development subject. International collaboration is growing, with institutions like MIT establishing innovation nodes. Pre-school is not free, with government vouchers available. School discipline is strict, and curriculum criticism centers on rote learning and ineffective history content, though there is a growing push for open-ended tasks and group work.

England’s education system is compulsory from age 5 to 18, with 93% of students attending state schools. School types include academies, free schools, community schools, foundation schools, voluntary aided/controlled (faith-affiliated), UTCs (tech-focused), and maths schools. Private schools (7%) charge fees and are not bound by the National Curriculum. The curriculum includes the Early Years Foundation Stage (ages 3–5) and 13 core subjects from age 5–16. Post-16 education offers diverse qualifications (A-levels, BTECs, T Levels, NVQs), with most degrees being three-year bachelor’s programs. Tuition fees (up to £9,535 annually) are repayable, and access is supported through bursaries and scholarships. A unified qualifications framework (RQF/FHEQ) aligns with European standards. Adult education includes distance learning (e.g., Open University) and vocational training.

Ghana’s education system spans kindergarten to tertiary levels, with a 12-year basic education cycle (ages 4–15) and academic or vocational secondary tracks. It is largely English-based, free, and compulsory, with enrollment increasing significantly since 2008 due to the FCUBE program. A hybrid grading system is used: numeric grades in primary/junior secondary, and the WASSCE (letters and points) in senior secondary. Tertiary admission requires a fee and online application with preference rankings. The system is governed by the Ministry of Education and supported by agencies like GES, NCCA, and NaSIA. Public-private partnerships share responsibilities: government provides salaries, textbooks, and infrastructure; private actors manage operations, supply furniture, and contribute via communities and NGOs. Teacher training is conducted through 46 Colleges of Education offering a three-year DBE diploma. Despite progress in literacy (from 54% to 71.5% since 1997), disparities persist—especially among women and rural populations—and tertiary education remains highly selective, with socioeconomic and gender gaps. Non-formal education focuses on life skills and hygiene, while TVET is underdeveloped, marked by low completion rates, poor training, and lack of recognized qualifications. Government initiatives like COTVET and STEP aim to improve integration, but TVET remains poorly linked to the job market. ICT integration is promoted in policy but implemented inconsistently, with curricula emphasizing device use over pedagogical application. The online scholarship system seeks to improve access and equity. A one-year national service requirement for graduates reflects a policy goal of civic integration, though its long-term impact remains uncertain.

Across all systems, education is a key driver of socioeconomic mobility and national development, though disparities in access, equity, and implementation persist—particularly for marginalized communities, women, and rural populations.
