# Global Education Systems: Comparative Overview

Governance models worldwide range from centralized frameworks to flexible systems, each presenting unique strengths and challenges. Nations like Taiwan, Iran, and Armenia achieve high literacy and STEM outputs through government investment but face hurdles such as teacher shortages, funding disparities, or restrictive curricula. Conversely, countries like Sweden and France prioritize national identity through distinct structural approaches: Sweden employs a flexible, stage-based model with near-universal enrollment, while France maintains a rigid, centralized system centered on the *baccalauréat* to gatekeep higher education.

Regional challenges are defined by persistent structural deficits and cultural priorities impacting equity. The Middle East and Eastern Europe struggle with resource constraints, outdated curricula, and severe teacher shortages. Specific nations face additional issues: Turkey contends with curriculum controversies and high private education costs; Sri Lanka and Egypt grapple with intense competition, deep-seated inequities between public and private sectors, and administrative corruption; and Ghana deals with declining vocational enrollment, heavy reliance on private schools, and post-pandemic digital learning gaps.

## Specific National Case Studies

### France and Hong Kong
France operates a dual higher education system featuring public universities under the Bologna process and elite *grandes écoles* with strict admission criteria. This structure is underpinned by strict secularism (*Laïcité*) and a highly stratified teacher recruitment system creating significant salary disparities. In contrast, Hong Kong, historically influenced by British education, is navigating disruptions from national security reforms that have introduced patriotism-focused curricula and mandatory oaths, leading to a sharp decline in enrollment. The system follows a rigid 3-6-3-3-2 model with a post-1997 shift toward Cantonese instruction, reserving English-medium education for a minority of schools.

### England and Ghana
England maintains a state-integrated model governed by a National Curriculum and Ofsted inspections, with compulsory schooling for ages 5 to 16 leading to GCSEs and A-levels. The landscape is dominated by state-funded schools, with a smaller private sector operating independently. While both Hong Kong and England refine their trajectories, Hong Kong focuses on integrating AI and Greater Bay Area exchanges, whereas England balances state mandates with private autonomy. Ghana utilizes a four-tier education system comprising primary, junior high, senior high, and tertiary levels, with basic education being compulsory and free from ages 4–15. Despite significant infrastructure expansion following the 1996 FCUBE program, the system faces persistent challenges, including declining vocational enrollment due to low prestige and heavy reliance on private schools.

### Shared Challenges and Future Trajectories
Both Hong Kong and England face distinct challenges: Hong Kong struggles with retention amidst political shifts and historical rote learning, while England offers diverse post-16 pathways including apprenticeships and T Levels alongside its traditional academic routes. In Ghana, the integration of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) lags behind implementation, often focusing on device operation rather than enhancing subject learning. To address equity gaps, the government has launched an online scholarship system to streamline access for low-income applicants and established a dedicated "Girls Education unit" to mitigate cultural barriers and early marriage affecting female enrollment.
