# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in Israel, Sri Lanka, Ireland, Egypt, Nigeria, Indonesia, Taiwan, Armenia, Iran, Norway, Sweden, Turkey, France, Hong Kong, and England**

## **National Education Systems: Diversity, Performance, and Key Challenges**

The education systems across these 15 countries reflect a rich tapestry of historical legacies, cultural values, and policy priorities. While many nations achieve strong performance in literacy, tertiary attainment, and international rankings—such as Ireland (second globally in teen reading literacy, PISA 2022), Norway and Sweden (leaders in equity and inclusive learning), and England (13th globally in literacy and science), persistent challenges in equity, access, quality, and teacher sustainability remain widespread. Common cross-cutting issues include deep-rooted colonial, religious, and gender-based disparities, regional inequality, underfunded marginalized communities, and misalignment between curricula and labor market needs.

In **Israel**, high performance in science, math, and engineering is offset by significant disparities between secular and religious tracks, particularly affecting Arab and Haredi communities. Arab schools face underfunding, high dropout rates, and limited access to higher education. **Ireland** excels in literacy and is a top global destination for higher education, supported by targeted programs like DEIS to reduce inequality. **Sri Lanka** struggles with fragmented oversight, intense competition in A-Level exams, and low university admission rates (under 16%), contributing to youth unemployment. **Egypt**, despite near-universal primary enrollment (96.9%), ranks poorly in reading proficiency (49th globally), with severe inequities driven by wealth, poor teacher quality, and corporal punishment. Youth unemployment remains high, especially among women. **Nigeria** and **Indonesia** face challenges of colonial segregation, rural-urban disparities, and teacher shortages. Nigeria has over 99 private universities and relies on informal education for literacy; Indonesia’s 12-year compulsory system serves over 50 million students but suffers from quality gaps in remote areas. Religious schools (*pesantren*, *madrassa*) offer culturally grounded education but remain unevenly integrated with secular content. **Taiwan** stands out for its rigorous discipline, standardized assessments, and high university access (over 95%), emphasizing national identity, Mandarin, and STEM. Despite strict discipline, corporal punishment is legally banned. **Armenia** achieved 100% literacy and strong alignment with European standards through Bologna integration and Erasmus+ participation, though post-2005 student absenteeism and dropout rates surged due to child labor and poor quality. **Iran** maintains a highly centralized system emphasizing religious values, sex segregation, and mandatory Islamic practices, producing strong STEM graduates (over 70% of university students are women), but faces a critical teacher shortage (~60,000 missing), school shortages, and curriculum bias—especially in foreign languages. English is banned in primary schools since 2018. **Norway** and **Sweden** exemplify inclusive, student-centered models: Norway’s *grunnskole* emphasizes play-based learning with no formal grading until secondary school, while Sweden offers free preschool and national testing at grades 3, 6, and 9, with diverse secondary pathways and strong special education. Both countries lead in equity and accessibility, though Sweden faces concerns over grade inflation. **Turkey** transitioned to a 12-year compulsory system (2012), emphasizing national identity and Atatürk’s ideology, with religious education taught from primary to secondary school—though textbooks are criticized for promoting Turkish nationalism and denying the Armenian genocide. Vocational training and university autonomy have improved, enhancing international recognition.

## **Higher Education Systems: Structure, Access, and Policy Shifts**

France, Hong Kong, and England offer distinct models of higher education shaped by historical, political, and cultural forces. In **France**, public universities—named after cities—balance teaching and research, with low tuition fees (€170–380/year) for EU students and strong integration with CNRS and INSERM. Degree structures follow the Bologna Process (Licence, Master, Doctorat), though traditional curricula and limited flexibility persist. Access to elite institutions is highly competitive via CPGEs: scientific CPGEs (e.g., MPSI, PC) prepare students for STEM, while business and humanities CPGEs (e.g., Prépa ECS, Khâgne) emphasize rigorous academic training. Oral exams (*colles*) are central to success. Teacher training is structured: primary teachers through INSPE (Bac+5), secondary teachers via *CAPES* or *Agrégés*, and university lecturers from *Maître de conférences* to *Professeur* via HDR. CPGE teachers are typically *Agrégés*, earning €2,000–7,500 annually. The system has expanded access, notably with the 1880 establishment of women’s schools.

In **Hong Kong**, the system evolved from a rigid exam model (HKCEE, HKALE) to a modern structure centered on the HKDSE (2012). Admission is managed via JUPAS, with English as the primary medium. Eight top universities—such as HKU, CUHK, and HKUST—are globally recognized. Public institutions offer subsidized education, while private and self-financing institutions provide alternatives. Adult and special education are accessible, and international curricula (UK, IB) are widely available. Class sizes have declined due to falling birth rates, prompting calls for small-group teaching. Post-2020, the system has shifted toward patriotism and national security, with revised curricula downplaying colonial history and attributing protests to foreign actors. The Citizenship and Social Development subject replaced Liberal Studies, and AI tools like ChatGPT are now permitted in coursework. Since the national security law, there has been a significant exodus of students and teachers.  

In **England**, higher education is state-funded and structured into post-16 pathways (A-levels, BTECs, T Levels, apprenticeships). Most bachelor’s degrees are three or four years long. Tuition fees are capped at £9,535 annually, with bursaries supporting disadvantaged students. International enrollment is strong, especially at elite institutions like Oxford and Cambridge. The system is governed by the RQF and FHEQ, aligned with European standards. Performance has improved: British 15-year-olds rank 13th globally in literacy, math, and science (average score 503.7), and the UK ranks 7th in the UN Education Index. The academy system has expanded, with 88% of schools rated as good or outstanding. Funding reaches £7.1 billion annually, with £780 million allocated to SEND.

## **Cross-Cutting Challenges and Opportunities**

All systems face **teacher shortages, low wages, regional inequities, and curriculum misalignment** with labor market and societal needs. Teacher pay remains low in Israel, Egypt, Nigeria, and France, leading to strikes and high turnover. In Indonesia and Nigeria, teacher training is improving but still insufficient. Curriculum reform is essential to promote critical thinking, digital skills, gender equality, and inclusive, global perspectives—especially in addressing bias and integrating diverse histories.  

Digital transformation is a shared priority: **Taiwan, Nigeria, Sweden, and England** are advancing digital literacy and e-learning, while **Turkey and Iran** have adopted hybrid models. Public-private partnerships, international collaborations (e.g., Erasmus+, Bologna Process), and investment in vocational and informal education offer pathways to broader access and relevance.  

Ultimately, despite diverse historical and cultural foundations, the global education landscape is converging on shared goals: **equity, quality, inclusivity, and alignment with economic and societal needs**. Sustainable progress will require sustained investment, policy coherence, and a commitment to reforming curricula, teacher support, and access for marginalized populations—particularly in underserved regions and vulnerable communities.
