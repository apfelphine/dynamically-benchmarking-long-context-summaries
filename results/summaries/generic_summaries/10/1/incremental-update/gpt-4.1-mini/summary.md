# Final Summary of Education in Hong Kong, Ghana, Sweden, England, Norway, France, Armenia, Turkey, Indonesia, Iran, Egypt, Taiwan, Sri Lanka, Nigeria, Israel, and the Republic of Ireland

**Historical and Curriculum Development:**  
Education systems across these regions reflect diverse historical, cultural, religious, political, and modernization influences. Hong Kong’s bilingual system evolved from colonial roots emphasizing patriotism and technology. Turkey has shifted from secularism toward Islamized curricula amid expanding tertiary access. Indonesia balances colonial and nationalist-religious legacies with equity challenges. Ghana expanded basic and higher education post-independence. Sweden, England, Norway, and France maintain standardized public education with debates on privatization and inclusivity. Armenia transitioned from Soviet literacy campaigns to multilingual education despite underfunding. Iran integrates ancient and Islamic traditions with reforms, gender segregation, and privatization. Egypt’s centralized system faces equity and quality issues despite expanded access. Taiwan’s centralized, Mandarin-medium, exam-focused schooling combines strict discipline with extended compulsory years. Sri Lanka merges Buddhist monastic and colonial missionary education under a centralized, tiered structure. Nigeria implements Universal Basic Education amid federal, state, and private institution growth but contends with quality concerns. Israel’s stratified system—state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, Arab—faces demographic shifts and equity efforts. The Republic of Ireland evolved with free upper secondary education since 1967, reforms reducing church influence, and alignment with European higher education frameworks emphasizing bilingualism and inclusivity.

**Education Structure, Language Policy, and Schooling Culture:**  
Most systems provide early childhood education, compulsory primary and lower secondary schooling, and varied academic and vocational upper-secondary options. Language policies reflect colonial and national histories, incorporating languages such as Cantonese, English, Mandarin, Turkish, Persian, Arabic, French, Armenian, Sámi, Hebrew, Irish, and indigenous tongues. Taiwan is notable for its strict, military-style discipline and exam orientation. School calendars extend from autumn to spring/summer with formal routines and uniforms. Sri Lanka mandates attendance through Grade 9 with progression via exams; Taiwan’s 3+3 system allows some to bypass exams via private continuous schooling. Nigeria employs a 9-3-4 cycle culminating in the SSCE, supplemented by private, religious, international, and alternative pathways. Israel mandates schooling through grade 12 with sectoral curricula; Haredi schools often exclude core secular subjects, affecting matriculation outcomes and workforce integration. Ireland mandates schooling ages 6–16 (or after three years of secondary), with mostly private pre-school increasingly supported by the state, including inclusive Irish-language options. Primary education spans eight years emphasizing holistic development, predominantly in church-controlled schools (~88% Catholic), though secularization reforms are advancing. Secondary education features a 3-year Junior Cycle ending with Junior Certificate exams, an optional Transition Year emphasizing experiential learning and personal development, and a 2-year Senior Cycle culminating in the Leaving Certificate. Secondary schools require at least 28 weekly tuition hours with school days typically from 8:20 to 16:00. Notably, only 12.3% of Irish students leave after Junior Certificate, below the EU average, with students ranking above average in OECD/EU assessments, especially in reading literacy.

**Types of Schools and Governance:**  
Governance ranges from highly centralized (France, Norway, Iran, Armenia, Egypt, Sri Lanka, Israel) to decentralized pluralistic systems including significant private, religious, and international schools (England, Sweden, Turkey, Ghana, Indonesia, Nigeria). Private and faith-based schools are variably regulated, with ideological influences prominent in Turkey, Iran, Egypt, and Hong Kong. Sri Lanka’s 10,000+ government schools include well-resourced National Schools and less-resourced provincial schools alongside Buddhist pirivenas, private British-curriculum schools, Islamic madrasas, and semi-governmental institutions. Nigeria has federally funded Unity Schools coexisting with under-resourced state and faith-based schools. Israel’s school sectors reflect religious and ethnic identities, with integrated Jewish-Arab schools benefiting from legal protections against sectarian discrimination. Ireland’s primary schools include state-supported national schools (mostly diocesan-controlled), Irish-language Gaelscoileanna (~6% attendance), multidenominational Educate Together schools, and independent preparatory schools. Reforms in Ireland aim to reduce religious influence and enhance inclusivity, especially in urban areas. Secondary education includes voluntary secondary schools (57% of pupils), community colleges (28%), comprehensive/community schools (15%), private fee-charging "grind" schools, and 3% Irish-language medium Gaelcholáistí.

**Tertiary and Teacher Education:**  
Universities across these countries confer certificates up to doctorates, with admissions largely exam-based. Turkey, Armenia, Egypt, Israel, and Taiwan emphasize internationalization; Iran and Indonesia face teacher shortages and resource gaps. Sri Lanka restricts public university intake via high-stakes A Level exams and district quotas; vocational education follows a seven-level National Vocational Qualifications framework; private universities encounter resistance. Taiwan’s U.S.-style public-private universities enroll most high school graduates, focusing primarily on sciences and humanities with professional tracks in medicine and law. Teacher education is centralized, supplemented by in-service training; however, graduate unemployment persists. Nigeria’s tertiary sector comprises federal, state, and private universities and polytechnics requiring SSCE/GCE and JAMB or equivalents, facing quality and political challenges in vocational education. Israel boasts high tertiary attainment (46% of Jewish adults) supported by OECD-level spending fueling a high-tech economy, yet Arab students face barriers from underfunding and limited access; government and nonprofit initiatives target inclusion and support amid low teacher wages and frequent strikes. Ireland aligns with European Higher Education cycles and qualifications frameworks, offering most courses in English with some in Irish, ranking among the top 20 countries globally (2022 US News), and fostering a popular environment for international students who often become global leaders.

**Adult, Special Education & Inclusion:**  
Mainstreaming special education is prominent in Hong Kong, Sweden, Norway, and England. Adult literacy and lifelong learning programs operate notably in Ghana, Sweden, and Egypt. Minority language and immigrant integration initiatives appear in Sweden, France, and Armenia. Taiwan emphasizes special education and serves mainland Chinese students. Turkey, Iran, and Egypt face inclusivity challenges linked to religion, ethnicity, and ideology. Nigeria’s informal education sector, supported by NGOs, advances literacy and economic mobility despite funding and stability challenges. Israel promotes inclusion with legal bans on sectarian discrimination, though inequalities persist especially in Arab and Haredi sectors; curricular gaps and stereotyped textbooks remain concerns. Reforms seek to improve vocational education and access; female students outperform males in dropout and enrollment metrics but males dominate STEM fields and the professoriate. Municipalities receive awards for educational achievements despite frequent teacher strikes and low remuneration. Ireland’s inclusive focus is reinforced by the 2004 Education Act governing special needs education and support from the National Council for Special Education including Special Needs Assistants. The Department of Education provides financial support and the DEIS program combats educational disadvantage, covering over 1,200 schools as of 2022. School holidays are standardized with approximately 182 days in primary and 167 in post-primary education, with adjustments such as COVID-19 closures in 2020.

**Performance and Challenges:**  
Quality disparities are extensive: Sweden and Norway lead in outcomes; England strengthens vocational engagement; Ghana, Indonesia, Egypt, Iran, and Turkey grapple with funding, equity, and curricular tensions between tradition and modernization. Taiwan achieves near-universal compulsory education with strong discipline, though corporal punishment and graduate unemployment remain concerns. Sri Lanka faces resource disparities between National and provincial schools, limited university places, and graduate underemployment; private university expansion meets resistance. Nigeria struggles with resource deficits, staffing shortages, and quality decline in state schools amid a growing yet uneven private sector; female education is hindered by socioeconomic barriers including early marriage, poverty, and insecurity, with UNICEF-supported programs showing mixed results. Israel’s substantial education spending underpins a high-tech economy and strong academic results but coexists with stark inequalities—Arab sector funding is roughly $192 versus $1,100 per Jewish student—with curricular gaps in Haredi and Arab education. Affirmative action and increased resource allocation aim to close gaps, though ongoing teacher strikes highlight systemic tensions. Ireland has modernized since 1967 with free upper secondary education, comprehensive curricula, reduced religious dominance, and increasing diversity, ranking second worldwide in reading and 11th in math in PISA 2022.

**Legacy and Comparative Context:**  
These education systems illustrate the complex interplay of history, culture, religion, politics, and globalization shaping structure, governance, language policy, and curricular content. Taiwan exemplifies centralized, disciplined, exam-centered education with extended compulsory schooling and comprehensive tertiary options, yet contends with labor market mismatches. Hong Kong adapts a bilingual, colonial-influenced system amid political and curricular shifts. Turkey confronts ideological Islamization of curricula; Indonesia balances secular and religious influences. Sri Lanka combines ancient traditions with colonial legacies under multi-religious centralized controls. Nigeria’s multi-tiered system spans federal, state, private, and vocational institutions addressing quality and equity. Israel maintains a stratified sectarian system with high academic achievement but significant inequalities addressed via government reforms promoting minority inclusion. Ireland integrates historical reforms with bilingualism, secularization, compulsory schooling laws, diverse school types, and alignment with European qualification frameworks, augmented by comprehensive support for disadvantaged and special needs students and a reputation for academic excellence.
