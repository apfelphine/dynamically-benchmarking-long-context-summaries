## Comparative Education Systems: Global Overview

Education systems across regions—including Hong Kong, Ghana, Sweden, England, Norway, France, Armenia, Turkey, Indonesia, Iran, Egypt, Taiwan, Sri Lanka, Nigeria, Israel, and Ireland—demonstrate diverse histories, local influences, and administrative structures. Key themes recur globally: efforts to improve equity and access, curricular quality and specialization, modernization, and reforms tackling persistent disparities.

### Asia
- **Hong Kong:** Combines British and Chinese traditions with bilingual and adult education.
- **Taiwan:** Twelve-year track system (academic, vocational, liberal arts), high university participation, and strong support for special needs and overseas education.
- **Sri Lanka:** Mix of government/private schools; competitive university entry; national vocational programs; stringent higher education standards.

### Africa
- **Ghana:** Compulsory 12-year education with academic/vocational branches, teacher training, life skills, but struggles with marginalized access and limited ICT.
- **Nigeria:** “9-3-4” model, unified exams; variable university quality; digital literacy initiatives; ongoing issues with resources and high out-of-school rates.

### Europe
- **Sweden:** Equity focus, subsidized preschool, secondary choices, free higher education for EU residents.
- **England:** Mandatory schooling with diverse post-16 pathways and regulated university fees.
- **Norway:** Play-based compulsory education, flexible progression, varied teacher training.
- **France:** Centralized secular system, academic/vocational lycée; affordable universities; reforms for demographic and research needs.
- **Armenia:** High literacy rates; exam-driven STEM/heritage curriculum; equity and retention remain challenges.
- **Turkey:** “4+4+4” system covers religious studies and multiple school types; frequent reforms, competitive university entry.
- **Ireland:** Predominantly Catholic, mixed-sex primary and secondary schools with integrated multi/inter-denominational options. The three-stage secondary system:
  - **Junior Cycle (12–15):** Compulsory subjects, culminating in the Junior Certificate exam.
  - **Transition Year (15–16):** Optional year focusing on personal and work development.
  - **Senior Cycle (15–19):** Concludes with the Leaving Certificate exam.
Just 12.3% of students leave after Junior Cycle, well below the EU average, and Irish students rank above the OECD/EU, notably second in teen reading literacy. Pre-school is free for two years; qualifications align with EU standards. The system supports special needs (Education for Persons with Special Educational Needs Act 2004, NCSE, special needs assistants) and disabilities, with targeted support for disadvantaged schools through DEIS. Ireland attracts international students and is known for global leader development. Schools operate for 167–182 days annually; closures occurred during COVID-19. Reforms seek increased secularism and equitable admissions. In 2022, Ireland’s PISA results were 2nd in reading and 11th in mathematics.

### Middle East
- **Iran:** Centralized, Islamized, Persian-taught curriculum; strong literacy and STEM focus; issues with privatization and unequal access.
- **Israel:** Three-tier compulsory system (state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, Arab, integrated) underpins national development. Bagrut exam influences postsecondary options; only ~46% earn full certificate. Universities are highly ranked and subsidized, but sectoral inequality persists. The Arab sector struggles with underfunding and dropouts; reforms target equity. Females outperform males academically but are underrepresented in STEM; teacher salaries are low, with frequent strikes. Municipalities are recognized for educational innovation, and efforts combat textbook bias.

### Indonesia and Egypt
- **Indonesia:** Twelve-year compulsory education, various public/private/Islamic schools, and decentralized oversight with regional disparities.
- **Egypt:** Nine-year compulsory system balancing tradition and modernization; infrastructural and access challenges; options for international and religious schooling.

---

This summary synthesizes global education system structures, curricular and specialization pathways, reform efforts, and ongoing challenges including equity, access, gender inclusion, modernization, poverty, regional disparities, minority integration, teacher status, and evolving policies. Ireland exemplifies the intersection of tradition and secular reform, international achievement, strong supports for special needs and disadvantaged groups, and resilience through the COVID-19 pandemic. Israeli and Irish cases highlight education's role in addressing social divides and fostering global competitiveness.
