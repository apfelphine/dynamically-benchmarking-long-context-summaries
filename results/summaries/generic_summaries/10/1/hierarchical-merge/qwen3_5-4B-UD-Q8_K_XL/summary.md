# Comparative Overview of Global Education Systems

Global education systems exhibit significant structural and cultural variations across continents. In Europe, models range from France's centralized, secular approach with selective *Grandes Écoles* to Ireland's high-performing, religiously influenced system supported by the DEIS scheme for disadvantaged students. The UK maintains capped tuition fees, while Sweden and Norway utilize unified frameworks with diverse support or random selection exams. Meanwhile, the Middle East and Africa display unique trajectories: Turkey's secular "4+4+4" model faces curriculum controversies, Iran prioritizes Islamic values with gender segregation, and Nigeria's "6-3-3-4" structure struggles with infrastructure deficits, funding gaps, and regional insecurity.

Asia presents a mix of rigorous discipline and specialized pathways. Taiwan mandates 12 years of compulsory education with high progression rates through standardized exams and vocational tracks, whereas Sri Lanka operates a five-stage system reliant on a lucrative private tutoring market to overcome structural rigidity. Israel boasts a highly developed four-track system that effectively links education to economic mobility and high tertiary attainment. In contrast, Egypt maintains universal primary enrollment through a centralized Al-Azhar-rooted system but suffers from severe structural inequalities, outdated methodologies, and high student-teacher ratios that necessitate expensive private tutoring.

# Regional Challenges and Specific Models

Persistent hurdles often accompany these distinct models. Centralized systems in Egypt and Iran ensure baseline enrollment but frequently struggle with equity, quality, and resource allocation, while Indonesia faces high dropout rates and rote learning pedagogies despite offering choices between state and private religious institutions. Taiwan's rigorous discipline and mandatory rituals result in high progression but unique cultural elements, whereas Sri Lanka's unregulated foreign qualifications and mismanagement coexist with high demand for graduates in sectors like medicine and IT.

Socioeconomic and demographic pressures further complicate these landscapes. France enforces low tuition for EU citizens but charges up to €40,000 annually for international students, maintaining low violence despite demographic pressures. Nigeria's system is disproportionately affected by rural and northern regional insecurity, alongside a literacy rate of 62.02%. Conversely, Ireland's investments in infrastructure and special needs education have fostered an environment producing numerous world leaders, while Ghana's transition to FCUBE boosted enrollment but faces ongoing quality concerns.
