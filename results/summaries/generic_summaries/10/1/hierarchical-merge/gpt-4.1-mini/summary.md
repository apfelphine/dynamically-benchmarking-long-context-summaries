# Education Systems Overview

## Hong Kong and Taiwan

Hong Kong’s education blends Chinese traditions, British colonial legacies, and political reforms, offering a pathway from kindergarten through 6 years each of primary and secondary education, culminating mainly in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE). Instruction emphasizes Cantonese alongside English and Putonghua. Post-handover reforms stress patriotism and national security education, sparking debate. Demographic shifts affect class sizes and quality. Higher education features eight government-funded universities with a four-year undergraduate cycle and centralized admissions.

Taiwan provides 12 years of basic education including kindergarten and emphasizes Mandarin, math, science, English (from grade 3), and arts. Academic and vocational tracks follow junior high, with national exams critically determining senior high and university placement. Over 95% of high school graduates pursue higher education at public, private, or technical institutions, supported by continuous teacher development and extensive private cram schools. Taiwan also attracts many international students, especially from mainland China.

## France

France’s centralized, secular system centers on laïcité, enrolling about 18 million students. Education starts with preschool at age three and progresses through elementary, collège, and lycée levels, concluding with the national baccalauréat or vocational diplomas. Public and private schools coexist, with some religious instruction in private institutions. Higher education comprises low-tuition public universities and elite Grandes écoles, selecting students via competitive preparatory classes. Teachers undergo master’s-level training and competitive exams. Recent public-private partnerships introducing private colleges into public universities have raised concerns about privatization.

## Sweden, England, and Norway

Sweden offers tuition-free upper secondary academic and vocational programs admitting students based on prior grades, and tuition-free university education supported by loans and grants. An expanded independent school sector fuels debate amid challenges on international assessments partly tied to demographics.

England mandates schooling from ages 5 to 16 with standardized tests culminating in GCSEs, extending compulsory education to 18 with A-level and vocational T-level pathways. The National Curriculum governs core subjects mainly in state schools inspected by Ofsted. English universities typically offer three-year degrees with regulated quality and capped tuition. England ranks highly in international literacy and STEM assessments.

Norway requires compulsory education from 6 to 16 over elementary, lower secondary, and flexible upper secondary levels mixing academic and vocational tracks including apprenticeships. Higher education admission is based on a general university entrance certificate with retake options. The system emphasizes inclusivity and robust support services, achieving high international assessment scores.

## Ghana and Egypt

Ghana provides 12 years of free compulsory basic education including kindergarten, with over 7 million enrolled and education accounting for a quarter of national spending. English is primary medium alongside local languages, sciences, and ICT. Ghana has attained gender parity in primary and secondary education and improved adult literacy. Challenges persist in technical and vocational education and training (TVET), which faces low demand and prestige. COVID-19 accelerated virtual learning, exposing resource gaps.

Egypt offers free basic education from ages 4 to 14 with general and technical secondary tracks. Issues include rote learning, large classes, infrastructure deficits, socioeconomic inequality, and reliance on private tutoring. Over 8,000 Al-Azhar Islamic schools operate in parallel. The higher education sector includes public and private universities regulated by the Ministry of Higher Education, improving in quality despite funding and autonomy constraints. Problems like exam malpractice and youth unemployment remain.

## Armenia, Turkey, Indonesia, and Iran

Armenia mandates 12 years of free education emphasizing Armenian language and culture, with growing foreign language instruction (English, Chinese). University admission is exam-based, with institutions aligning to Bologna standards. Socioeconomic factors contribute to absenteeism and dropouts. Non-formal centers offer tech and creative skills, especially for rural youth.

Turkey enforces 12 years of compulsory education with general and religious vocational tracks (Imam Hatip schools). The curriculum includes Turkish nationalism and politicized minority content. English is prioritized alongside Kurdish and Arabic. Its 160+ universities participate in Erasmus programs and focus research on agriculture, health, IT, and defense.

Indonesia mandates 12 years of compulsory education with free primary and junior high and nominal senior high fees. The 2013 curriculum offers specialization in sciences, social sciences, languages, and religion. Instruction is mainly in Indonesian alongside Islamic education in pesantren and madrasah. Challenges include resource inequality, teacher shortages in remote areas, and quality improvements. Tertiary education aligns with a national qualifications framework.

Iran blends ancient traditions with post-1979 Islamic Revolution reforms including Islamization, gender segregation, and Islamic curricula. Education is free and compulsory in elementary levels, with university admission via the national Konkour exam. Women comprise about 70% of university students. Curricula emphasize scientific, cultural, and Islamic values, with recent restrictions on English and increased focus on French. Education represents about 20% of government spending, supporting reforms on quality, critical thinking, and entrepreneurship.

## Sri Lanka, Nigeria, Israel, and Ireland

Sri Lanka’s education, with over 2,300 years of history, is compulsory up to Grade 9 and governed by multiple ministries. Progression and university entry rely on national exams (Scholarship Exam, G.C.E. O and A Levels). While tertiary education is free at state universities, capacity is limited. Vocational training supports non-traditional paths. The system faces rigidity and inequity, with scholarships aiding access but administrative challenges persist.

Nigeria uses a 9-3-4 structure culminating in national exams (SSCE, GCE) for school progression. It comprises federal, state, private, religious, and international schools, with alternative university access routes like IJMB and JUPEB. Nigeria hosts 153 universities and many polytechnics, with growing private sector roles. Literacy has improved but regional disparities, insecurity, and socio-cultural barriers remain. Government aims to raise literacy to 95% by 2030, focusing on female education through social programs.

Israel’s education covers kindergarten to grade 12 with compulsory schooling and multiple tracks serving its diverse population: state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab schools. The matriculation exam (Bagrut) is critical for higher education, military, and employment. Government-subsidized universities use matriculation and psychometric tests for admission, offering tuition discounts for veterans. Efforts to increase minority participation proceed amid funding gaps in Arab schools. Female students outperform males in academics, though STEM and leadership are male-dominated. Israel invests about 7.3% of GDP in education, supporting its tech-driven economy, despite issues like large classes and low teacher morale.

Ireland mandates education from ages 5 or 6 to 16, with free upper secondary education. Schools are largely Catholic-run, with diverse secondary types including community, comprehensive, fee-paying, Irish-language, and multidenominational. Secondary education includes the Junior Cycle exams, an optional Transition Year, and the Senior Cycle culminating in the Leaving Certificate, vital for university entry. Irish students score above average internationally, especially in reading and STEM. Third-level education ranks globally competitive. Special needs education is legislated with varying support. DEIS targets disadvantaged schools with extra resources, and infrastructure investments support international student recruitment and leadership development.
