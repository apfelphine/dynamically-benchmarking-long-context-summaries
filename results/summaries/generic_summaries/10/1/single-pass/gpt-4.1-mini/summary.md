# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong's education evolved from early village schools and missionary efforts in the 19th century to a bilingual curriculum blending Chinese and Western knowledge. Government and mission schools established foundations for Chinese-English instruction, with debates over vernacular education. Post-1997 reforms introduced project learning and curricula emphasizing national identity, patriotism, and security, influenced by political shifts including the national security law, which has led to declining student enrollments and teacher resignations. Pre-school education is mostly fee-based with government vouchers, and primary and secondary education is compulsory, structured into kindergarten, primary (6 years), junior secondary (3 years), and senior secondary (3 years) ending with the HKDSE exam. A minority attend international schools or EMI (English Medium Instruction) schools, which are considered prestigious. Tertiary education includes eight UGC-funded universities and numerous private institutions, with English as the primary language. Artificial Intelligence tools have recently been permitted for coursework. Cross-border education cooperation with Guangdong aims to foster regional talent.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana's formal education traces back to various early schools including castle-based institutions and missionary schools in the 18th and 19th centuries, with free and compulsory basic education introduced in the mid-20th century. Despite economic and political challenges in the 1980s, reforms improved access to education, establishing a 20-year system from kindergarten to undergraduate level covering basic, secondary, and tertiary education. Basic education is compulsory and free, divided into kindergarten, primary, and junior high school leading to standardized exams. Secondary education splits into senior high school (3 years) with academic or vocational tracks, concluding with WASSCE exams. Tertiary education has expanded, including many public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education. Technical and vocational education faces challenges like low prestige and poor completion rates. The education system incorporates public-private partnerships for funding. Governance involves decentralization, and literacy programs target rural and female populations. ICT integration is ongoing but uneven.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden's education system is centered on a nine-year compulsory comprehensive school divided into lower, middle, and upper stages, followed by elective three-year upper secondary school (gymnasieskola) with vocational and preparatory tracks. Preschool is widely available and partly subsidized. Swedish primary education includes 16 compulsory subjects from early grades, with grades given starting in year 6. National standardized tests occur in grades 3, 6, and 9. Upper secondary school offers 18 national programs, both vocational and preparatory, with diploma projects required. Higher education follows the Bologna process with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, and various grading systems coexisting. Private independent schools (friskolor) are publicly funded but operate independently; their rise has been controversial regarding profit motives and inequality. Sweden’s PISA results have fluctuated, with immigration and testing issues identified as factors.

# Education in England
Education in England is compulsory from ages 5 to 18 with free state-funded schooling and a mix of school types including academies, community schools, free schools, grammar schools, and private schools. The national curriculum covers key stages from early years to secondary education with mandatory subjects and national exams (e.g., GCSEs at 16). Post-16 education includes sixth form colleges, further education, apprenticeships, and T-levels. Higher education offers diverse degree pathways with tuition fees capped, loans, and bursaries. The education system evolved from religious and charitable origins, expanded via acts in the 19th and 20th centuries, and features mandatory early years education. Ofsted inspects state schools. Private schools typically do not follow the national curriculum strictly. Adult and lifelong education opportunities are available. National qualification frameworks align with European standards. English students perform well internationally in literacy and reading metrics.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education system includes compulsory elementary (grades 1–7) and lower secondary school (grades 8–10), followed by optional upper secondary education (grades 11–13) with vocational or general tracks. Preschool is widely offered, often free for children over three. Grading starts at lower secondary level; tests are teacher-assigned with some randomized external exams (trekkfag). The 1994 Reform merged previous branches into one comprehensive system. Teacher qualifications differ by education level. Higher education consists of universities and university colleges offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, alongside private schools. Special education emphasizes inclusion. The academic year is split into two semesters with national holidays observed. Norway maintains several grading systems. Vocational upper secondary education combines school and apprenticeship. Higher education underwent expansions since the 18th century.

# Education in France
Modern French education emerged in the late 19th century emphasizing compulsory, free, and secular schooling under Jules Ferry. The centralized Ministry of National Education regulates a uniform national curriculum across primary and secondary schools. Primary education spans preschool and elementary levels, followed by collège (middle school) and lycée (high school), concluding with the baccalauréat exam in general, technological, or professional tracks. Private schools may follow state curriculum or offer alternative programs with state oversight. International schools also operate. Higher education is divided into public universities and elite grandes écoles. Preparatory classes (CPGEs) prepare students for grandes écoles through intensive coursework and oral exams (“colles”). Teacher training and recruitment are highly structured. Tuition in public institutions is low due to state funding. The education system historically focused on transmitting French culture and identity.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia has a compulsory, free 12-year general education system divided into primary (grades 1–4), middle (5–9), and high school (10–12) with both general and specialized streams. Preschool education begins around age two. Public schools follow a national curriculum blending global trends with local culture; private schools operate independently but are subject to licensing and quality controls. Entrance exams at secondary level determine university admission. Armenia has many higher education institutions, including Yerevan State University and specialized universities, some offering programs in multiple languages. The country participates in European education quality frameworks and exchange programs. Challenges include dropout rates linked to child labor. Non-formal education centers provide skill development. Private education plays a significant role alongside public schooling.

# Education in Turkey
Turkey’s education system is overseen by the Ministry of National Education, with compulsory 12-year education following recent reforms. The system comprises pre-primary (optional nursery/kindergarten), primary (8 years), secondary (high school, 4 years), and higher education. Curriculum changes have included secular reforms, introduction and later expansion of religious education courses, and the recent inclusion of jihad concepts. Education has historically been stratified by social groups and languages, influenced by political shifts and laws banning unapproved schools. Vocational education and apprenticeships are integral. Admission to universities involves national exams, with over 160 universities currently operating, including elite technical universities. Foreign languages are taught from early grades, dominated by English, with other languages recently introduced. Private schools and international schools coexist with public institutions. Criticisms include frequent system changes, political indoctrination in curricula, and quality disparities.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education system spans early childhood education (playgroups and kindergartens), compulsory 12 years of schooling covering primary (6 years), junior secondary (3 years), and senior secondary (3 years) education, plus higher and vocational education. Historically influenced by Dutch colonialism and Islamic traditions (pesantren), education expanded post-independence focusing on nationalism and secularism. Curriculum emphasizes Pancasila ideology. Schools include public secular and religious Islamic schools (madrasah). Vocational education offers diverse technical tracks. The system faces challenges in teacher quality and resource distribution, especially in rural areas. Early education is mostly private. Higher education institutions have diversified across academic, vocational, and professional categories, aligned with national qualification frameworks. Private Islamic and secular schools coexist alongside public schools. There is growing emphasis on technology and international accreditation.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education system has ancient roots with modern structures evolving significantly post-19th century. Current compulsory education covers six years primary, three years lower secondary, and three years upper secondary with academic and vocational tracks. University education includes bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral programs, with admissions via a national entrance exam (Konkour). Education is highly centralized, with increasing Islamic influence post-1979 revolution, including gender segregation and Islamization of curricula. Private schools exist but widening inequalities have arisen. Female enrollment in higher education exceeds male, with notable achievements despite social challenges. Teacher education is centralized via Farhangian University. Foreign languages include Persian, Arabic (compulsory), English, and newly integrated French. Distance and hybrid learning have expanded, particularly after the pandemic. Education reforms emphasize research, inclusivity, and efficiency, though teacher shortages and educational costs remain issues.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education traces from ancient schools through Islamic educational traditions and modern state reforms under Muhammad Ali and subsequent rulers. The system is highly centralized, offering compulsory basic education (ages 4–14), secondary education (divided into general and technical tracks), and higher education, including public and private universities and Al-Azhar religious schools. Postcolonial reforms increased access but quality challenges persist, with private tutoring widespread due to low public school quality. Enrollment rates have increased, but standardized test performance remains low internationally. Recent reforms target curriculum improvements, teacher training, and technical education expansion. International schools follow foreign curricula. Challenges include underfunding, disparities in resources, teacher motivation, and wealth-based inequality. COVID-19 accelerated distance education adoption.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan has a 12-year compulsory education system comprising six years elementary, three years junior high, and three years senior high school (both regular and vocational tracks). Education is publicly funded with schools emphasizing Mandarin instruction alongside English and native languages. National exams determine entry to higher education, which includes universities, universities of science and technology, and junior colleges offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Private education and cram schools supplement formal education, reflecting societal focus on academic achievement. Special education services and nursing education are provided. International programs attract foreign students, and teacher in-service training is supported by governmental platforms.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's education system includes primary (5 years), junior secondary (4 years), senior secondary (2 years), collegiate (2 years), and tertiary education. Education is free and primarily state-funded, managed by multiple ministries with central and local roles, and includes diverse school types: national, provincial, religious, private, and international. Secondary education ends with G.C.E. Ordinary and Advanced Level exams, required for university admission. Universities are limited with competitive admissions favoring urban and higher socioeconomic groups; private universities exist but are limited by policy. Vocational education is managed separately. Private tuition is prevalent due to competitive exams. Challenges include underfunding, infrastructure disparities, high dropout rates, and criticism over rigidity and inequality. Non-government schools include Muslim schools and madrasas.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria offers a 9-3-4 system: six years primary, three years junior secondary, three years senior secondary education, followed by tertiary education. Universal Basic Education aims for free, compulsory 9 years of schooling. Secondary education includes federal, state, and private schools with varying quality and resources. Exams like the SSCE and GCE are administered by WAEC and NECO. Tertiary education includes federal, state, and private universities with expanding numbers. Vocational education is overseen by national boards but hindered by funding and quality issues. Informal education and literacy programs attempt to address educational deficits. Female education faces barriers like early marriage and socio-cultural factors, though initiatives promote girls’ enrollment. Literacy remains a challenge, especially in northern regions. Private and international education exist alongside public systems. Advanced level programs like IJMB and JUPEB provide university pathways.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system is divided into primary, middle, and high school, along with various tracks: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab schools. There is growing demand for integrated Jewish-Arab schooling. Education is highly valued culturally, with strong participation in tertiary education (46% among adults). Despite high attainment, disparities exist, notably for Haredi and Arab populations who receive less funding and face restricted educational inclusion. The matriculation exams (“bagrut”) and Psychometric Entrance Test determine higher education access. Universities are subsidized, with significant international rankings. Challenges include teachers’ low pay and strikes. Arab education is under-resourced, with efforts underway to close gaps. Israeli educational culture emphasizes academic achievement, tied to social mobility.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland’s education system offers compulsory schooling from ages 6 to 16 (or three years of secondary education), conducted primarily in English with Irish-language schools also widespread. Primary education lasts eight years; secondary includes a three-year Junior Cycle, optional Transition Year, and two-year Senior Cycle culminating in the Leaving Certificate exams. Schools include national, religious, and multi-denominational schools; the majority remain under Catholic patronage but multidenominational schools are growing. Higher education is well-ranked internationally and features various qualification frameworks aligned with European standards. Special education and supports for disadvantaged schools are established, with increasing diversity and accommodation. Schools operate on a term system with typical holidays akin to other Western countries.
