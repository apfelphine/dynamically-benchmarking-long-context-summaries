### **Education in Hong Kong**

#### **Historical Overview**
Hong Kong’s education system evolved from small village schools established by British missionaries in the mid-19th century. Early schools, like Li Ying College (founded 1075), were rare, but by the 1860s, village schools had expanded. The British introduced a bilingual curriculum (Chinese and English) in Queen’s College (1862), emphasizing Confucian values alongside Western studies. Missionaries played a key role in establishing medical schools (e.g., Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese, 1887) and girls' schools (e.g., Ying Wa Girls' School, 1900).

#### **Structure and Policies**
Education is compulsory from age 6 to 18, with free public schooling. The system includes:
- **Primary and Secondary Education**: Structured into junior and senior years, with standardized exams (e.g., HKDSE for secondary school graduates).
- **Medium of Instruction**: Most schools use English (EMI schools) or Chinese (CMI schools), with a push toward Putonghua (Standard Mandarin) in Chinese language classes.
- **Higher Education**: Eight UGC-funded universities (e.g., HKU, CUHK, HKUST) and numerous private institutions. Tuition-free for public universities, though fees vary for private schools.

#### **Recent Challenges**
- **National Security Law (2020)**: Led to a decline in enrollment in prestigious schools, with many families opting for international education.
- **Curriculum Shifts**: New textbooks emphasize patriotism and national security, excluding sensitive topics.
- **AI Integration**: Universities like HKUST and EdUHK allow AI tools in coursework, focusing on responsible use.
- **Education Reform**: The Target-Oriented Curriculum (TOC) shifted from traditional teaching to experiential learning, though implementation varied.

#### **Special Education and Diversity**
- **Special Education**: Integrated into mainstream schools, though debates persist over discrimination and separation.
- **Immigrant and Non-Cantonese-Speaking Children**: Free "Induction Programmes" and full-time "Initiation Programme" support integration.
- **International Schools**: Over 175 schools offering curricula in English, German, French, and Japanese, catering to expatriates and affluent families.

### **Education in Ghana**

#### **Historical Overview**
Ghana’s education system traces back to pre-colonial informal learning and missionary schools established in the 19th century. The University of Moliyili (1700s) was one of the earliest learning centers, while colonial-era schools (e.g., Castle Schools) taught Latin and religion. Post-independence, Ghana prioritized education as a tool for national development, introducing free universal basic education in 1961.

#### **Structure and Policies**
- **Basic Education**: Free and compulsory for 12 years (kindergarten to senior high school). Curriculum includes English, Ghanaian languages, mathematics, science, and social studies.
- **Secondary Education**: Divided into academic and vocational tracks. Students take the Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE) after junior high school and the West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE) after senior high school.
- **Higher Education**: 6 public and 49 private universities, with a focus on STEM and social sciences. The Bologna Process has standardized degrees (bachelor’s, master’s, PhD).
- **Vocational Education**: Limited but growing, with challenges in completion rates and industry alignment.

#### **Recent Challenges**
- **Funding and Infrastructure**: Low spending on education (25% of annual budget) and teacher shortages.
- **Equity and Access**: Gender disparities and regional disparities persist, with rural areas lagging.
- **Non-Formal Education**: Literacy campaigns (e.g., Functional Literacy Skills Project) target rural and female populations.
- **Digital Literacy**: Limited access to technology, despite government initiatives.

### **Education in Sweden**

#### **Historical Overview**
Sweden’s education system evolved from the 1842 national school act, which mandated primary schooling (folkskola). The 1962 introduction of the 9-year comprehensive school (grundskola) replaced vocational and preparatory tracks. Post-1990s reforms emphasized experiential learning and reduced class sizes.

#### **Structure and Policies**
- **Compulsory Education**: 9 years (ages 6–15) in grundskola, with a focus on core subjects (Swedish, mathematics, English, etc.).
- **Upper Secondary Education (Gymnasieskola)**: 3 years, divided into preparatory (e.g., business, natural sciences) and vocational programs. Students take the "diploma project" as a capstone.
- **Higher Education**: Bologna-compliant system with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Public universities are tuition-free for EU/EEA students; private schools (friskolor) operate under public funding.
- **Special Education**: Inclusive model with support from specialized services.

#### **Recent Challenges**
- **PISA Results**: Declining scores in reading and mathematics, attributed to immigrant influx and testing issues.
- **Independent Schools (Friskolor)**: Controversial due to perceived profit motives and lower educational standards.
- **AI and Education**: Universities explore AI tools for teaching, balancing innovation with traditional methods.

### **Education in England**

#### **Historical Overview**
England’s education system dates back to medieval schools teaching Latin for clergy. The 19th century saw reforms expanding state-funded schools, with the 1870 Elementary Education Act and 1902 Education Act establishing compulsory schooling. Women’s access to higher education grew with institutions like Bedford College (1849).

#### **Structure and Policies**
- **Compulsory Education**: Ages 5–18, divided into Foundation Stage (ages 3–5), Key Stages 1–5 (ages 5–16), and post-16 education.
- **School Types**:
  - **State-funded**: Academies, community schools, free schools, and university technical colleges (UTCs).
  - **Private**: Fee-charging schools (e.g., public schools for 13–18-year-olds) and international schools.
- **National Curriculum**: Covers core subjects (English, mathematics, science) and additional electives (e.g., languages, arts).
- **Higher Education**: Public universities are tuition-free for UK students, with fees capped at £9,535/year. Private colleges offer vocational and academic degrees.

#### **Recent Challenges**
- **PISA Rankings**: England ranks 13th globally in literacy, mathematics, and science.
- **Discipline and Standards**: Debates over school discipline policies and rising academic pressure.
- **AI and Education**: Universities like Oxford and Cambridge explore AI tools for teaching and research.

### **Education in Norway**

#### **Historical Overview**
Norway’s education system dates to the 1736 compulsory reading law and the 1827 introduction of the folkeskole (primary school). The 1970s saw the transition to the 12-year grunnskole system. Private schools (e.g., Steiner/Waldorf) and religious schools operate under government approval.

#### **Structure and Policies**
- **Compulsory Education**: Ages 6–16 in grunnskole (primary and lower secondary school). Upper secondary school (videregående skole) is optional but mandatory for 16–19-year-olds.
- **Grades**: 1–6 scale, with 6 being the highest. Students take national exams (e.g., grade 10 exam) for promotion.
- **Higher Education**: Universities and university colleges offer bachelor’s, master’s, and PhD degrees. Public institutions are tuition-free for Norwegians; private schools charge fees.
- **Special Education**: Inclusive model with support from specialized services.

#### **Recent Challenges**
- **PISA Results**: Norway ranks highly in mathematics but faces criticism for grading practices and immigrant integration.
- **Teacher Shortages**: High demand for qualified teachers, especially in rural areas.
- **AI and Education**: Universities explore AI tools for teaching and research.

### **Education in France**

#### **Historical Overview**
France’s education system was shaped by Napoleon’s reforms and the 1881 "Jules Ferry Laws," which made education free, secular, and compulsory for ages 6–12. The 1968 student revolts led to reforms, including the Bologna Process for higher education.

#### **Structure and Policies**
- **Basic Education**: Free and compulsory for ages 6–16. Includes preschool (maternelle), primary school (école élémentaire), collège (middle school), and lycée (high school).
- **Higher Education**: Three levels (licence, master’s, doctorate) with tuition fees ranging from €170–€380/year for EU students. Grandes écoles (elite institutions) offer specialized degrees.
- **Vocational Education**: Integrated into the curriculum, with apprenticeships and technical schools.

#### **Recent Challenges**
- **AI and Education**: Universities explore AI tools for teaching and research.
- **Curriculum Shifts**: Emphasis on "common culture" and national identity in history and literature.
- **Private Schools**: "Facultés Libres" and private colleges operate under government oversight.

### **Education in Armenia**

#### **Historical Overview**
Armenia’s education system dates to the 17th-century University of Moliyili and Soviet-era reforms. Post-independence, Armenia shifted to a bilingual curriculum (Armenian and Russian) but later emphasized Armenian. The 1990s saw privatization and increased English language instruction.

#### **Structure and Policies**
- **Compulsory Education**: Ages 6–18, with 12 years of schooling. Includes preschool (ages 2–6), primary (grades 1–4), middle (grades 5–9), and high school (grades 10–12).
- **Higher Education**: 26 state universities and 33 private institutions. English and Chinese language schools are expanding.
- **Special Education**: Integrated into mainstream schools, with support from specialized services.

#### **Recent Challenges**
- **Student Absenteeism**: Rising dropout rates linked to child labor and education quality.
- **Teacher Shortages**: 60,000 teachers needed, with rural areas lacking qualified staff.
- **AI and Education**: Limited adoption, but universities explore digital tools.
