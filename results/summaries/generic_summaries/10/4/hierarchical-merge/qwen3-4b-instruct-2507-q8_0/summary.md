# **Master Merged Summary: Comparative Overview of Education Systems in France, Turkey, England, Iran, Ghana, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Nigeria, Sweden, Ireland, Norway, Israel, Armenia, and Sri Lanka**

## **Core Features Across Systems**  
All national education systems emphasize state control, national identity, and equity, though their implementation varies significantly based on cultural, political, and economic contexts. Secularism and national identity are central in France, Turkey, and Sweden, with France and Turkey maintaining strong ideological underpinnings—France through *laïcité* and Turkey through Atatürk’s secular nationalism. England, Ireland, and Norway prioritize inclusivity, student well-being, and equitable access, with Norway and Ireland leading in social integration and support for marginalized students. Iran and Taiwan stand out for their academic rigor and STEM focus, while Hong Kong and Israel demonstrate high performance in international rankings despite facing political or systemic challenges.

## **Key System Highlights**  
- **France**: Secular, mandatory from age 6 to 18, with a structured *maternelle* to *lycée* path culminating in the *baccalauréat* or vocational certifications. Access to elite *grande écoles* is highly selective, with only 5% of CPGE applicants succeeding. The system enforces *laïcité*, banning religious symbols in public schools—sparking ongoing debates on religious freedom.  
- **Turkey**: Centrally managed, emphasizing Turkish nationalism and secular values. The "4+4+4" reform extended compulsory education to 12 years. Religious education includes multiple world faiths but is criticized for historical bias, especially regarding the Armenian genocide. Public schools lag behind private institutions in quality and English proficiency.  
- **England**: State-funded, compulsory from age 5 to 18, with a diverse school landscape including comprehensives, academies, and private institutions. Key assessments include GCSEs and A-levels. The academy system has improved quality, with 88% of schools rated good or outstanding. England ranks 13th globally in literacy and math.  
- **Iran**: Centralized, Islamic-influenced system with strong emphasis on STEM and female enrollment. The Konkour exam governs university access. Hybrid and distance learning has expanded access, especially in rural areas. Challenges include teacher shortages, rising privatization, and political control over reforms.  
- **Ghana**: Free, universal basic education (FCUBE) has improved access and literacy (from 54% to 79%). However, technical and vocational education (TVET) remains weak—90% of training is informal—with poor infrastructure and low employability. Equity in tertiary education remains unequal.  
- **Taiwan**: Highly structured, rigorous, and university-focused. Over 95% of high school graduates enter university, driven by strong academic standards and institutional quality. Public universities like NTU and NTHU lead in engineering and medicine.  
- **Hong Kong**: High academic rigor and global university rankings (e.g., HKU, CUHK), but political reforms since 2020 have severely impacted the system. The 2022 *Citizenship and Social Development* curriculum promotes patriotism and downplays Hong Kong’s colonial history, leading to declining enrollment, teacher departures, and curriculum overreach.  
- **Nigeria**: Access-oriented via the Universal Basic Education (UBE) policy, with free compulsory schooling. However, state schools suffer from underfunding, teacher shortages, and poor infrastructure. Private schools offer better quality, but gender disparities and rural access remain significant. Vocational training is misaligned with labor needs.  
- **Sweden**: Equitable, inclusive, and student-centered from age 6 to 16 in *grundskola*. Upper secondary offers both academic and vocational pathways. Strong support for marginalized and immigrant students, though debates persist over testing integrity and school quality.  
- **Ireland**: Inclusive system with compulsory education to age 16 or completion of three years of second-level education. Strong performance in PISA (2nd in reading, 11th in math). DEIS program supports disadvantaged schools with infrastructure and financial aid. Special needs education is well-supported.  
- **Norway**: Holistic, inclusive system with unified upper secondary education. Emphasis on social inclusion, IT integration, and flexible pathways. Grading uses a 1–6 scale; students may graduate via teacher assessments. Strong PISA performance, though math remains a challenge.  
- **Israel**: High post-secondary attainment (46% of 25–64-year-olds hold tertiary degrees). Compulsory education to Grade 12, with state-secular, religious, and Arab tracks. Arab students face underfunding and higher dropout rates. The Bagrut exam is key for university and military entry. Gender disparities persist, with men dominating STEM and women excelling in social sciences.  
- **Armenia**: Free, compulsory 12-year system with high primary and secondary enrollment. Strong programs in science, medicine, and engineering. Armenia participates in the Bologna Process and Erasmus+. Challenges include rising dropout rates and child labor. Tumo centers promote 21st-century skills.  
- **Sri Lanka**: Colonial legacy persists in its structure, with G.C.E. O/L and A/L exams serving as university entry points. High competition, widespread private tutoring, and low graduation rates. Only 16% of A/L qualifiers gain admission to state universities. Vocational education offers NVQSL levels in skilled trades. The system is criticized for rigidity and excessive competitiveness.

## **Common Challenges and Trends**  
Across all systems, persistent challenges include **teacher shortages**, **privatization**, **curriculum bias**, **underfunded vocational education**, and **demographic shifts**. Political influence significantly impacts educational autonomy—most notably in Hong Kong, Israel, and Armenia. Systemic inequities remain, especially in gender, rural access, and socioeconomic status. While many countries have expanded access and literacy, structural weaknesses in TVET and alignment with labor markets hinder long-term development. Taiwan and Ireland exemplify strong outcomes in quality, international competitiveness, and student retention. Norway and Sweden offer models of equity and inclusion, while Israel and Armenia demonstrate resilience in post-conflict and post-colonial settings.  

Ultimately, the success of each system reflects not only policy design but also societal values, political stability, and investment in human capital.
