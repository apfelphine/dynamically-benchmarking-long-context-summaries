## International Education Systems Comparison

### Overview of National Systems and Structures
Global education models exhibit high diversity in structure and duration, with nations ranging from Taiwan's 12-year compulsory system to France's centralized model. Many countries share common issues such as infrastructure deficits, rote learning, and literacy gaps. For instance, Nigeria targets 95% digital literacy by 2030 while battling resource and gender bias, and Hong Kong uses bilingualism and experiential learning to counter rote memorization.

### Specific National Models and Reforms
Various nations utilize unique pedagogical frameworks:
*   **Sweden** mandates nine years of basic education (*Grundskola*, ages 6-16), complicated by private funding (*Friskolor*) raising equity concerns.
*   **Ireland** requires schooling from age six to sixteen, utilizing the DEIS scheme to support disadvantaged students and maintain high literacy.
*   **Norway** features a tiered system that prioritizes early-year play and offers both general and vocational routes, integrating special education.
*   **Israel** operates a K-12 system with distinct state-secular, religious, and Arab tracks, supporting its high-tech industry despite inequalities for Arab students.
*   **Armenia** has achieved 100% literacy via free primary and secondary education, blending national culture with Russian and English instruction.
*   **Sri Lanka** maintains a rigid, historically tiered system, with compulsory education up to Grade 9 and intense exam competition.
*   **Indonesia** educates over fifty million students across nine years, integrating diverse Islamic educational models (*pesantren*, *madrasah*, *sekolah islam*) with vocational options.
*   **Egypt** has a rich history influenced by French traditions and reforms from Muhammad Ali (1805-1848), which established religious ($\textit{mansouri}$) and secular ($\textit{madrasas}$) schools, later supplemented by Nasser's free public education push.

### Modern Egyptian Education System and Challenges
Egypt's current system is centralized and expansive, comprising public, private, and parallel structures like Al-Azhar and specialized agricultural schools. It mandates free basic education (ages 4-14) and offers general and technical tracks, with a recent focus on vocational training via the ITC. While primary enrollment is high (96.9% net enrollment), the system suffers severely from quality issues, low international scores, a "wealth gap," high teacher-to-student ratios, and persistent rote learning. Infrastructure and funding challenges are evident as enrollment grows, leading to reliance on private tutoring due to public education inadequacies. Public school teachers often face issues regarding low qualifications, low pay, and corporal punishment, compounded by systemic fund embezzlement. Moreover, high youth unemployment in Egypt is a critical issue, stemming partly from insufficient Vocational and Technical Education and Training (TVET); in 2019, the university graduate unemployment rate reached 36.1%, with female graduates experiencing a higher rate of 53.2%.

### Comparative Analysis
In summary, while global educational frameworks vary greatly in scope—from Armenia's literacy success to Indonesia's vast reach and Egypt's complex evolution—most systems globally face shared obstacles concerning equity, instructional quality, and curriculum effectiveness.
