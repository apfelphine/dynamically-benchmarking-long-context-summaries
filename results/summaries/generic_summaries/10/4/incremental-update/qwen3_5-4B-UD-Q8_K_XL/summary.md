### Expanded Education Systems: Indonesia and Egypt

**Indonesia's Educational Landscape**
Indonesia, the fourth largest education system globally, mandates 12 years of compulsory schooling with high completion rates but low tertiary participation. Rooted in the state ideology of Pancasila, the system currently faces teacher shortages, low salaries, and uneven resource distribution. It comprises public, private, and religious institutions, including vocational tracks (SMK) and special education. A significant component is the Islamic sector (*pesantren*, *madrasah*, and *sekolah Islam*), which operates under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI) since 2012, catering to a demographic prioritizing religious identity over the nationalism-focused public system.

**Egypt's Historical and Cultural Context**
Egypt's educational history dates to Alexandria (300 BCE) and features key milestones such as the founding of Al-Azhar (970 CE) and the *ijāza* system. The 18th-century French Expedition under Napoleon introduced modern scientific inquiry, later revived by Muhammad Ali through a dual system of religious (*mansouri*) and secular (*madrasa*) schools.

**Egypt's Modern Era and Structural Evolution**
While the Khedive Ismail era expanded access, it widened disparities between elite urban tracks and rural *kuttab*. British occupation (1882–1907) neglected education to suppress nationalism, whereas Nasser post-independence implemented free compulsory education. Today, enrollment is high (96.9% primary), yet the system faces qualitative challenges: low international test scores, high student-teacher ratios, rote memorization, and systemic issues like funding embezzlement and corporal punishment.

**Current System Structure and Challenges**
The modern Egyptian system is centralized, divided into Basic (ages 4–14), Secondary (ages 15–17), and Tertiary levels. Teacher training requires four years of pre-service university education. However, the system grapples with significant disparities in quality and equity. It comprises three distinct sectors: a centralized public sector (struggling with low teacher quality, inadequate salaries, and poor infrastructure), private international schools, and the religiously oriented Al-Azhar system. These structural weaknesses lead to high reliance on private tutoring, which consumes family income and exacerbates wealth disparities. Despite government reforms since the late 1990s and curricular updates, Egypt continues to rank low globally in primary education quality.

**Youth Unemployment and Labor Market Impact**
Beyond structural and qualitative issues, Egypt faces severe youth unemployment, largely driven by insufficient access to Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) programs. In 2019, the unemployment rate among university graduates reached 36.1%, with significant gender disparities: 25.1% for males and 53.2% for females.
