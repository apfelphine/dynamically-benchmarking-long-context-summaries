# Education in France
French education has roots in reforms initiated by Napoleon (universities and secondary) and Guizot (elementary). Jules Ferry’s 1881 laws mandated free, secular, and compulsory elementary education for ages 6 to 12, significantly reducing the Catholic Church's role. The curriculum emphasizes French literature and history to foster a common national culture, adapting to rising student diversity. Governed centrally by the Ministry of National Education and Youth, the school year runs from September to July with nationwide standardized holidays. Education is compulsory up to age 16, with exceptions for certain situations. Primary education starts with preschool (maternelle) emphasizing socialization and basic literacy, followed by elementary school (grades CP to CM2). Secondary education includes collège (middle school, ages 11–15) and lycée (high school, ages 15–18), preparing students for the baccalauréat exam with general, technological, or professional tracks.

The private sector mainly consists of schools under contract with the state, following the national curriculum, and independent private schools that may teach alternative curricula. France hosts 105 international schools, regulated by AEFE. Higher education features a three-level structure (Licence, Master, Doctorat) with public universities admitting all baccalauréat holders, and selective grandes écoles offering specialized elite education. Tuition is low for public universities but higher for private/business schools. Teacher recruitment involves competitive exams (CAPES and Agrégation) with varying statuses. Religious instruction is generally excluded from public schools (except Alsace and Moselle), reflecting the principle of laïcité.

# Education in Turkey
Post-Ottoman Turkey centralized education under the Ministry of National Education with reforms beginning in the 1920s, combining disparate Ottoman educational streams into a secular, state-controlled system emphasizing national identity. Compulsory education was expanded from five to twelve years by 2012 reforms, structured as 4+4+4 (primary first and second levels, followed by secondary), including the reopening of religious Imam Hatip schools. Pre-primary education is optional, and primary education lasts eight years, covering core subjects including history and Kemalism. Secondary education, or high schools (lise), includes general, vocational, and technical tracks, with entrance based on varying and frequently changing exams. Foreign languages, especially English, are taught from early grades, with Kurdish and other minority languages introduced recently as electives. Religious education has been reintroduced since 1949 and is compulsory from grade 4 onward, focusing mainly on Islam alongside ethics and other religions.

Turkey hosts various private schools (for minorities, foreigners, and international curricula), and replaces private tutoring centers (dershane) with academic high schools. Higher education includes public and foundation (private) universities with over a million students, admission via national exams, and growing international student enrollment. Engineering programs are ABET-accredited. The system emphasizes Turkish nationalism and Atatürk’s ideals, though it faces criticism for politicized curriculum changes and quality concerns in textbooks.

# Education in England
Education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18, with schooling free in state-funded institutions. Early years include optional preschool, moving into primary (key stages 1 and 2) and secondary education (key stages 3 and 4), culminating in GCSEs at age 16. Post-16 education is mandatory but may be through full-time study, apprenticeships, or part-time training until age 18. Secondary schools include comprehensive, selective grammar, academies, free schools, and faith schools. Ofsted monitors school standards.

Higher education comprises universities, colleges, and private institutions offering undergraduate and postgraduate degrees. Universities charge tuition fees (up to £9,535 per annum from 2024), with available bursaries and loans. University admission is monitored by the Office for Students; quality is overseen by the Quality Assurance Agency. Postgraduate options include master's and doctorate degrees. Vocational qualifications and apprenticeships, such as T Levels introduced in 2020, supplement the academic pathways. Adult and lifelong education are widely provided, including distance learning via institutions like the Open University. National qualifications frameworks align with European standards. International assessments rank English students above OECD average, with ongoing reforms to improve standards.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education dates from ancient Persia, with formalized systems emerging in the Safavid and Qajar eras. The Literacy Corps (1969–79) significantly advanced literacy. Post-1979 Islamic Revolution introduced Islamization of curricula, sex segregation in schools, and religious ceremonies. Education is centrally managed by the Ministry of Education with six-year elementary, three-year lower-secondary, and three-year upper-secondary tracks, plus technical/vocational options.

Higher education aligns with European standards, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Public universities admit via the nationwide Konkour exam; Islamic Azad University offers private education with fees. Around 4.5 million students enroll in higher education, including prestigious institutions like Tehran University. Women make up more than 70% of university students. Unique features include specialized gifted schools (NODET) and compulsory government service for university graduates. Foreign languages focus mainly on English, with recent curricular additions of French, Kurdish, and Arabic. Hybrid learning programs and digital platforms have expanded post-pandemic. Challenges include teacher shortages, privatization trends, and mandatory Islamic practices in schools.

# Education in Ghana
Education in Ghana evolved from informal, oral, and apprenticeship systems to formal schooling introduced by European missionaries and colonial authorities. Post-independence reforms led to free and compulsory basic education (ages 4 to 14), structured into kindergarten, primary, and junior high school levels, followed by senior high school (SHS) or vocational education. The government aims for universal basic education, though challenges remain in quality and access.

Tertiary education has grown with public and private universities, polytechnics, and teacher training colleges. Admission depends on performance in national exams (BECE and WASSCE). Vocational education includes formal and informal apprenticeships, but suffers from low prestige and inadequate resources. Literacy rates have improved but gender and regional disparities exist. The Ministry of Education governs the system, with decentralization efforts enhancing local management. Private sector involvement is significant at all levels, providing supplementary educational services. ICT integration is ongoing but uneven, with digital learning challenged by resource limitations.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan’s public education system provides 12 years of compulsory schooling (6 years elementary, 3 years junior high, 3 years senior high), with admissions controlled by national exams. Preschool and special/vocational education are also available. The school year runs from September to June with two semesters and holidays around Lunar New Year.

Senior high schools offer science and liberal arts tracks, while vocational high schools emphasize practical skills. Higher education includes numerous public and private universities and junior colleges, many offering associate to doctoral degrees, with a strong focus on engineering and technology. University education is competitive but generally less pressured than secondary schooling.

Private schools and cram schools (buxiban) supplement education, focusing heavily on exam preparation. Early childhood centers and English immersion preschools are common. Graduates frequently pursue overseas postgraduate study. Teacher training is offered in universities of education, with ongoing professional development through centralized digital platforms. Special education services are integrated, and Taiwan is a hub for Mandarin language learning for international students.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education system evolved from early Chinese village schools and missionary institutions to a predominantly public, English-medium system with Chinese language education. After the 1997 handover, shifts include increased emphasis on patriotism and national security in curricula, diminished political content, and curriculum reforms promoting experiential learning. Recent years have seen declines in school enrollment and faculty retention due to political and social changes.

Education is compulsory from age 6 to 18 (with some exemptions), covering kindergarten, primary (6 years), junior secondary (3 years), and senior secondary (3 years) levels, culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE). Schools include government, aided (charitable/religious), direct subsidy scheme (DSS), private, and international schools offering curricula such as IB, British GCSE/A-levels, and American APs. Mediums of instruction vary between Cantonese, English, and Mandarin for Chinese subjects.

Tertiary education includes eight government-funded universities ranked globally, and several self-financing private institutions. Adult education and special education programs exist, with transitions toward integrated schooling for special needs students. Non-Cantonese and immigrant children receive specific language support. The city has a large international school sector, though school fees are high.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system comprises six years of primary education, three years of junior secondary, and three years of senior secondary education, followed by tertiary education (universities, polytechnics, colleges). Primary education includes core subjects and regional languages; admission to secondary schools is via an entrance exam. Federal and state governments fund schools, with federal "unity" schools perceived as elite.

Secondary education consists of academic and vocational streams, with national exams (SSCE and GCE) determining progression. Private schools are numerous and generally offer higher quality and smaller classes. Advanced level programs (IJMB, JUPEB) enable university direct entry.

Tertiary education is dominated by public institutions but supplemented by private universities; admission is competitive and governed by the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB).

Vocational education struggles with outdated curriculum, shortages, and low uptake. Informal education and literacy programs targeting adults and the unemployed, supported by NGOs, exist but face funding and implementation challenges. Female education has improved, but barriers like early marriage and insurgencies persist. Literacy remains low nationally, with regional disparities, though recent government initiatives aim to improve both general and digital literacy.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden offers a nine-year compulsory comprehensive school (grundskola) starting at age 7 with grades 1–9, followed by a three-year elective upper secondary school (gymnasieskola) offering preparatory and vocational programs. Preschool education, mostly free from age 3 to 6, prepares children socially and cognitively.

The national curriculum requires 16 compulsory subjects and includes national tests in grades 3, 6, and 9. Grading starts in grade 6, with a letter scale from A to F. Upper secondary schools provide 18 national programs, including International Baccalaureate; admission is based on grades. Higher education follows Bologna Process structure with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, offered at universities and university colleges. Funding includes public grants and independent schools (friskolor), which are publicly funded but privately run, constituting over 10% of enrollment.

Standardized tests showed Sweden's performance close to OECD averages but with recent fluctuations and some concerns over test administration. The independent school system is debated politically, with concerns over profit and equality. Anti-discrimination policies apply across education. Vocational education exists but is separate from degree programs. Student aid and language proficiency requirements exist for higher education.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland's education is compulsory from ages 6 to 16 or until completing three years of secondary education. Primary education usually starts at age 4–5 and lasts eight years; secondary education is divided into Junior Cycle (3 years), optional Transition Year, and Senior Cycle (2 years) culminating in the Leaving Certificate. Most schools are denominational, predominantly Catholic, though multidenominational and Irish-language (Gaelscoileanna) schools exist and are growing.

Third-level education is widely available with universities and colleges teaching mainly in English, some offering courses in Irish or other European languages. The system includes special education provisions and policies targeting disadvantaged areas (DEIS schools). Free preschool is provided for two years prior to primary school.

School hours vary with a typical day running from around 8:30–15:30 with half days on some weekdays. School holidays are spread across three terms with breaks at Christmas, Easter, and mid-term.

The education system is recognized for high literacy and academic standards, with students ranking well in international assessments. The government supports equality initiatives and has begun investing in school infrastructure.

# Education in Norway
Organized education in Norway dates back to medieval cathedral schools and evolved into the folkeskole system with compulsory schooling introduced by the late 19th century. Today, compulsory education spans ages 6–16, covering primary (grades 1–7) and lower secondary (grades 8–10). Upper secondary school (videregående skole) is optional, lasting three years, with general and vocational tracks.

Primary education focuses on social skills, basics in subjects, and includes no official grades for early years. Lower secondary introduces grading and electives, with Norwegian students performing well in OECD assessments. Upper secondary reform merged previous separate tracks and introduced more flexibility.

Teachers are categorized by qualifications reflecting education and teaching level. Higher education includes universities and university colleges offering bachelor, master, and doctoral degrees, with admission based on grades and standardized testing. Private higher education is rare.

Special education integrates most students into mainstream schools with support services. Grading systems include both old numeric and newer letter scales. The academic year runs from mid-August to June with several breaks and national holidays.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system consists of primary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), and high school (10–12) with compulsory education through 12th grade. Schools are divided into state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors, each with distinct curricula and languages of instruction. Integrated Jewish-Arab schools exist but are few.

The system emphasizes academic excellence and higher education as keys to social mobility, with Jewish Israelis holding high tertiary education rates. Matriculation exams (bagrut) are critical for post-secondary access; passing rates vary significantly across sectors. Post-secondary studies are selective and supported financially, boasting internationally ranked universities.

Arab education faces resource disparities and lower pass rates; government and NGOs work to improve inclusion. Gender disparities favor females in enrollment but males dominate academia and STEM fields. Teachers face strikes over reforms and wages. Textbooks and curricula have political and cultural sensitivities.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia achieves near-universal literacy with compulsory free primary and secondary education structured into primary (grades 1–4), middle (5–9), and high school (10–12). Specialized secondary schools exist. Public schools dominate, operating under government oversight and following a national curriculum; private schools complement with alternative programs but may deepen inequality.

Higher education includes numerous state and private universities, many integrated into European educational frameworks (Bologna Process), with active participation in Erasmus programs. Universities cover wide academic fields; notable institutions include Yerevan State University and Medical University. Student absenteeism and dropout, linked to child labor and quality issues, are challenges.

Non-formal centers provide 21st-century skills development. Armenia maintains active quality assurance mechanisms aligned with European standards.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education system, shaped by over 2300 years of Buddhist monastic and colonial traditions, is divided into primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate, and tertiary education stages. Education is overseen primarily by several ministries, with free and compulsory education up to grade 9 and generally free schooling for most subsequent stages.

The schooling system includes government national and provincial schools, private schools, religious schools (Buddhist pirivenas, madrasas, Christian schools), and international schools. Primary education lasts 5 years with a national scholarship exam; secondary includes junior and senior sections with national exams (GCE O/L and A/L) that serve as university entrance qualifications.

Tertiary education is competitive, with free state universities accessible via A/L exams; private universities exist but face political resistance. Vocational and technical training is promoted through formal institutes and non-formal programs. Private tuition is widespread due to exam pressures. Leadership training for undergraduates was briefly mandatory and later abolished.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education system evolved from Islamic pesantren and colonial Dutch systems with stratified access during colonial times, to a unified national system emphasizing secular and religious education post-independence. Formal education is compulsory for twelve years with a kindergarten stage (PAUD), primary (grades 1–6), junior (7–9), and senior high/vocational schooling.

Public secular schools fall under the Ministry of Education, while religious schools are supervised by the Ministry of Religious Affairs. Islamic education includes pesantren, madrasah, and integrated Islamic schools. Technical and vocational education is available at various levels.

Higher education includes universities, institutes, and polytechnics offering academic, vocational, and professional education aligned with the Indonesian National Qualifications Framework. There is widespread reliance on cram schools for exam preparation. Challenges include resource allocation, teacher qualifications, and geographical disparities.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education spans from ancient formal institutions like the Academy of Alexandria and al-Azhar to modern reforms under Muhammad Ali and subsequent rulers. The system is highly centralized with free compulsory basic education (ages 4–14), followed by secondary and tertiary stages. Al-Azhar runs a parallel religious education system focused on Islam.

Despite broad access, quality issues persist: poor standardized test performance, reliance on private tutoring, teacher shortages, low salaries, and infrastructure deficits. The COVID-19 pandemic catalyzed distance learning adaptations. Higher education comprises numerous public and private universities, facing challenges of capacity and quality. Recent strategic plans emphasize reform, research, and vocational training enhancement. Egypt hosts many international schools following diverse foreign curricula.


# Summary
The global education systems described share some common characteristics: compulsory basic education, a structured progression through primary, secondary, and higher education levels, governed by national ministries or agencies, and increasing focus on inclusivity and quality assurance. Common challenges include managing equitable access, addressing teacher quality and remuneration, updating curricula to balance traditional knowledge with modern skills, and integrating technology and vocational training to meet labor market needs. Several countries feature elite or specialized institutions (grandes écoles, preparatory programs, pesantren) alongside generalized public schooling. Religious and private education coexist in many contexts with varying degrees of regulation and influence. International assessments highlight varying performance levels and ongoing reform efforts globally.
