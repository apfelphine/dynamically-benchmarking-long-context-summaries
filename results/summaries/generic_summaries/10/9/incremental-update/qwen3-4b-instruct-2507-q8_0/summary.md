# **Updated Working Summary**

Education systems across the world reflect diverse historical, cultural, and political influences. In Ireland, education is compulsory from age 6 to 16, with a strong emphasis on broad-based learning and high international performance—especially in reading. The system includes primary, Junior Cycle, Transition Year, and Senior Cycle, with a mix of public and private schools and growing equity efforts through DEIS and secular governance. In Nigeria, a 6-6 structure leads to the SSCE, with pathways to university via WAEC, NECO, IJMB, and JUPEB; challenges include rural underdevelopment, gender bias, and low literacy (62% in 2018, rising to 69% by 2022), now prioritized through digital and functional literacy. Israel’s system is rooted in academic excellence, with high STEM attainment and strong university rankings, though disparities persist between Jewish and Arab students. Sri Lanka features a tiered system with compulsory education until Grade 9 and a highly competitive tertiary sector; private and religious schools coexist with state institutions. Taiwan offers 12-year compulsory basic education and a robust higher education sector, with strong public universities, high graduate rates, and emphasis on engineering and research, supported by digital platforms and vocational pathways. Egypt has seen progress in access and enrollment but continues to face quality issues, high inequality, poor international outcomes, and persistent youth unemployment. Iran has a well-funded, ideologically shaped system with high female enrollment in higher education and strong performance in engineering and science, despite teacher shortages and curriculum constraints. Indonesia’s system is compulsory for 12 years, with free primary and middle school and modest high school fees; it emphasizes secular education and Pancasila, though influence has waned since 1998. Islamic education is primarily delivered through *pesantren* (boarding schools) in rural areas, offering deep Islamic study, while *madrassa* schools integrate Islamic and secular curricula. Higher education includes *Universitas*, *Institut*, *Politeknik*, and *Akademi*, now aligned with the **Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI)**.

In Turkey, education was reformed post-Republic under Law 2287 (1933), unifying separate systems into a secular, centralized model. The 1924 *Law of Integration of Education* abolished medrese schools and established national curricula. The Turkish alphabet was introduced in 1928, and language institutions promoted Turkish. Education expanded dramatically: from 5,100 schools and 361,500 students in 1923 to over 58,000 and 16 million by 2001. Compulsory education was extended to 12 years (2012). The system includes primary (8 years), secondary (3+ years), and vocational tracks. Public and private schools coexist, with private institutions often offering advanced language instruction. Since 2011, all students receive a standard high school diploma via final examination. The International Baccalaureate (IB) has been available since 1994. A new "4+4+4" system (2012) structures education into four years of elementary, four of middle school (with religious vocational options), and four of secondary. Vocational education prepares students for employment or higher education, with apprenticeships available. Public universities offer low-cost education; private institutions charge up to $30,000 annually. Turkish universities—especially technical ones—are recognized for quality and comparable to U.S. counterparts, with ABET accreditation. International students are drawn to Turkey for affordability and accessibility, with over 795,000 enrolled in 2016. Religious education was banned from 1927–1949, reintroduced in 1956, and now includes 8 years of instruction from 4th grade onward, covering Islam, secularism, ethics, and global religions. English is taught from 2nd grade, with additional languages like Kurdish, Abkhaz, and Arabic added. The system emphasizes Turkish nationalism and secular democratic values, historically curbing religious fundamentalism. Textbooks omit or downplay the Armenian genocide, and students are required to write essays refuting it, raising concerns about curriculum bias.

In Armenia, education is free and compulsory up to age 18, with 100% literacy. Post-1990s, the system shifted to emphasize Armenian history and culture. The K–12 structure includes preschool, primary (1–4), middle (5–9), and high school (10–12), with high enrollment but lower high school completion. English and Chinese are increasingly taught. Public schools dominate (97%), funded by the state, and follow national standards. Entrance exams determine university access. Higher education includes 26 state and 33 private institutions, with strengths in sciences, medicine, engineering, and Armenian studies. Notable institutions include Yerevan State University and private universities like the American University of Armenia. Armenia is a full member of the European Higher Education Area, joined the Bologna Process in 2005 and Lisbon Recognition Convention in 1997, and participates in EU programs like Erasmus+ and TEMPUS. Student absenteeism and dropout rates have risen sharply, linked to child labor and poor education quality. New technology-based centers (e.g., Tumo, SMART) offer 21st-century skills training.

In France, education was modernized under Jules Ferry with free, secular, mandatory schooling. The Ministry of National Education governs a standardized curriculum. Schooling is mandatory from age 6 to 16, with preschool, primary (5 years), and secondary (middle and high school) stages. The baccalauréat or vocational certification concludes secondary education. Private schools are categorized as "under contract" (state-aligned) or independent (with oversight and possible religious instruction). Higher education includes public universities, IUTs (technical institutes), and elite *grandes écoles*. Public universities offer low tuition (€160–380/year for EU students), while elite institutions charge more. Research is led by CNRS and INSERM. *Grandes écoles* are highly selective and prestigious, specializing in engineering, business, and humanities (e.g., École Polytechnique, HEC Paris, Sciences Po). Preparatory classes (CPGEs) prepare students for *grandes écoles*; only 5% of applicants are admitted. Scientific CPGEs focus on STEM; business and humanities CPGEs emphasize math or economics. Weekly oral exams (*colles*) are stressful but essential. Teacher recruitment requires master’s degrees and competitive exams (CAPES, Agrégation). Public schools are secular (*laïcité*), with a 2004 law banning conspicuous religious symbols. Education statistics show 18 million pupils and 2.9 million in higher education; youth proportion has declined from 40% (1970s) to 32% (2020s).

In Norway, education began with cathedral schools in the 1100s; compulsory reading introduced in 1736, followed by *folkeskole* (1827), later replaced by *grunnskole* (1970s). Private schools exist under the 2003 Independent Schools Act but are rare and non-selective. The system includes *barneskole* (6–12), *ungdomsskole* (13–16), and *videregående skole* (16–19). Students are graded in lower secondary; grades influence upper secondary admissions. Electives include languages or career studies. The system emphasizes equity, access, and foundational skills. Lower secondary education is strong in international assessments, though some criticism exists on student support. Upper secondary offers general and vocational tracks, unified in 1994, with a "2+2" apprenticeship model. Teachers are categorized by qualifications. Higher education includes universities (theoretical), university colleges (applied), and private institutions. Admission requires a general university certificate. Special education is integrated into mainstream schools. Grading uses a 1–6 scale (6 highest, 2 lowest passing), with Bologna dual grading. The academic year spans two semesters with national holidays. Inclusive education is a long-standing policy.

In England, education is primarily state-funded, with 93% of children aged 3–18 attending free schools. Key types include academies, community, free, foundation, voluntary aided/controlled, UTCs, and maths schools. Most secondary schools are comprehensive, though some are selective (e.g., grammar schools via eleven plus). Nearly 90% of secondary schools are specialist. All state schools are inspected by Ofsted. School meals are free for low-income pupils. Private schools (7%) are fee-charging and not bound by the National Curriculum. Home education is permitted but not financially supported. The National Curriculum covers core subjects from ages 5–16, structured by key stages. Early years focus on seven developmental areas. Post-16 education offers A-levels, BTECs, T Levels, and NVQs. Higher education is provided by universities, colleges, and private institutions, with undergraduate degrees lasting three years (four for integrated master’s), capped at £9,535 per year from 2024–25. Postgraduate options include master’s and doctorates. Adult education includes access courses, distance learning, and vocational training. A unified qualifications framework (RQF/FHEQ) aligns with European standards. Access and equity have improved due to bursaries and outreach, though fees remain a barrier.

British 15-year-olds rank 13th globally in literacy, math, and science (average score: 503.7), with primary students ranking 4th in reading (PIRLS 2021). The UK ranks 7th in the UN Education Index. The Labour government introduced city academies in deprived areas, leading to improved school ratings—by 2015, 88% of schools were rated good or outstanding (up from 68% in 2010). Since 2018, schools are funded via a national formula, with increased budgets (6% in 2020–21, rising to £7.1 billion in 2022–23) and £780 million allocated for students with Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND).

In Sweden, the education system is structured with clear levels: preschool (1–6), primary (7–16), upper secondary (16–19), and higher education. A nine-year mandatory comprehensive *grundskola* (ages 6–16) emphasizes equity and foundational skills. Primary education began with a national school act in 1842, standardized by 1919, and reformed in 1962 to include vocational and preparatory tracks. Preschool is free for working, studying, or unemployed parents from age 1, with daily access from age 3. Students take 16–17 compulsory subjects, including core subjects like Swedish, math, and English. National tests in grades 3, 6, and 9 assess core subjects, though test leaks have led to cheating. Upper secondary offers preparatory (60%) and vocational (40%) programs. Preparatory programs provide university eligibility; vocational programs focus on job-specific skills. Students must pass 12 subjects (for preparatory) or 8 (for vocational) and complete a mandatory diploma project (graded E or F). Admission is based on elementary school grades; some programs require entrance exams. Higher education is aligned with the Bologna Process, offering bachelor’s (180 credits), master’s (120 credits), and doctoral (240 credits) degrees. Higher vocational education exists outside the Bologna framework. In 2018, 15.6% of students who left compulsory education failed to qualify for upper secondary—up from 12.3% in 2011—mainly affecting boys and students with foreign backgrounds. Sweden ranks highly in early childhood education but faces mixed PISA results, with a notable drop in reading scores linked to immigration and testing flaws, prompting ongoing reform.

In Ghana, education evolved from oral traditions and informal apprenticeships to a formal system influenced by colonial rule. Key milestones include the Philip Quaque Boys School (1765), mission schools (19th century), and free, compulsory primary education under Kwame Nkrumah (1961). Rapid expansion led to quality issues and declining enrollment due to teacher shortages. The 1987 Education Act introduced a 12-year pre-tertiary cycle, compulsory schooling from age 6 to 14, and vocational programs. The 1992 Constitution and FCUBE policy (1996–2005) emphasized universal access, gender parity, and teacher welfare. Enrollment has risen (primary net enrollment: 84% in 2011; literacy: 79.04% in 2018), though vocational education faces challenges due to low prestige and industry demand. Ghana spends about 25% of its annual budget on education and ranks 46th globally in quality (2013–14). The system includes basic (12 years, ages 4–15), secondary (academic or vocational), and tertiary education. Basic education includes kindergarten (2 years), primary (6 years), junior high (3 years), ending with the BECE. Secondary education offers academic (WASSCE after 3 years) or vocational tracks. Tertiary education includes 6 public and 49 private universities, 10 polytechnics, and colleges of education. Admission is based on WASSCE scores (max 24 points). Graduates must serve one year in national service. Grading uses A1 to F9 (A1 excellent). Teacher training occurs through 46 public Colleges of Education. Adult literacy initiatives (e.g., FNLP) have improved literacy, especially among women and rural populations. Technical and vocational education (TVET) is prioritized to combat unemployment but faces low completion rates, poor training quality, lack of certification, and weak job placement, leading to a negative public reputation. Efforts like STEP, COTVET, and the National Apprenticeship Programme aim to strengthen TVET, though most training remains informal. Tertiary education faces equity challenges: rising selectivity, gender and rural disparities, and socioeconomic barriers. ICT integration is policy-driven but underimplemented, with limited device access and a gap between curriculum and practice.

In Hong Kong, education evolved from missionary-led, bilingual curricula to a modern system emphasizing experiential learning through the Target Oriented Curriculum. Post-1997, curricula shifted to promote patriotism and national security. The imposition of the national security law led to a significant decline in school and university enrolment, with thousands of students and teachers leaving. Textbooks were revised to downplay Hong Kong’s colonial status and attribute protests to foreign influence. The education system has transitioned from a half-day to full-day schooling, with declining birth rates leading to smaller classes and ongoing debates about small class sizes. Discipline is strictly enforced through guidelines and reporting, though critics argue it may hinder student development. The system is often described as "spoon-fed," emphasizing exam performance and rote learning, with excessive homework and memorization—particularly in history. While reforms promote group work and critical thinking, traditional practices still dominate, raising concerns about student well-being and meaningful learning. After 1997, most schools switched from English to Chinese (Cantonese) as the medium of instruction; around 20–30% remain English Medium Instruction (EMI), often considered elite due to higher university acceptance. The government promotes Putonghua (Standard Mandarin) in Chinese language teaching, with growing adoption. Tertiary education has expanded significantly since the 1970s, with government-funded places rising from ~11,500 (1975) to over 59,000 (2000). Many students study abroad. Hong Kong universities offer bachelor’s degrees with honours distinctions (first to third class); undergraduate programmes now last four years (since 2012). Admission is primarily via the Joint University Programmes Admissions System (JUPAS). English remains the primary medium of instruction and assessment. Eight UGC-funded universities (e.g., HKU, CUHK, HKUST) are globally recognized, with HKU, CUHK, and HKUST among the world’s top 100; PolyU, CityU, and HKUST among top 200 or best young universities. Publicly-funded institutions include HKAPA (performing arts); self-financing institutions include HKMU, HKSYU, HSUHK, and others. Adult education is growing, supported by the EMB, evening courses, and institutions like the Open University of Hong Kong. International schools (175 as of 2015) offer diverse curricula (UK, IB), with high fees and strong parent-driven reputations. The system includes government, aided, DSS, and private schools, with a shift from selective to more inclusive secondary education since 2012. Increasing numbers of local Chinese and mainland students are enrolling in international schools.
