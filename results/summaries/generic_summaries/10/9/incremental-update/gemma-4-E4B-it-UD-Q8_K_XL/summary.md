## Comparative Education Systems: Global Trends and National Models

This comparative study analyzes diverse national education systems—including Taiwan, Sri Lanka, Nigeria, Israel, Ireland, Egypt, Iran, Indonesia, Turkey, Armenia, France, Norway, England, Sweden, Ghana, and Hong Kong—against global trends such as AI integration in Open and Distance Learning (ODL).

**National System Variations and Challenges:**
Global models exhibit significant variation: England spans ages 5-18, Sweden mandates free preschool from age one, and France utilizes a secular system culminating in the *baccalauréat*. Norway features a "2+2" apprenticeship model. Many nations face specific issues; Taiwan has 12 years of compulsory education, Sri Lanka suffers from intense competition, Nigeria contends with resource shortages, and Egypt is characterized by rote learning due to centralization. Iran employs a centralized, Islamized structure, while Indonesia faces financial constraints and Turkey deals with state indoctrination. Armenia maintains 100% literacy while addressing child labor, and Ghana mandates education from ages 6 to 14.

**Ghanaian Educational Landscape:**
Ghana's system provides 12 years of free, compulsory basic education (K-JHS) before secondary and tertiary levels, overseen by the Ministry of Education. The state funds teacher salaries and provides free primary textbooks, though infrastructure funding relies on public-private partnerships. Despite anti-illiteracy efforts, TVET implementation is weak due to poor data. Higher education shows rising inequality, particularly impacting affluent males, and while ICT is mandated, device access limits its classroom utility.

**Structural and Regional Benchmarks:**
Sweden's nine-year $\text{grundskola}$ leads into optional upper secondary ($\text{gymnasieskola}$), with tertiary education aligning with the Bologna Process, supported by CSN aid, featuring diverse $\text{Friskolor}$ and vocational tracks. Ireland is recognized for strong PISA scores, and Armenia also adheres to the Bologna Process for higher education.

**Hong Kong's Evolving System:**
Hong Kong features a complex, evolving structure with compulsory, free public primary education and a secondary split (junior/senior years) culminating in the HKDSE. Historically shifting from village schools to government-funded, bilingual institutions since the 1997 TOC adoption, its tertiary sector includes UGC-funded and self-financing universities, with undergraduate programs standardizing to four years taught largely in English via JUPAS. The system encompasses various school types (Government, Aided, etc.) and has historically supported international curricula.
*   **System Dynamics:** The system shows trends toward inclusive special needs education, though moderate to severe cases are excluded, and the Education Bureau assists immigrants with free "Induction" and "Initiation Programmes."
*   **Curricular and Cultural Focus:** The curriculum shifted from the HKCEE to the HKDSE (starting 2012/13), and while new textbooks promote patriotism, a significant portion of secondary education (20-30%) uses English Medium Instruction (EMI) against the government’s Pushonghua promotion. Furthermore, the system places strong emphasis on discipline and exam scores, often supported by cram schools, leading to high workloads and past criticisms regarding mechanical learning, despite reforms introducing group projects. Historically, primary schools used half-day schedules, though most are now full-day, and declining birth rates are spurring discussion on smaller class sizes.
