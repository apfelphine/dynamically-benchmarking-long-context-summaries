# Updated Summary of Education in the Republic of Ireland, Israel, Nigeria, Sri Lanka, Taiwan, Egypt, Iran, Indonesia, Turkey, Armenia, France, Norway, England, Sweden, Ghana, and Hong Kong

## Republic of Ireland  
Education is compulsory from ages 6 to 16 or completion of three secondary years, encompassing free Irish-language preschools, primary, secondary (Junior Cycle and Leaving Certificate), and tertiary education aligned with European standards. Predominantly Roman Catholic schools are becoming more diverse and inclusive. The system performs well internationally despite COVID-19 disruptions.

## Israel  
Education spans kindergarten to grade 12 across state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors. By 2030, Haredi and Arab students will be a majority in elementary education. Challenges include low matriculation and employment among Haredi students, underfunded Arab schools, and STEM gender gaps. Reforms promote integration through anti-discrimination policies. Education spending is about 7.3% of GDP; matriculation exams gate higher education and employment.

## Nigeria  
Nine years of compulsory basic education lead to the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination. Diverse public, private, and religious schools coexist alongside federally funded unity schools. There are 99 universities, including technical institutions. Vocational education faces funding and policy challenges. Female participation increases despite cultural and security barriers. Literacy stands near 69%, with digital literacy initiatives targeting 95% by 2030.

## Sri Lanka  
Multiple ministries manage education from primary to university, with national exams at Grades 5, 11 (O-Level), and 13 (A-Level). The system integrates Buddhist and colonial legacies, offering free multilingual education in over 10,000 schools. Private tutoring is prevalent amid exam pressures. The 15 state universities face graduate unemployment. Vocational education provides formal and non-formal courses but faces critiques regarding system rigidity.

## Taiwan  
Twelve years of compulsory education cover elementary, junior and senior high, with preschool fee waivers. Curriculum emphasizes Mandarin, English from Grade 3, native languages, STEM, arts, and social studies. Approximately 95% of junior high students advance via exams to senior academic or vocational tracks. Higher education includes universities, vocational colleges, and cram schools. Special education supports diverse needs; corporal punishment is banned but occasionally reported.

## Egypt  
Free public schooling (ages 4–14) offers academic and vocational secondary tracks rooted in Islamic traditions. Higher education comprises public and private universities and technical institutes facing quality and autonomy challenges. The Al-Azhar system specializes in Islamic studies. Challenges include uneven quality, widespread private tutoring, low teacher pay, corporal punishment, and access inequities. Enrollment has grown but youth unemployment remains high. Initiatives promote new diplomas and distance education accelerated by COVID-19.

## Iran  
Free, centralized education is compulsory from ages 6 to 15, with optional upper secondary and vocational streams. Spending accounts for about 20% of expenditures and 5% of GDP. Digital and hybrid learning expanded post-pandemic despite rural teacher shortages. The Konkur exam gates higher education. Iran ranks third globally in engineering graduates (70% female) and fifth in STEM graduates; women comprise over 70% of university students. Gifted education exists through selective schools like NODET. The system is culturally aligned, research-oriented, and increasingly inclusive.

## Indonesia  
Mandating 12 years of education—primary, junior, senior high—with free tuition through junior high, Indonesia’s curriculum emphasizes secular and Pancasila civics knowledge. Enrollment rates were 93.5% primary, 78.8% junior high, 60.7% senior high, and 36.3% tertiary (2018). Islamic education includes pesantren and madrasas. Higher education offers academic, vocational, and professional programs within a national framework.

## Turkey  
Turkey’s centralized, secular 12-year system (4+4+4) includes elementary, middle, and secondary education with general and Imam Hatip religious vocational tracks. Public schooling is free; private schools charge tuition. Secondary education offers diverse tracks, culminating in a university entrance exam. Vocational education includes apprenticeships. Over 167 universities enroll more than one million students, some internationally accredited. The system integrates Atatürk’s secular nationalism but has recently increased religious content and reduced Atatürk coverage. Minority education rights remain contentious.

## Armenia  
Achieving universal literacy by 1960, Armenia aligns with European Lisbon and Bologna frameworks. Education is compulsory and free for 12 years, covering primary, middle, and high school with general and specialized tracks. Public schools blend global and Armenian culture; private schools offer innovative curricula. Higher education includes 26 state and 33 private institutions excelling in sciences, engineering, medicine, IT, and Armenian studies, with instruction in multiple languages. Participation in Erasmus+, Jean Monnet, and TEMPUS supports internationalization.

## France  
France emphasizes free, mandatory, secular schooling under the Ministry of National Education. Education begins at age 3 (preschool), followed by elementary, middle, and high school, culminating in the baccalauréat. Private schools include state-contracted and independent faith-based institutions. Higher education offers Licence, Master’s, and Doctorat degrees aligned with Bologna, through public universities and elite Grandes Écoles. Teacher recruitment varies; public schools are secular, with bans on religious symbols except in some regions. About 18 million students attend nationally.

## Norway  
Compulsory education runs from age 6 to 16 (grunnskole), followed by upper secondary (16–19) combining general and vocational "2+2" models merging schooling and apprenticeships. Predominantly public, private schools are rare. Literacy and foundational skills are emphasized. Educators have varying pedagogical qualifications. Higher education includes universities and colleges offering bachelor’s to professional degrees. Special education focuses on inclusion and individualized support. The academic year has two semesters plus breaks.

## England  
Education or training is compulsory from 5 to 18, primarily via state-funded schools (93% attendance), including academies, community, free, foundation, voluntary aided/controlled, university technical colleges, and selective sixth-form maths schools. Private schools serve 7% attendance. The curriculum covers Key Stages 1–4 with mandatory Relationship & Sex Education and Personal, Social, Health and Economic education. Post-16 education includes sixth forms, further education colleges, apprenticeships, traineeships, and vocational qualifications. Universities grant degrees through doctorates; tuition reached £9,535/year (2024–25). Qualifications follow regulated frameworks aligned with Europe. Adult and lifelong education are supported by institutions like the Open University.

British 15-year-olds rank highly in literacy and science (PISA), with improved school performance linked to academies and expanded SEND funding.

## Sweden  
Education evolved from 19th-century parish schools to a 9-year compulsory comprehensive school (grundskola) with national testing and an A–F scale. Preschool is free and widespread from age 3, supporting early learning. Special education includes Sámi schools and needs-based programs; homeschooling is limited.

Upper secondary education (gymnasieskola) is voluntary, divided into preparatory (academic) and vocational national programs, both qualifying for university admission. Preparatory tracks cover Social Science, Business, Natural Science, Technology, and International Baccalaureate; vocational tracks emphasize trades and services. Admission depends on grades or entrance tests.

Higher education comprises public universities and colleges offering Bologna-aligned degrees; SweSAT aids admission. Education is free for Swedish/EU/EEA/Swiss citizens. Independent publicly funded schools (friskolor) offer diverse pedagogies but lack fees and entrance exams, stimulating political debate on equity and quality.

Sweden’s PISA and PIRLS performance is near OECD averages, with past declines and partial recovery, attributed partly to immigration and testing irregularities.

## Ghana  
Post-independence reforms introduced compulsory free 12-year basic education mainly in English, covering kindergarten through tertiary, with over 6 million enrolled. The system divides Basic (Kindergarten, Primary, JHS), Secondary (SHS and TVET), and Tertiary education. Key exams include BECE and WASSCE. Vocational education remains largely informal despite policies and frameworks like COTVET and STEP.

Access to tertiary education is competitive and unequal; female, rural, and low-income underrepresentation persists, though targeted initiatives have improved female enrollment. TVET faces challenges in reputation, completion, facilities, and employment. Education funding consumes 23–25% of government expenditure, with significant private sector basic-level delivery due to funding constraints. Teacher training occurs at Colleges of Education and universities, supplemented by distance education. Adult literacy is ~71.5%. ICT integration is a policy priority but access and pedagogical use remain limited.

## Hong Kong  
Blending British colonial and Chinese traditions, Hong Kong offers bilingual, bicultural education with compulsory free schooling from ages 6 to 18. The system comprises three years of kindergarten (voluntary), six years of primary, and six of secondary (divided into junior and senior levels), culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE). Some schools offer the International Baccalaureate. Most local secondary schools teach mainly in Cantonese; 20–30% elite schools use English-medium instruction (EMI), correlating with higher university acceptance. International curricula include UK, US AP, and IB programs, with limited regulatory oversight. Enrollment in international schools by local Chinese students is rising.

School types include government, aided (charitable/religious funded), Direct Subsidy Scheme (DSS) with flexible curricula and fees, caput (pupil-funded), private local and international schools, plus the English Schools Foundation serving partly English-speaking students.

Special education shifted toward integration since the 2000s, mainstreaming most special schools except those for moderate to severe intellectual disabilities. The Education Bureau supports non-Cantonese-speaking and immigrant children, including large Pakistani and increasing white student populations attending Cantonese-medium schools.

Adult education grows among mature learners via evening classes supported by non-profits, Vocational Training Council, and the Open University. Vocational education is regulated and accessed through commerce or external programs. Digital integration, such as ChatGPT use in universities, reflects technological adaptation. Hong Kong hosts MIT’s innovation node and weekend Japanese supplementary education. Regional cooperation with Guangdong under the Greater Bay Area agreement promotes education and talent development. Recent reforms propose shortening secondary education to two years.

Historically, half-day schooling addressed high demand with some Saturday sessions; improvements and declining birth rates led to full-day schools, smaller classes, and discussions of promoting small class teaching to alleviate teacher workload and enhance student support.

The system emphasizes strong discipline with reported misbehavior and detailed Education Bureau guidelines outlining school discipline roles and strategies.

Culturally, the education system faces criticism as "spoon-fed," focusing on rote learning and exam scores over critical thinking. Cram schools are prevalent, and despite reforms encouraging group work and open-ended tasks, excessive workload and memorization—particularly in subjects like history—remain common concerns.

## Overall Themes  
Global education systems vary widely in structure, governance, language, and inclusion, balancing secular and religious influences. Common challenges include equitable access, quality assurance, vocational education status, teacher shortages, gender gaps, and digital transformation. STEM education and inclusion are priorities in contexts like Iran, Taiwan, and Sweden. Sociocultural and resource constraints affect outcomes globally. Lifelong learning, international cooperation, and ongoing reforms underpin global educational advancement.
