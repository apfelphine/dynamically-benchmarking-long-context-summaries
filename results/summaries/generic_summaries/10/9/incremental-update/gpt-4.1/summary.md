**Summary**

### Education in Ireland

Ireland enforces compulsory education from ages 6 to 16, featuring Irish language instruction and government-supported preschool. Secondary education concludes with state exams, and recent reforms emphasize literacy and curriculum outcomes.

### Education in Israel

Israel’s system is divided into secular, religious, Arab, and Haredi streams, with compulsory schooling through grade 12. Disparities exist, but Christian Arab and female students excel in non-STEM fields. University admission depends on the Bagrut exam, and ongoing reforms target curriculum updates.

### Education in Nigeria

Nigeria offers nine years of free, compulsory education across academic and vocational tracks, assessed by national exams. Private schools outperform public ones, and challenges in literacy, equity, digital/girls’ access, and funding are addressed through international partnerships.

### Education in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka provides free, compulsory education through Grade 9, shaped by colonial and religious traditions. Admission to university is competitive; alternatives include vocational and tertiary commissions. Unemployment affecting graduates outside select fields is common.

### Education in Taiwan

Taiwan mandates twelve years of education across academic and vocational streams, delivered bilingually. High tertiary enrollment has led to a surplus of graduates, with policy focusing on teacher development and support for gifted and disabled students.

### Education in Egypt

Egypt’s system serves ages 4–14 via public, private, and international schools. While access has improved, challenges remain around quality, teacher training, academic support, youth unemployment, and parallel Islamic education.

### Education in Iran

Iran’s Islamic system features centralized control, Persian-language instruction, and programs expanding elementary, distance, and research offerings. University entry relies on the Konkour exam, female STEM participation is high, but marginalized groups face access barriers.

### Education in Indonesia

Indonesia provides twelve years of compulsory education in academic, vocational, and special needs tracks. Resource disparities and rote learning persist, especially in rural areas. Recognition of Islamic schools varies; higher ed includes universities and polytechnics.

### Education in Turkey

Turkey uses a "4+4+4" compulsory model, with academic, vocational, technical, and religious streams. Recent reforms stress religious/national content, minority language restrictions, private school demand, and university challenges.

### Education in Armenia

Armenia transitioned from Soviet to European models, joining the Bologna Process and modernizing schools with EU support. Schooling covers primary to high school, with private options and exams for university entry.

### Education in France

France provides centrally managed, secular, free compulsory education, ending in baccalauréat or vocational diplomas. State-regulated private schools serve mainly religious education. Teacher qualification relies on exams; higher education is selective and EU-aligned.

### Education in Norway

Norway’s compulsory education features diverse pathways and robust special needs services. Universities adhere to Bologna standards and enjoy strong international rankings.

### Education in England

England delivers free state education for ages 3–18 across various school types and supports home education. Compulsory schooling runs to age 16, followed by academic/vocational options. Higher education features capped fees, diverse postgraduate tracks, and evolving funding.

### Education in Sweden

Sweden’s system offers partially free preschool, compulsory grundskola, and competitive gymnasieskola. Special support is provided for Sámi and disabled students, while adult education, homeschooling, and friskolor contribute to ongoing debates on equality. Reforms target migration, assessment, and grade inflation.

### Education in Ghana

Ghana’s Ministry oversees decentralized, twelve-year free compulsory education, leading to vocational or academic tracks after BECE. Completion rates, quality, and access remain challenges; equity initiatives support disadvantaged groups. Tertiary expansion and ICT adoption are underway, with private providers prominent. Adult literacy efforts have yielded improved outcomes, especially for women and rural populations.

### Education in Hong Kong

Hong Kong mandates free primary and secondary education, covering multiple school types: government, aided, DSS, caput, private, international, and the English Schools Foundation. DSS schools have more autonomy, and curricula vary (local, UK, IB). Recent years brought a shift from half-day to full-day schedules, smaller classes due to declining birth rates, and debates over their effectiveness. Discipline is emphasized via EDB policies and school-based discipline teams. The educational culture is often criticized for its emphasis on rote learning and exam success, heavy workloads, and excessive homework, despite curriculum reforms promoting group projects and open-ended assignments. Problems persist with student engagement and history education. Special education increasingly favors inclusion, while non-local students receive language support. International schools cater to expatriates and locals, with rising demand and variable fees. An expanded tertiary sector emphasizes international partnerships and innovation. There is growing use of AI in universities, staff/student departures, and continued curriculum reforms, including national security themes and adult education opportunities.
