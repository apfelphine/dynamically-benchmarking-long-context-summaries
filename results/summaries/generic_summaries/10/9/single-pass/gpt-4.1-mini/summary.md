# Education Summaries by Country

---

## Republic of Ireland
The Irish education system mandates schooling from ages 5 to 16, encompassing primary and secondary education, with free upper second-level education introduced in 1967. Primary education includes Junior/Senior Infants through Sixth Class, while secondary education is structured around a Junior Cycle (culminating in the Junior Certificate), an optional Transition Year, and a Senior Cycle ending with the Leaving Certificate. Education is predominantly English-medium, except in Gaelscoileanna where Irish is primary. Schools vary by patronage, with most under church control, primarily Catholic, though multidenominational and secular schools have increased. Higher education is mainly English-medium with some courses in other languages. Special needs education is supported by legislation and additional assistants. The Department of Education runs programs aimed at disadvantaged schools and promotes equality of opportunity. International students are welcomed, and school years are structured with standard breaks. COVID-19 led to school closures in 2020.

## Israel
Israeli education is divided into primary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), and high school (10–12), with compulsory education through grade 12. Schools are categorized into state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors, with growing proportions of Haredi and Arab pupils. Haredi education focuses heavily on religious studies, with low matriculation exam participation. Integrated Jewish-Arab schools exist but are few. The Pupils’ Rights Law prohibits sectarian discrimination. Education is culturally and socially valued, underpinning Israel’s high tertiary attainment rates (46%). Higher education requires matriculation and a psychometric test. Israeli universities rank well globally, especially in STEM. The Arab sector faces resource disparities and lower matriculation rates, though affirmative actions and NGO programs aim to close gaps. Gender disparities exist in dropout and matriculation rates, but women dominate higher education enrollment. Teachers’ low pay and union strikes challenge the system. Textbooks and curricula have been criticized for bias, and student/faculty strikes have occurred. Awards recognize outstanding education initiatives.

## Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system follows a 6-3-3-4 or 9-3-4 structure: six years of primary, three years each of junior and senior secondary, and tertiary education. The Universal Basic Education program mandates free, compulsory schooling for nine years. Primary and secondary education curricula include core and elective subjects, with examinations such as SSCE. Federal and state governments fund schools differently; federal schools tend to be better resourced. Private schools are often expensive but offer better facilities and qualified teachers. Tertiary education comprises public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges, with increasing numbers of private institutions. Vocational education and informal apprenticeships are significant but face challenges including outdated curricula and political instability. Literacy rates remain low, particularly in northern regions, with ongoing literacy programs and digital literacy targets. Female education faces barriers including cultural factors and insecurity, with UNICEF supporting initiatives to improve girls’ enrollment and retention. Rural education remains underdeveloped. International and advanced level programs like IJMB and JUPEB facilitate university access.

## Sri Lanka
Sri Lankan education is managed by multiple ministries overseeing schools, universities, and vocational training. Education has a 2300-year history with Buddhist monastic origins, Christian missionary influences during colonial times, and post-independence reforms establishing free education. The system divides into primary (grades 1–5), junior secondary (6–9), senior secondary (10–11), collegiate (12–13), and tertiary education. Students must pass national exams (Scholarship exam, G.C.E. Ordinary and Advanced Levels) for progression. Schools are mostly government-run (national and provincial), with private, international, semi-government, and madrasas also present. Tuition is free but competitive exams and private tutoring are widespread. Higher education is highly competitive and primarily public, with few private universities due to opposition. Vocational education is managed separately with NVQ levels. Recent criticisms include rigidity and limited private university options. Leadership training for undergraduates was mandatory from 2011 to 2015.

## Taiwan
Taiwan has a 12-year compulsory education system spanning preschool, elementary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and senior high school (10–12), with vocational tracks available. Public education is mostly in Mandarin, with instruction in Taiwanese and Hakka languages optional. High school students choose science or liberal arts tracks; vocational high schools emphasize practical skills. Higher education follows an American-like system with universities awarding bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees; many public and private universities exist. Teacher training is conducted in universities and normal universities. Private education, including numerous cram schools, are popular due to cultural emphasis on achievement. Special education and nursing education are available. Taiwan is a global destination for learning Mandarin and hosts international students. Vocational education includes junior colleges and universities of science and technology.

## Egypt
Egypt’s educational tradition dates to ancient times, with early institutions like the “House of Life” and the al-Azhar system. Modern education was introduced in the 19th century under Muhammad Ali, blending traditional Islamic and Western models. British occupation limited expansion and access. Post-1952 reforms under Nasser instituted free public education and expanded access. The current system is centralized with compulsory basic education (ages 4–14) followed by secondary (general and technical) education. Tertiary education includes numerous public and private universities and technical institutes. Quality remains uneven; test scores have lagged internationally, and private tutoring is widespread due to weaknesses in public schooling. The parallel Al-Azhar system focuses on religious education, predominately for Muslims. Agricultural education is extensive. Challenges include teacher quality, funding, equity gaps, corporal punishment, and high youth unemployment. COVID-19 accelerated distance education adoption. International schools offer foreign curricula. Higher education reforms continue amid ongoing quality concerns.

## Iran
Iran’s education has ancient roots, with early academies and religious schools. Modern education reform began in Qajar times, accelerating during the Pahlavi era with Western models, and was Islamized after the 1979 Revolution, including gender segregation and revised curricula. The system is centralized under the Ministry of Education, providing free and private schools at all levels. Compulsory education lasts grades 1–9, with upper secondary optional. Higher education enrollment is high, especially among women, who constitute over 70% of university students. Admission is highly competitive via the national Konkour exam. Universities have been modernized and rank well regionally. Foreign languages include Arabic, English, and increasingly French. Distance education is expanding. Government reforms aim at vocational training and globalized education, though privatization and teacher shortages are growing concerns. Islamic prayer and politically motivated educational controls have increased. Private high schools have surged in cost.

## Indonesia
Indonesia’s education history includes Islamic pesantren, Dutch colonial stratified education, Japanese occupation disruptions, and post-independence nationalist reforms. The Ministry of Education administers secular and religious schools separately. Education is compulsory for 12 years, including primary (6 years), junior secondary (3 years), and senior secondary (3 years), with vocational tracks. Early childhood education is widespread but largely private. The majority instruction medium is Indonesian. Islamic schools (pesantren, madrasah) coexist with public schools. Teacher qualifications have been raised. Higher education institutions include universities, polytechnics, and academies. A national qualifications framework aligns levels from certificates to doctorates. Educational challenges include teacher shortages in remote areas and infrastructure deficits. The curriculum emphasizes secular national values but accommodates religious education. International education is growing, along with vocational and skill-based education alternatives.

## Turkey
Post-Republic Turkey unified and secularized education, closing religious schools and adopting the Latin alphabet. Primary education lasts 8 years and is compulsory from ages 6 to 14, with a new 4+4+4 system dividing education into three 4-year stages and extended compulsory schooling to 12 years. Secondary education includes general and vocational tracks. Higher education is state and foundation university-based, with around 76 universities as of early 2000s. Foreign languages like English are taught broadly. Religious education, initially removed, was reintroduced as a subject. Education includes a heavy nationalistic and Atatürk-focused indoctrination. Curricula and exam systems have been frequently modified, drawing criticism. Private and minority schools exist under specific treaties. Turkish universities vary in quality, with several technical universities recognized internationally. Turkey is a major destination for international students with government scholarship initiatives.

## Armenia
Armenia boasts near-universal literacy and a Soviet-influenced historically centralized education system that has since integrated Armenian cultural emphasis and shifted to Armenian as primary instruction language. The K-12 system consists of preschool (ages 2–6), primary (grades 1–4), middle/basic (5–9), and high school (10–12) with compulsory free education. Secondary education offers general and specialized tracks with national exams determining university eligibility. The majority of schools are public; private schools operate independently but must meet national standards. Armenia has numerous higher education institutions, including Yerevan State University and specialized academies, with increasing integration to European educational frameworks such as the Bologna Process and Erasmus programs. Absenteeism and dropouts linked to child labor remain concerns. Emerging tech-focused centers offer modern education and skills development.

## France
France’s education system has roots in 19th-century reforms emphasizing secular, mandatory, and free schooling. The Ministry of National Education centrally controls curriculum and teacher employment. Education is divided into maternelle (preschool), école élémentaire (primary), collège (middle school), and lycée (high school). Public schools teach a uniform national curriculum, preparing students for national exams like the baccalauréat. Private schools operate either under contract with the state or independently with different curricula. Higher education features universities and prestigious grandes écoles, with selective preparatory classes (CPGEs) leading to elite institutions. Tuition fees are low due to state funding; financial aid supports low-income students. The system emphasizes literature, history, and integrates European standards via the Bologna process. Teacher recruitment includes competitive exams (Agrégation, CAPES). Religion is excluded from public schools except in specific regions, following strict secularism laws.

## Norway
Norwegian education is compulsory from 6 to 16 years, divided into barneskole (primary, grades 1–7), ungdomsskole (lower secondary, grades 8–10), and videregående skole (upper secondary, grades 11–13). The system reformed to integrate general and vocational studies, with apprenticeships common. Higher education includes universities (theoretical), university colleges (combined academic and vocational), and private institutions. The grading system ranges from 1–6 or scale aligned with Bologna. Special education emphasizes inclusion. Teachers have distinct qualifications according to level taught. Education reforms have increased access and curriculum breadth, including the introduction of electives and IT integration. National standardized tests exist in grades 3, 6, and 9. Norway balances public and few private schools tendered as alternatives.

## England
Education in England is compulsory from age 5 to 18, with state-funded and private sectors. The National Curriculum guides education and assessments at multiple Key Stages: Early Years, KS1–KS4, and post-16 (sixth form). Primary education covers ages 5–11; secondary 11–16 or 18 with further education options including A-levels, vocational qualifications, and apprenticeships. Schools include community, academies, free schools, faith schools, grammar (selective), and independent schools. Universities offer undergraduate and postgraduate degrees with tuition fees (up to £9,535). The system emphasizes standardization, inspections by Ofsted, and offers diverse pathways including vocational training. Home education is permitted. England ranks well in international assessments though challenges remain. Special programs and international schools exist. Adult education and lifelong learning are widely supported.

## Sweden
Sweden’s education system offers compulsory schooling from ages 6/7 to 15/16 via grundskola, with preschool provided prior. The curriculum includes 16 compulsory subjects. Grading starts at year 6 using a six-level scale (A-F). Upper secondary education (gymnasieskola) provides preparatory and vocational national programs, with a diploma project requirement. Higher education aligns with Bologna, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, with a variety of grading systems and standardized entrance exams (SweSAT). Independent (free) schools coexist with public schools, funded publicly but privately managed, and are subject to national standards. PISA performance is near OECD average, with concerns over immigrant student performance and testing integrity. School breaks, discipline guidelines, and anti-discrimination policies are institutionalized.

## Ghana
Ghana’s education evolved from informal oral traditions to formal mission and colonial schools, expanding post-independence with free universal basic education and a 12-year pre-tertiary system. It comprises kindergarten, six years of primary, three years junior high, and three years senior high or vocational education. Access increased over time, but challenges include teacher shortages, funding disparities, and vocational education stigma. Tertiary education includes universities, polytechnics, and colleges. Literacy rates and enrollment have improved but gender and socioeconomic disparities persist. The education system is overseen by the Ministry of Education and agencies, supported by international donors. ICT use is growing, but infrastructure and quality gaps remain. Private education has grown, complementing public efforts.

## Hong Kong
Education in Hong Kong blends British colonial legacies and Chinese cultural elements. Pre-primary education is fee-based but subsidized. Primary and secondary education is compulsory up to age 15/18, administered by the Education Bureau, with public and aided schools dominant alongside private and international schools. The medium of instruction shifted from English to Chinese (Cantonese and Putonghua), with some EMI schools designated as elite. Senior secondary culminates in the HKDSE exam; options include international curricula. Higher education is highly competitive with eight UGC-funded universities and several self-financing institutions. Trends include increased emigration of students and teachers since the national security law. Curricula have been adjusted to emphasize patriotism and conformity. Adult, special, and immigrant education are provided. International schools are numerous and varied, serving local and expatriate populations. The education culture is rigorous, often criticized for rote learning and high pressure.
