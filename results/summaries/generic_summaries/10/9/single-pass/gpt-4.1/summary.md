# Comparative Summary of Education Systems

## Republic of Ireland
Ireland’s education system is highly centralized, with free compulsory education for children aged 6–16, though most start school between ages 4–5. The structure includes primary (eight years), secondary (Junior and Senior Cycle, plus optional Transition Year), and tertiary (universities and colleges). While English is predominant, Irish remains a core subject. Most schools have religious patrons, mainly Catholic, though multi- and non-denominational schools are increasing. Secondary education features voluntary secondary schools, community colleges, and comprehensive schools, with a curriculum emphasizing broad, general knowledge. Recent reforms target inclusivity, special needs, and areas of disadvantage (DEIS program). Ireland ranks highly in international student assessments and attracts many international students.

## Israel
Israel’s three-tier system covers primary, middle, and high school, with compulsory education from kindergarten through grade 12. Schools follow secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab tracks. Education is highly valued culturally, contributing to socioeconomic mobility and the high-tech economy; around 46% of Israelis hold tertiary degrees. However, gaps persist between educational tracks, especially for Haredim and Israeli Arabs, who face underfunding and higher dropout rates. The dominant path to higher education is the bagrut (matriculation), central to university and job prospects. Despite a high university attainment rate, performance on international standardized tests shows substantial inequality. Women have higher rates of academic success but remain underrepresented in STEM faculty positions.

## Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system comprises six years of primary, three years of junior and three years of senior secondary, plus varying lengths of tertiary education (universities, polytechnics, colleges). Universal Basic Education (UBE) aims for nine years of free, compulsory schooling. Quality varies markedly across public, state, and private schools, with private and federal schools often superior. Vocational, technical, informal, and adult literacy programs exist, but face funding and implementation challenges. While female education rates have improved, regional disparities and security issues still severely disrupt access and literacy rates. Tertiary education has expanded, with diverse entry routes and programmes, but underfunding, infrastructure issues, and inequality persist.

## Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka offers free, compulsory education from primary through grade 9, with a centralized curriculum managed by various ministries. Historical religious and language influences have produced a diverse array of schools (Buddhist, Hindu, Muslim, Christian). Secondary education culminates in national Ordinary Level and Advanced Level exams for university entry, but limited university spots mean many pursue vocational or overseas education. The country also features private, international, and semi-government schools, with tuition classes popular. Recent reforms address competition, access, and calls for private universities, though resistance remains.

## Taiwan
Taiwan’s highly regulated education system is now twelve years compulsory, including six years of elementary, three of junior high, and three of senior high. Instruction is in Mandarin, with English and native languages included. Secondary students choose academic or vocational tracks, progressing to university or employment. Universities have proliferated, with high participation rates and a preference for STEM fields. Numerous public and private universities exist, with many private supplementary cram schools. Taiwan’s system is competitive, exam-oriented, and features strong teacher training and professional development requirements. Vocational and junior colleges supplement traditional university pathways.

## Egypt
Egypt’s education is highly centralized and segmented into public, private, and religious (Al-Azhar) systems. Free compulsory education spans nine years, but quality issues persist due to overcrowding, low teacher status/wages, and reliance on rote memorization. Secondary education offers general and technical tracks, recently supplemented by Applied Technology Schools. Higher education is extensive but hampered by underfunding and rising student numbers. International and private schools provide alternative curricula, mainly for the affluent. Reforms aim to address inequity, efficiency, and quality, but challenges include low teacher qualifications, wealth disparities in access, and youth unemployment.

## Iran
Iran’s education system is centralized, with a six-year elementary and three-year lower-secondary mandatory education. Upper-secondary offers theoretical, technical/vocational, and manual skills branches. Higher education is competitive, admission via a national exam (konkur), and tightly regulated. The system emphasizes Islamic values post-1979, with segregated schools and religious content. There is a significant teacher shortage, rapid privatization (especially in cities), and growing inequity. Women now dominate university enrollment. Hybrid and distance learning are expanding, but regional disparities and dropouts remain issues. Iran produces many STEM graduates and invests heavily in education as a share of government spending.

## Indonesia
Indonesia mandates twelve years of schooling: six years primary, three junior secondary, and three senior secondary, with academic/vocational and Islamic school options. Curriculum is centrally set but has become more localized post-1998. Disparities persist, with urban and private schools often outperforming rural and public institutions. Teacher shortages and quality issues remain, especially in remote regions. Higher education includes academic, vocational, and professional tracks with universities, institutes, and colleges, and entry is competitive. Islamic education (pesantren, madrasah) is an important alternative, and private/charter school numbers are growing.

## Turkey
Turkey provides 12 years of compulsory education, now structured as 4+4+4: primary, lower secondary, and upper secondary, with public, private, vocational, and religious (Imam Hatip) schools. The Ministry of National Education oversees curriculum and school operations. Secondary students follow academic or vocational tracks, with high-stakes university entrance exams for higher education. Universities are distinguished, with technical fields having high reputation. Recent years have seen frequent curriculum changes, politicization in textbooks, expansion of religious content, fluctuating teacher requirements, and debate over private schools and educational standards.

## Armenia
Armenia has universal compulsory education (grades 1–12), with high literacy rates since the Soviet era. The system has shifted to emphasize Armenian language and culture, with Russian and English as key second languages. Schooling is divided into preschool, primary, middle, and high school, culminating in state exams. Both public (97%) and private schools operate under strict licensing and curricular standards. Tertiary education includes 26 state universities, a strong tradition in scientific and technical fields, and increasing internationalization and quality assurance partnerships. Despite high enrollment, student absenteeism and equity concerns (gender, rural, wealth) remain.

## France
France maintains a centralized, secular education system with free, mandatory schooling from ages 3 to 16. The curriculum and timetable are standardized nationally, emphasizing French identity, literature, and history. Primary and secondary schooling (collège and lycée) culminates in the baccalauréat for higher education entry. Private and international schools exist, mostly following the national curriculum; religious instruction is restricted by law. Higher education divides between universities and “grandes écoles,” the latter highly prestigious and selective, feeding into elite professions. Fees are low for EU students, with extensive public funding. Teacher recruitment is competitive; standards are maintained by state oversight and inspection.

## Norway
Norway’s system builds on egalitarian and inclusive principles. Elementary and lower secondary education (ages 6–16) is compulsory, with extensive public provision and few private schools. Upper secondary (ages 16–19) offers academic and vocational streams, and is largely state funded. Higher education is provided by universities and university colleges, mostly public, with admissions based on GPA from prior schooling. Teacher training is rigorous, and special education is integrated within mainstream schools, supported by specialized services. Norway’s education model is highly inclusive, prioritizes student welfare, and benefits from substantial government investment.

## England
England mandates education from ages 5–18, with students following a sequence of primary, secondary, and post-16 education (GCSEs, A-levels, vocational/apprenticeships). State education is largely free, though an independent private sector exists for about 7% of children. School types include academies, community, foundation, faith, free schools, UTCs, and selective grammar schools. National curriculum is standardized, emphasizing broad skills and core academic subjects. Post-16 students may opt for further education, apprenticeships, or vocational training. Higher education comprises universities and colleges, with a wide array of qualifications. University tuition fees are high, but loans and bursaries are available. Educational attainment is high by international standards, with strong focus on inspection and accountability.

## Sweden
Sweden provides nine years of compulsory schooling (typically ages 7–16), followed by non-compulsory but highly attended three-year gymnasium (upper secondary) with vocational and academic tracks. Preschool is widely available, and independent “friskolor” (free schools) receive state funding. Grading is standardized nationally, and national tests assess students at key stages. Higher education follows the Bologna Process, with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees offered at universities and university colleges. Tuition is free for EU/EEA students; fees apply for others. The system is characterized by inclusivity, quality assurance, substantial vocational and adult education, and strong parental choice.

## Ghana
Ghana’s education system comprises basic education (12 years: kindergarten, primary, junior high), secondary (senior high, technical/vocational), and tertiary (universities, polytechnics). Education is compulsory and partly free at the basic level, though resources and access remain uneven. Policy reforms over decades have worked to expand access, address teacher training and encourage technical/vocational education. Enrollment is high in primary, but completion rates drop in later stages. Private and international schools supplement public provision, while donor and community partnerships are important. Equity issues—gender, rural access, and socioeconomic barriers—persist, but new technology and scholarship initiatives aim to reduce gaps.

## Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education system is built around compulsory primary and secondary education, with significant government, aided, and private/international schools. English and Chinese are principal mediums of instruction, with shifts toward Putonghua/Standard Mandarin. Secondary education is divided into junior and senior cycles, ending in the HKDSE or International Baccalaureate. Higher education is competitive, with extensive participation abroad, and the territory hosts many high-ranked universities. International schools, adult, and special education expand options. Socio-political changes have tightened curriculum control and increased emphasis on national security and patriotism in recent years.
