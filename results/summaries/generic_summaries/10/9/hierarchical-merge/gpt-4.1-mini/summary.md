# Education Systems in Europe, the Middle East, Asia, and Africa

Education systems across these regions reflect diverse cultural, political, and religious contexts, with common themes of expanding access, improving quality, and addressing equity challenges.

## Europe and the Middle East

Ireland mandates education from ages 6 to 16, offering a primarily state-funded system historically influenced by the Catholic Church but increasingly diverse in school types. Israel’s compulsory education through grade 12 features distinct sectors for secular, religious, and Arab students, alongside integrated schools promoting coexistence. Both countries face inclusivity challenges, yet Ireland performs well internationally, and Israel boasts strong university achievements particularly in science and engineering.

Iran’s system is centrally controlled with integrated Islamic and gender-segregated schooling, while Turkey provides 12 years of compulsory education balancing academic and vocational paths, albeit with restrictions on minority language instruction. Armenia offers free, culturally focused K-12 education, aligning with European frameworks but confronting dropout challenges.

France operates a centralized secular system culminating in the baccalauréat and boasts selective Grandes écoles, Norway emphasizes inclusivity and flexible academic/vocational upper secondary education, and England has a stratified landscape with compulsory schooling up to 18, diverse school types, national qualifications like GCSEs and A-levels, and expanding post-16 pathways including apprenticeships and technical qualifications.

## Asia

Indonesia mandates 12 years of education managed by multiple ministries to accommodate Islamic and secular schools, with high primary enrollment but variation in quality and rural access. Sri Lanka’s structured five-stage system includes elite schools and a vibrant private tutoring culture addressing educational gaps.

Sweden provides free comprehensive education from preschool through upper secondary, balancing vocational and academic tracks with provisions for minorities and adult learners. Its higher education is publicly funded, with debate over independent schools’ roles.

Hong Kong offers a multi-tiered system with diverse curricula (local and international), shifting from rote learning to active and patriotic education, expanding inclusive special education, and fostering international schooling and cross-border collaboration, while addressing student stress and workload.

## Africa

Egypt provides free basic education rooted in a rich tradition but struggles with underfunding, large classes, and teacher shortages, prompting reforms and expanding distance learning amid high graduate unemployment. Nigeria features six years each of primary and secondary schooling with many tertiary institutions, improving adult literacy and digital skills despite persistent gender and rural disparities. Ghana’s centrally governed system has improved access and gender parity through a structured 20-year pathway, yet vocational education and equity remain challenges addressed by scholarships, digital innovation, and adult literacy efforts.

---

Overall, these education systems integrate state and private provisions while striving to enhance inclusivity, teacher quality, and alignment with labor market needs, navigating historical legacies and sociopolitical complexities in rapidly changing global contexts.
