# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in the Republic of Ireland, Israel, Nigeria, Sri Lanka, Taiwan, Iran, Indonesia, Turkey, Armenia, France, Norway, England, Sweden, Ghana, and Hong Kong**

## **Core Features and Comparative Insights**

The education systems across these 14 countries reveal a rich tapestry of national identities, historical legacies, and socio-political priorities shaping access, equity, and quality. Ireland, Taiwan, Norway, and France stand out for their high international performance, policy coherence, and commitment to inclusivity. Ireland’s inclusive, multilingual system ranks second in PISA reading and 11th in mathematics, supported by free preschool, targeted interventions (e.g., DEIS), and alignment with European standards. Taiwan achieves near-universal tertiary enrollment (over 95% of graduates enter university), driven by a robust public system, strong vocational integration, and world-class institutions like National Taiwan University and National Tsing Hua, underpinned by a credit-based framework and growing global appeal.

In contrast, Nigeria and Sri Lanka face deep-rooted structural and socio-economic challenges. Nigeria’s six-year elementary and 6-3-3-4 secondary structure is hampered by teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and poor labor market alignment, with literacy at 62.02% (2018) and gender disparities exacerbated by insecurity. Sri Lanka’s highly selective system—where less than 16% of qualified applicants gain university admission—favors urban elites, leading to widespread graduate unemployment and brain drain, while rigid academic tracks and limited private universities restrict access. Indonesia offers a secular, compulsory 12-year system with strong primary completion, but secondary and tertiary participation remains low, with *pesantren* and *madrasah* playing a significant role in shaping religious and cultural identity. Iran’s centralized, Islamized system emphasizes religious values, critical thinking, and STEM output, with strong female participation in higher education, though it struggles with teacher shortages and high dropout rates.

Turkey promotes a state-driven nationalist narrative—denying the Armenian genocide and emphasizing Turkish secularism—while expanding international academic ties and offering accessible public universities. Armenia has transitioned to a multilingual, EU-integrated system with strong performance in medicine, engineering, and IT, supported by innovation hubs like Tumo and COAF SMART, though student absenteeism and child labor remain pressing. Sweden delivers nine years of compulsory comprehensive education, emphasizing equity and foundational skills, with two main secondary pathways—academic and vocational—leading to a required diploma project. Higher education follows the Bologna Process, with strong performance in early childhood and recent improvements in PISA and PIRLS scores, though concerns over test integrity and equity persist.

England features a state-funded, largely comprehensive system with 93% of children attending free state schools, guided by the National Curriculum and inspected by Ofsted. It includes mandatory RSE and PSHE, with diverse post-16 pathways including A-levels, BTECs, T Levels, and apprenticeships. While British 15-year-olds rank 13th globally in literacy, math, and science (average score: 503.7), rising tuition fees and funding pressures threaten equity. Hong Kong offers free public primary and secondary education, culminating in the HKDSE or IB, with a curriculum now emphasizing patriotism and national security under the National Security Law. The system faces declining enrollment, teacher departures, and curricular shifts that downplay colonial history, while also grappling with excessive exam focus and rote learning. Despite reforms promoting critical thinking and group work, concerns persist about academic rigidity.

## **Cross-Cutting Themes and Challenges**

Across all systems, key challenges emerge in equity, inclusion, teacher quality, curriculum ideology, and vocational integration:

- **Equity and Access**: Nigeria, Ghana, and Sri Lanka struggle with low tertiary participation, regional disparities, and socio-economic barriers. Turkey and Iran show progress in access but face deepening inequalities in funding and curriculum. Sweden and Norway lead in equitable access, with universal support for special needs and inclusive design.
- **Inclusion and Diversity**: Ireland and Norway lead in inclusive practices, integrating special education and linguistic diversity. Israel promotes coexistence through integrated schools like Neve Shalom, while Turkey, Iran, and Hong Kong maintain religious or national narratives that limit pluralism.
- **Teacher Quality and Infrastructure**: Teacher shortages and poor working conditions are widespread—especially in Iran, Nigeria, rural Turkey, and Ghana—due to low pay, high workload, and inadequate training. Norway and France emphasize professional development and autonomy, while Iran and Armenia rely on centralized training.
- **Curriculum and Ideology**: National narratives shape curricula—Turkey’s denial of the Armenian genocide, Iran’s religious mandates, France’s *laïcité* and elite focus, and Hong Kong’s shift toward patriotism and national security illustrate how education reinforces political and cultural identities.
- **Vocational and Tertiary Integration**: Taiwan, Norway, and Sweden lead in integrating vocational pathways with academic tracks. Ghana and Nigeria remain fragmented, with underdeveloped and underfunded TVET systems. Hong Kong’s vocational stream is formalized but constrained by political and social pressures.

## **Conclusion**

These education systems demonstrate how national context—cultural traditions, economic priorities, and historical legacies—shapes both opportunities and persistent gaps. Ireland and Taiwan exemplify high-quality, inclusive, and accessible models. Norway and Sweden offer mature, equitable, and student-centered frameworks. France and England represent elite-driven systems with strong research and international ties, though equity and access remain contested. Iran and Armenia show strong performance in STEM and gender equity despite structural constraints. Ghana and Hong Kong highlight the enduring impact of poverty, conflict, political change, and underfunding on educational outcomes. Sustainable reform requires addressing teacher shortages, improving infrastructure, expanding digital access, and fostering inclusive, pluralistic curricula that reflect national diversity and global engagement. Ultimately, while all systems strive for excellence and equity, long-term success depends on aligning education policy with social justice, economic needs, and democratic values.
