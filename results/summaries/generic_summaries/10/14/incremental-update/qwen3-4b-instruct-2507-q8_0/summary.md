### **Updated Working Summary of Education in Armenia, Norway, Indonesia, Sweden, Egypt, Hong Kong, Sri Lanka, Ghana, Israel, England, the Republic of Ireland, France, Nigeria, Turkey, Taiwan, and Iran**

Armenia maintains a highly developed, 100% literate education system with strong emphasis on national history, culture, and language, though numeracy historically lagged. K–12 education is compulsory and free, with a national curriculum in public schools (97% of institutions), while private schools offer specialized programs. Higher education includes 26 state and 33 private universities, aligned with European standards via the Bologna Process and Erasmus+ participation. Challenges include rising absenteeism and dropout rates, linked to child labor and poor perceived quality, offset by initiatives like Tumo and COAF SMART in 21st-century skills.

Norway’s education system, rooted in medieval cathedral schools and formalized through the *grunnskole*, is comprehensive and publicly dominated, with compulsory education from age 6 to 16. The system features a clear structure across elementary, lower, and upper secondary levels, with grading on a 1–6 scale and a hybrid system post-Bologna. Higher education includes universities, university colleges, and private institutions, with no formal distinction between vocational and academic paths. Graduation rates are 73%, and reforms like *Kunnskapsløftet* emphasize flexible transitions and general studies. Teacher qualifications are tiered, and special education is fully integrated.

Indonesia has the fourth-largest education system globally, with free primary-to-high-school education and high enrollment/survival rates. Teacher training has modernized, though shortages persist, especially in remote areas. The curriculum includes specialization in natural and social sciences and language/literature, with distinct Islamic school types (pesantren, madrasah, sekolah islam). Higher education is structured under the KKNI framework with career paths from Technician to Expert, including Sarjana (Bachelor), Magister (Master), and Doktor (Doctorate) levels.

Sweden’s upper secondary education requires 2500 course points, including universal Level 1 subjects and a mandatory diploma project. Admission is based on elementary grades, with rising non-qualifiers (15.6% in 2018) among boys and foreign-origin students. Higher education follows the Bologna Process with structured bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, using ECTS credits. Grading is criteria-referenced and convertible across Europe. Free education is available for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens. Vocational education exists outside Bologna, and public opinion remains divided over private school profit motives.

Egypt’s education system, historically a major intellectual hub, evolved through colonial neglect and post-independence universalization of free public education (ages 4–14). Despite high net enrollment and low dropout rates, quality remains poor—reflecting outdated methods, high student-to-teacher ratios, and reliance on rote learning. A significant wealth gap persists, and private tutoring accounts for up to 61% of educational spending. Reforms aim to improve governance and curriculum relevance, with a Japan-inspired engineering diploma launching in 2025. The Al-Azhar system provides religious education, and dual education models support technical training.

Hong Kong’s education evolved from missionary-led bilingual programs to a structured, English- and Western-knowledge-focused system. Compulsory education runs from age 6 to 18, with public schools free and private/international options offering GCSEs, A-levels, or IBDP. The HKDSE exam determines university access, and the system has transitioned from a two-tiered to a streamlined three-year senior secondary structure. Most schools are Chinese-medium, with 20–30% English-medium, often elite. Class sizes have decreased due to low birth rates, but discipline remains strict. Higher education is led by eight UGC-funded universities (e.g., HKU, CUHK), with global rankings and increasing international student enrollment. International schools, though unregulated and costly, offer diverse curricula.

Sri Lanka’s system, rooted in Buddhist temples and colonial reforms, is administratively fragmented with three ministries. Free and universal education has expanded, but disparities persist between national, provincial, and pirivena schools. The G.C.E. O/L and A/L exams are key for university access. Undergraduate admission is highly competitive, with only ~16% of qualified students admitted, and low graduation rates. Private universities are limited due to public protests and concerns over quality. Vocational training is managed through NVQSL levels and technical colleges, with non-formal programs supporting adult learners.

Ghana’s decentralized system, supported by donor funding (now 2.5%), allocates 23% of the national budget to education. Teacher training is provided through 46 Colleges of Education, with access via distance learning in rural areas. Adult literacy improved from 54% (1997) to 71.5% (2010). Technical and vocational education (TVET) remains underdeveloped, with poor quality and low completion rates. Higher education access is unequal, with acceptance rates dropping from 96% (2001) to 52% (2011), and rural and female students underrepresented.

Israel features a three-tier system with state-secular, state-religious, and Haredi/Arab tracks. Education is highly valued, with 46% of Israeli Jews holding post-secondary degrees, supporting a high-tech economy. The Bagrut exams determine access to universities, military, and jobs. Despite high overall attainment, disparities persist—especially in Arab and Druze communities—due to underfunding, access barriers, and curriculum bias. Arab students face significant challenges, with over 5,000 relocating to Jordan. Israeli universities (e.g., Tel Aviv, Hebrew, Technion) rank globally in science and engineering. Teacher wages are low, and strikes over pay and class size disrupt learning.

England ranks 13th globally in literacy, math, and science, with 88% of schools rated good or outstanding since 2018. The academy system has improved outcomes, with increasing funding and £780 million allocated for Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND).

The Republic of Ireland offers free upper second-level education since 1967, with a Junior and Senior Cycle leading to the Leaving Certificate. English is the primary language, though Irish is taught in Gaelscoileanna. The system follows EHEA/NFQ frameworks, with strong performance in OECD assessments. DEIS and NCSE support disadvantaged and special needs students. Schools operate on a three-term system, with DEIS expanding to 1,200 schools by 2022.

France has a centralized, standardized system with mandatory schooling from age 3 to 18, culminating in the *baccalauréat* or vocational qualifications. Private schools follow national or religious curricula. Higher education includes public universities, IUTs, and elite *grandes écoles*, with rigorous CPGE preparatory programs for STEM and business fields. Admission to *grandes écoles* is highly competitive, and *colles* (oral exams) are a defining feature. Tuition is low for EU/EEA students, with higher fees for international applicants. Religious instruction is banned except in Alsace-Moselle, and the 2004 laïcité law prohibits conspicuous religious symbols.

Nigeria’s Universal Basic Education (UBE) system provides free, compulsory 9-year schooling, with a 6-3-3-4 secondary structure. Students take WAEC/NECO exams for university entry. Tertiary education includes 153 institutions (40 federal, 45 state, 68 private), with direct entry via IJMB and JUPEB. The system faces challenges in teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and poor labor market alignment. Female education remains constrained by gender norms, poverty, and insecurity. The national literacy rate rose to 69% by 2022, with digital literacy targeted at 95% by 2030.

Turkey reorganized education in 1933 under a secular, centralized system, with compulsory education extended to 12 years (2012). The curriculum includes Turkish, math, science, and foreign languages (especially English), with religious education introduced in primary school. The 2012 "4+4+4" reform standardized primary and secondary education. Vocational training is extensive, supported by apprenticeships. The 2017 curriculum introduced "jihad" in Islamic law and removed evolution, sparking controversy. Public universities offer low-cost education, while private institutions are expensive. Turkey is active in EU programs and has seen rising international enrollment.

Taiwan features a highly structured, disciplined system with compulsory education from primary to junior high (since 1968), and a planned 12-year system by 2014. National exams determine high school and university access. Tertiary enrollment exceeds 95%, with top institutions like NTU and NTCH offering rigorous programs. Engineering is a major field, and private universities (e.g., Fu Jen) offer specialized programs. Cram schools are widespread, and the 3+3 high school system allows students to skip the CAP test and attend private schools with reduced academic pressure. Special education is well-established, and teacher training includes internships and digital platforms for professional development.

Iran has a well-developed, highly centralized higher education system, with engineering and science leading in graduation rates and over 70% of university students being women by 2015. Despite ideological restrictions, women’s access to education has expanded significantly since 1979. The system produces a high number of STEM graduates and ranks globally in engineering. Specialized schools (NODET, "Nemoone Dolati") and international programs (e.g., IB) are available. Education spending is substantial—20% of government funds and 5% of GDP—allocated with 50% to secondary and 21% to tertiary education. A GPA-based grading system is used, and reforms emphasize scientific, cultural, and Islamic values. The Ministry of Education guides curriculum development through the Organization for Educational Research and Planning (OERP). Literacy and enrollment have steadily increased, though a teacher shortage (60,000 missing) and lack of school counselors (30,000 short) persist, especially in rural areas. Internet access and distance learning (via Payame Noor University) are widely available. Private schools are expanding rapidly, contributing to growing inequality. Mandatory Islamic practices and study of General Soleimani’s will are enforced in schools and universities. A 2024 law criminalizes protests against educational laws deemed unconstitutional without public referendum.
