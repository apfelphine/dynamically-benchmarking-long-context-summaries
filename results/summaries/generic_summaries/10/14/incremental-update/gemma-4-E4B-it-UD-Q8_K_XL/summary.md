## Global Education Systems Comparison

**System Structures & Regulations:**
Global educational models are highly diverse, featuring centralized, secular systems (e.g., **France**) alongside standardized approaches (**Ireland**, **England**). Vocational pathways exist in countries like **Ghana** and **Norway**, while others exhibit unique structures such as **Israel**'s four-track K-12 (*Bagrut*), **Sri Lanka**'s reliance on private tutoring, and **Hong Kong**'s focus on AI and patriotism. Centralized models in **Egypt** have lower-quality religious tracks (Al-Azhar). **Sweden** balances comprehensive education (preschool to doctorate) with equity issues stemming from private schools (*Friskolor*). **Indonesia** mandates twelve years across differentiated tracks, and **Armenia** guarantees free K-12 and 100% literacy.

**Regional System Deep Dives:**

*   **Turkey:** Implements a centralized "4+4+4" structure with general and vocational options, shaped by Atatürk's nationalism. While public university costs are low and technical fields benefit from TÜBİTAK recognition, the system faces criticism regarding frequent structural changes, curriculum bias, and reliance on private textbooks, though foreign language instruction is common.
*   **Nigeria:** Utilizes a 12-year system concluding with the SSCE, encompassing tertiary, vocational, informal, and secondary levels. Tertiary education is largely government-run, with the National Board for Technical Education working to mitigate unemployment despite infrastructure limitations.
*   **Taiwan:** Mandates education from nursery through university, formalizing a 12-year program by 2014. The culture stresses discipline while banning corporal punishment. Education is segmented into Elementary (Grades 1-6, English starts in Grade 3), Junior High (Grades 7-9, specialized sciences/exams), and Senior High (Grades 10-12, offering national or vocational tracks), with over 95% historically attending university. Higher education is supported by the Ministry of Education, which established the Teaching Excellence Universities Award in 2006; medical is six years, and law is four years undergraduate. Private cram schools supplement the system, and centralized special education supports students aged 2 to 22.
*   **Iran:** Education is centrally managed, evolving from Persian traditions, and has undergone reforms such as Islamization of textbooks, gender segregation, and the introduction of credit/vocational tracks. The structure includes Pre-primary, six years of Elementary, and three years of Lower-Secondary, with optional Upper-Secondary. Tertiary education ranges from Associate to Doctoral degrees. The system receives substantial government funding (20% of spending, 5% of GDP) and aims for knowledge growth, shifting the teacher's role to a facilitator. Key aspects include Persian as the national language, secondary requirements including Arabic and one foreign language (often requiring private tutoring for English quality), and university admission via the national Konkour exam. **Recent Iranian developments include a 2024 law prohibiting protests at educational sites and a 2023 mandate for Islamic prayer time for schoolchildren.**
    *   **Iranian Higher Education Focus:** Iranian higher education shows strong growth in Engineering and Science, alongside rapidly developing social sciences, and exhibits significant interest in entrepreneurship. Women are highly represented, constituting over 70% of university students in 2015, showing high enrollment and graduation rates. The system includes specialized institutions like NODET for gifted children and is overseen by bodies like OERP. Overall, the country maintains high literacy rates, a large public student population, and notable gender parity in university attendance.
