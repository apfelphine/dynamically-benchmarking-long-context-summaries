# Education Systems Overview: Comprehensive International Summary

This summary integrates detailed insights on education systems across multiple countries, capturing historical developments, structure, curricula, vocational and higher education, inclusion policies, language and religion influences, technological advancements, reform initiatives, and contemporary challenges within each nation’s socio-political context.

---

## Armenia
- Universal literacy since 1960; education provided in Armenian and foreign languages.
- Preschool from age 2; compulsory 12-year free K-12 education culminating in state exams.
- Public schools face funding and attendance challenges linked to labor demands and quality concerns.
- Higher education is multilingual, internationally engaged, with dedicated technology centers.

## Norway
- Tradition of folkeskole; compulsory schooling until age 16.
- Tracks include academic and vocational with apprenticeships; early learning emphasizing play.
- Formal grading begins in lower secondary; integrated special education since the 1970s.
- Higher education influenced by Danish models, regulated admissions.

## Indonesia
- Extensive system (~50 million students); compulsory primary through high school.
- Free primary and middle school; nominal high school fees.
- Enrollment rates: primary 93.5%, middle 78.8%, high 60.7%, tertiary 36.3%.
- Rural teacher shortages addressed by improved training.
- Islamic education integrated; higher education aligns with National Qualification Framework.

## Sweden
- Nine years compulsory schooling since 1962; preschool from age 1.
- National tests and grading from Grade 6; support for Sámi, disabled, adults.
- Voluntary upper secondary offers multiple programs.
- Tuition-free higher education for Swedish/EU citizens since 2011.
- High-quality early childhood education; PISA results influenced by socioeconomic and immigration factors.

## Egypt
- Historic al-Azhar center (~970 CE); medieval madrasas limited female access.
- 19th-century reforms increased elitism; Nasser introduced free public education.
- 1990s reforms expanded access; COVID-19 accelerated distance learning.
- Centralized system for basic, secondary, vocational, and higher education (~30% enrollment).
- Parallel Al-Azhar Islamic system with over 8,000 institutions.
- Challenges include underqualified teachers, corporal punishment, private tutoring inequities, and youth unemployment linked to vocational gaps.

## Hong Kong
- Transition from village-based to bilingual Chinese-Western models.
- Post-1997 active learning reforms; post-2020 patriotism focus amid National Security Law.
- Kindergarten through secondary education with HKDSE exams.
- Majority teach in Cantonese; prestigious English-medium and international schools.
- Eight top universities; four-year undergraduate degrees.
- Integrated special education except severe intellectual disabilities.
- Diverse governance; international student support.
- Critiques include rote learning, workload, strict discipline.

## Sri Lanka
- Multiple ministries cause inefficiencies.
- 2,300-year Buddhist temple education heritage with colonial reforms.
- Free post-independence education emphasizing language and religion.
- Diverse schools (Buddhist, Hindu, Catholic, Muslim, secular).
- National exams; compulsory until Grade 9.
- Widespread private tuition; competitive free higher education.
- Vocational training via technical centers and apprenticeships; relatively rigid system.

## Ghana
- Decentralized governance; education accounts for ~23% of state expenditure.
- Teacher training colleges and universities underpin system.
- Kindergarten to tertiary structure.
- Free compulsory basic education targeting universal literacy and gender parity.
- National exams: BECE, WASSCE.
- Senior High offers academic, technical, vocational tracks; TVET reforms address labor mismatches.
- Expanding private tertiary sector; mandatory National Service.
- Adult literacy focus, especially among rural females; ICT and COVID-19 accelerated digital adoption.

## Israel
- Segmented sectors: state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, Arab, private/integrated.
- Compulsory through 12th grade; growing Haredi and Arab populations; disparities in matriculation pass rates.
- Half of Israeli Jews earn post-secondary degrees; Arab Christians outperform other minorities.
- Nine strong STEM universities.
- Education as social mobility vehicle; challenges include inequality, large classes, low pay.
- Arab sector underfunded; females advantaged in higher education; men dominate STEM fields.
- Teacher reforms face union resistance; coexistence programs.
- Frequent strikes coupled with localized innovation.

## England
- Compulsory schooling from 5 to 18; Foundation Stage through Key Stages 1–4 and post-16 education.
- Diverse provision: state (academies, selective), private, and home-schooling.
- National Curriculum (ages 5–16); Ofsted inspections.
- Mandatory PSHE and Relationships/Sex Education; GCSE exams at 16.
- Post-16 education delivered via sixth forms, colleges, apprenticeships.
- Qualifications include A-levels, BTECs, NVQs, T Levels.
- Apprenticeships integrate work and study.
- Higher education comprises universities/colleges with mostly three-year degrees; tuition capped (~£9,535 in 2024–25).
- Postgraduate qualifications from certificates to doctorates.
- Universities regulated by OfS and QAA; significant international student influx.
- Adult education via access courses, Open University; integrated academic-vocational qualifications.
- UK ranked 13th on PISA 2018; academy schools improved outcomes in deprived areas; ~88% schools rated good/outstanding (2023).
- Increased national funding formula with enhanced SEND support.

## Republic of Ireland
- Compulsory education ages 6–16 or three years of secondary schooling; free upper secondary since 1967.
- Mostly private preschool provision with some state support.
- Primary education mainly Roman Catholic; growing multidenominational schools.
- Nearly all mixed-sex schools (~99.5%).
- Secondary schools span ages 12–19; ~90% complete Leaving Certificate.
- School sectors: voluntary secondary (57%), community colleges (28%), comprehensive (15%), private "grind" schools.
- Irish-medium secondary schools ~3%.
- Curricula emphasize individuality; religious instruction church-administered.
- Junior Cycle, optional Transition Year, Senior Cycle culminating in Leaving Certificate.
- Early school leaving low (12.3%), below EU average.
- Irish students exceed OECD/EU averages in literacy and STEM.
- Ireland ranked among top 20 globally (2022).
- Special needs supported by NCSE with classroom assistants.
- DEIS initiative targets disadvantaged schools.
- €200M infrastructure loan in 2023.
- Popular international student destination with notable universities.
- School year structured with mandatory minimum days; COVID-19 closures in 2020.

## France
### General Education
- Public system shaped by Napoleon and Ferry; free, secular, compulsory ages 6–16 since 2021.
- Laïcité principle central; religious instruction banned except Alsace/Moselle.
- 2004 ban on visible religious symbols in schools.
- Centralized Ministry of National Education.
- Stages: preschool (2–6), primary (6–11), middle (11–15), high (15–18) ending in baccalauréat or vocational certificates.
- State-contracted private schools follow national curricula.
- 100+ international schools regulated by AEFE.
- School year from September to July with multiple breaks.
- Population ~67 million; 18 million enrolled including 2.9 million in higher education.
- Minimal school violence reported.

### Higher Education
- Dual system: public universities and Grandes Écoles (engineering, business, humanities, political science).
- Selective preparatory classes (CPGE) accept ~5% of high school graduates.
- 2021 higher education enrollment was 1.65 million, mostly bachelor’s and master’s students.
- Low tuition fees (€170–380/year EU/EEA), private/business schools up to €40,000.
- Public-private partnerships since 2018 raise privatization concerns.
- Faculty includes part-time industry experts; research linked with CNRS and INSERM.
- Emphasis on specialization, low fees, and elite leadership pathways.

### Teacher Training and Roles
- Primary teachers trained at INSPE with master’s degree; teach ~28 hours/week.
- Secondary teachers require CAPES and master's; agrégés pass Agrégation exam for senior status and pay.
- University teachers hold doctorates; secondary PRAG/PRCE have heavier teaching loads without research.
- CPGE teachers often agrégés or higher ranks.

### Historical Context
- Education expanded from 1800s; girls’ secondary education from 1880 onward.

## Nigeria
- Structure: six years elementary, six years secondary divided into junior and senior phases.
- SSCE exams administered by WAEC and NECO; university admission via JAMB.
- Advanced programs (IJMB, JUPEB) allow second-year university entry.
- Since 2015, 129 English-medium international schools complement national curriculum.
- 99 universities (federal, state, private); decentralized expansion.
- Federal secondary schools low-fee and merit-based; underfunded state schools; better-resourced but costly private/religious schools.
- Vocational education expanded since 1980s but hampered by teacher shortages, outdated curricula, political interference.
- National Technical Certificates offered; apprenticeships continue amid challenges from child labor laws.
- Informal/adult literacy decentralized and underfunded; NGOs play a role.
- Female education hindered by teen pregnancy, early marriage, poverty, militancy; UNICEF cash incentives improve access.
- Rural secondary schools face infrastructure/staff shortages, insecurity, low prioritization.
- Adult literacy rose from 62% (2018) to 69% (2022) with marked regional disparities; northern regions most challenged.
- Government targets 95% digital literacy by 2030, collaborating with UNESCO on vulnerable groups.
- National Bureau of Statistics tracks literacy by gender and state.

## Turkey

### Historical Development and Structure
- Ministry founded 1923; unified secular system established 1924.
- Latin alphabet adopted 1928; cultural-language associations active in 1930s.
- Schools grew from 5,100 in 1923 to 58,800 in 2001.
- Compulsory education extended to 12 years by 2012.
- 2017 curriculum removed evolution theory, added Islamic religious elements.

### Education System
- Optional pre-primary (ages 3–6).
- Compulsory primary (8 years; 6–14 years).
- Secondary minimum 3 years; general, vocational, technical.
- "4+4+4" system splits schooling into 4 years primary, 4 years middle (general or Imam Hatip religious), 4 years secondary.
- Senior high tracks include Turkish-Math, Science, Social Sciences, Foreign Languages; vocational track untracked.
- Core subjects include republican history; diplomas require exams; university admissions linked to tracks.
- International Baccalaureate offered in 53 schools.

### Vocational Education
- Various vocational schools and 2–4 year apprenticeships; proficiency exams required under Law no 3308.

### Higher Education
- 167 universities (public and private); over 1 million students; 373,000 graduates as of 2006.
- Programs range 2–4 years undergraduate and 2+ years graduate; English-language prep common.
- Accreditation bodies like ABET oversee quality; Higher Education Council monitors universities, appoints rectors.
- Active Erasmus and Socrates exchange; ~800,000 international students (2016).
- Türkiye Scholarships aim to attract 500,000 foreign students.
- Foreign applicants admitted via YÖS or international credentials.

### Research
- TÜBİTAK and TÜBA coordinate research in agriculture, health, biotech, nuclear energy, IT, defense.

### Private and Minority Schools
- Private schools include Turkish, minority (Greek, Armenian, Jewish), foreign (French, German), and international variants.
- Dershane study centers converted to academic high schools by 2015.
- Minority languages protected by Treaty of Lausanne; Hebrew preserved.
- Decline in French-language education; many Jewish students shifted to Turkish or private schools.

### Religious Education and Curriculum
- Religious education banned post-1923; reintroduced gradually from 1980.
- Mandatory "Culture of Religion and Knowledge of Ethics" from Grade 4 covers Islam and other religions.
- Creationism introduced in 1985 (under U.S. influence); evolution theory removed; criticized for weak scientific basis.
- Curriculum emphasizes Turkish nationalism based on Atatürk; portrays Armenians negatively and denies the Armenian genocide.
- Mandatory displays of Atatürk portraits and symbols.

### Criticism and Challenges
- Frequent reforms and exam changes create controversy.
- Reports of decreased scientific content and increased religious influence.
- State textbooks often low quality; private books widely used despite prohibitions.

### Education for Religious Minorities
- Treaty of Lausanne guarantees minority education rights.
- Post-Republic religious classes banned.
- Many Jewish schools closed or struggled financially by 1928.
- Remaining Jewish schools aligned curricula with national standards.

## Taiwan

### Historical Context and Structure
- Influenced by Dutch, Ming loyalists, Qing, Japanese; Christianity introduced by Dutch colonizers.
- Post-1945 ROC governed school revitalization.
- Public system covers nursery to university; compulsory primary to junior high since 1968; full 12-year compulsory schooling by 2014.
- Progression controlled by national exams.
- Strict discipline, uniforms, cleaning schedules; corporal punishment banned officially but persists.
- Two-semester school year with Lunar New Year breaks; Mandarin as instruction medium.

### Educational Structure
- Kindergarten (3–6 years).
- Elementary (Grades 1–6; ages 6–11).
- Junior High (Grades 7–9; ages 12–14).
- Senior High (Grades 10–12; ages 15–17), national since 2019.
- Postsecondary includes junior colleges (associate degrees) and universities (bachelor, master, doctorate).

### Curriculum and Progression
- National Basic Education fully implemented by 2014.
- Core subjects: Mandarin, math, science, English (starting grade 3), native languages, social studies, arts.
- Junior High adds advanced sciences, technology, civics, home economics, PE, scouting.
- Students assigned to schools by residence; entrance exams determine senior high or vocational placement.
- Private schools popular alternatives.

### Senior High and Vocational Education
- ~95% advance to senior or vocational high school.
- Senior High: university entrance focus; science or liberal arts tracks; mandatory military education.
- Vocational senior high offers 3-year technical programs; graduates enter workforce or further study.

### Higher Education
- Over 95% of high school graduates enter universities, creating degree inflation and graduate unemployment.
- Prestigious public research universities emphasize science, engineering, and arts; lower tuition and government-supported.
- Private universities focus on humanities, law, business; attract significant international students.
- University life less pressured; many pursue graduate studies abroad.
- Some institutions date to Japanese colonial era; others reestablished post-Chinese Civil War.

### Nursing and Language Education
- Nursing education offers three entry paths with national licensing exam.
- Taiwan popular for Chinese language learning; ~40,000 mainland Chinese students (2015).

### Special Education and Private Supplementary Education
- Special education implemented since 1984; supports disabilities and giftedness through special classes and itinerant programs (ages 2–22).
- Private “cram schools” provide after-school tutoring focused on core subjects.

### Preschool and Kindergarten
- Public and private preschools/kindergartens.
- English immersion increasing despite legal debates.
- Government aims to increase kindergarten enrollment by 2020.
- "3+3" high school system allows bypassing junior high exit exams via private education.

### Teacher Training and Graduate Programs
- Teacher preparation requires four-year degrees, internships, national certification exams; supported by digital platforms.
- Doctoral studies last 2–7 years, including international programs.
- Medical education is six-year undergraduate plus clinical training; law studied as a four-year undergraduate major without separate law schools.
- Annual Teaching Excellence Awards since 2006.

## Iran

### Historical Overview
- Education tradition from ancient times (~550 BC), with Academy of Gondishapur (~3rd century).
- Safavid era blended Islamic values with modern sciences.
- Late 19th century Qajar reforms established schools, libraries, and publishing.
- Literacy Corps (1969–1979) improved literacy during White Revolution.
- Post-1979 Islamic Revolution Islamized education with segregation, mandatory observances, revised curricula.

### Education System and Structure
- Centralized Ministry runs public and private schools.
- Western-style schools/universities introduced mid-19th century.
- Cultural Revolution (1980) enforced Islamic education values.
- Approximately 4 million children dropped out before K-12 completion (2014).
- Pre-primary optional (ages 5–6).
- Elementary (6 years, ages 6–12), compulsory.
- Lower-secondary (3 years, ages 12–15), compulsory.
- Upper-secondary (3 years, 15–18), not compulsory; theoretical, technical-vocational, manual tracks.
- Postsecondary includes 2-year technical/vocational associate degrees, undergraduate, and graduate programs.
- Prestigious universities include University of Tehran and Sharif University, with ~60,000 PhD students (2013).

### Grading and Examinations
- Scores 0–20, passing GPA >10.
- Grades range A (16–20) to F (0–9.99).
- University admission via national Konkour exam.

### Budget, Policies, and Reforms
- Education consumes ~20% of government expenditure and 5% of GDP; 50% on secondary and 21% on tertiary education.
- Since 2023, daily Islamic prayers mandatory in schools; university students study General Soleimani's will.
- Protests in education banned without referendum.
- Post-pandemic expansion of hybrid learning with government grants.
- Reforms (2005–2010, 2012) target improved quality, research culture, reduced rote learning, global and just education, and family involvement.

### Privatization and Challenges
- Increasing privatization and urban cost increases; private schools much costlier than public.
- Supreme Leader criticized public schools in 2023.
- Teacher shortage (~60,000 teachers, ~30,000 counselors), notably in poorer provinces.
- Centralized teacher education at Farhangian University with national entrance exams.

### Language Instruction
- Persian official language.
- Arabic mandatory grades 7–12.
- English most popular foreign language; French introduced in 2022 to reduce English dominance.
- English teaching banned in primary schools since 2018.
- Over 5,000 foreign language schools nationwide.

### Technology and Distance Education
- Growing internet access and government targets for online services.
- Schools equipped with computers and intranet.
- Payame Noor University provides distance education.

### Higher Education
- ~4.5 million enrolled in 2013; ~750,000 graduates annually.
- 16 universities closed post-1979, later reopened with Islamic curricula; many professors dismissed for ideological reasons.
- Leading universities include University of Tehran, Tarbiat Modares (postgraduate only), Sharif University of Technology.
- Islamic Azad University offers high-fee private alternatives; combined enrollment over 3 million university students, including medical students.

### Additional Highlights in Iran’s Education
- **Higher Education Excellence:** World-leading in engineering, construction, and science graduation rates; social sciences growing despite ideological constraints. Students labeled “starred” face unofficial restrictions.
- **Entrepreneurship Growth:** Increasing interest with dedicated university courses (e.g., Tehran, Sharif) and the KARAD Plan fostering entrepreneurship at 12 universities.
- **Women’s Education:** Women make up over 70% of university population (2015), representing a significant post-revolution advancement. Iran leads globally in female-to-male primary enrollment ratio (1.22:1).
- **Gifted Education:** NODET (SAMPAD) runs competitive schools for gifted students; government also controls selective "Nemoone Dolati" schools.
- **Educational Research:** Organization for Educational Research and Planning (OERP) oversees curriculum development, research, textbook publishing, and standards.
- **Notable High Schools:** NODET schools (Allameh Helli, Farzanegan), Tehran’s Iranzamin, and several offering International Baccalaureate programs.
- **Statistics:** Iran ranks 3rd worldwide in engineering graduates (70% female), and 5th in STEM graduates (335,000 in 2016). Literacy rate near 85%, with significant youth literacy gains, especially among girls.
