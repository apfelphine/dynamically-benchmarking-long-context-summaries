# Education in Armenia
Armenia boasts a 100% literacy rate since 1960, with education historically influenced by Ottoman, Persian, and Soviet policies. Today, primary and secondary education are free and compulsory, with a focus on Armenian language and culture. Russian remains widely taught, and English learning is growing rapidly. The government established Armenia's first Chinese language school in 2018 to strengthen ties and regional language proficiency.

The K-12 system includes preschool (nursery and kindergarten), compulsory 12-year secondary education divided into primary, middle, and high school, and state exams critical for university entrance. Public schools dominate, funded by the government with a national curriculum emphasizing holistic education, while private schools offer specialized programs but often result in access inequalities. Higher education encompasses numerous state and private institutions, including Yerevan State University and medical universities, participating actively in European educational frameworks like the Bologna Process. Challenges like rising student absenteeism linked to child labor persist. Technology-based centers such as the Tumo Center provide modern skills development.

# Education in Norway
Education in Norway is organized into compulsory elementary school (ages 6–12), lower secondary school (ages 13–16), and upper secondary school (ages 16–19). The folkeskole evolved into the grunnskole system, with reforms focusing on inclusive education and flexible learning paths. While most secondary education is publicly funded, private schooling exists under strict regulations. Norway emphasizes foreign language learning and has modernized curricula to include IT.

Higher education is accessible mainly via universities and university colleges, requiring a general university admissions certificate. The Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance oversees educational quality. Vocational education follows a "2+2" apprenticeship model. Special education integration is prioritized, tailored to individual needs. The academic year runs August to June with breaks including summer and national holidays. Grading varies by level, with recent adoption of the Bologna system in higher education.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia's education reflects a history from Islamic boarding schools (pesantren) and Dutch colonial stratified schooling. Today, children begin early education with PAUD before progressing through 12 years of compulsory education, divided into primary, junior high, and senior high school. Public schools predominate but religious schools, especially Islamic madrasahs and pesantrens, play a significant role. Challenges include uneven quality, teacher shortages, and regional disparities.

The national curriculum includes specialization streams and religious education is integrated differently across school types. Higher education institutions are varied, from universities to polytechnics, aligned with the Indonesian National Qualification Framework. Vocational education and private schooling are expanding, while public education continues to face quality and equity challenges.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden offers a comprehensive nine-year compulsory education from ages 6 to 16, followed by elective three-year upper secondary education (gymnasieskola) divided into preparatory and vocational programs. Preschool education is widespread and subsidized. The national curriculum mandates a broad range of subjects including languages, sciences, and social studies, with grading introduced from grade 6.

Upper secondary students choose among 18 national programs, including the International Baccalaureate. Admission is merit-based, with diploma projects required. Higher education follows the Bologna Process with universities and colleges offering bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. Independent (free) schools receive public funding but cannot discriminate and are subject to debate regarding quality and profit models. Sweden participates actively in international assessments, showing average OECD performance with ongoing efforts to improve.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt's education history dates to ancient times with continuities in Islamic and modern reforms. The current system comprises compulsory basic education (ages 4–14), secondary education with general and technical tracks, and extensive higher education including public and private universities. The Al-Azhar system runs parallel focused on Islamic education.

Despite expanded access and reforms, challenges remain in quality and equity, with reliance on rote learning and private tutoring. Technical and vocational education is promoted through various initiatives. Higher education faces issues with resource constraints and growth pressures. International curricula and schools add diversity. Recent reforms and pandemic-induced distance learning initiatives are shaping the future.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education evolved from early missionary schools to a modern bilingual system with both Chinese and English instruction. Compulsory education runs from age 6 to 15/16 with structured primary and secondary stages culminating in the HKDSE examination. Schools include government, aided, private, direct subsidy scheme, caput, and international schools, with a significant emphasis on English medium instruction, especially in elite EMI schools.

Higher education features eight publicly funded universities, several self-financing institutions and a vibrant international education sector. The system faces challenges with recent political changes, teacher retention, and curriculum reforms emphasizing patriotism and national security. Adult education and special education integration gain importance. International schools cater to diverse populations with various curricula including IB, British, and American systems.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education system, administered by multiple government ministries, features free and compulsory education spanning primary to tertiary levels. Roots trace back over 2,300 years with Buddhist monastic education coexisting with colonial missionary and secular schools. Post-independence efforts expanded access and introduced national languages as mediums of instruction.

The school structure includes primary (grades 1–5), junior and senior secondary (grades 6–11), collegiate (grades 12–13), and universities. National exams like the GCE Ordinary and Advanced Levels determine progression. Private, international, and religious schools coexist. Teacher training occurs in public colleges and universities. Vocational education is managed by a specialized commission. Private tutoring is widespread. Higher education is competitive with limited university access, leading many students to study abroad or vocational fields.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education evolved from informal, oral traditions and mission schools to a formal system established under British colonial rule. Post-independence reforms focused on universal basic education and literacy campaigns. The system includes basic education (kindergarten to junior high), senior high school, and tertiary institutions, with curricula taught primarily in English.

Recent decades have seen expansion in enrollment, especially in basic and secondary education, although vocational training is underdeveloped and informal training dominates. Governance involves central and local authorities with donor support. Teacher training is centralized, with ongoing efforts in ICT integration. Disparities exist in access to tertiary education, with higher enrollment among males and wealthier populations. Private education plays a significant role alongside public provision.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system comprises primary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), and high school (10–12) with compulsory education spanning kindergarten to grade 12. Schools are divided into state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors, each with distinct curricula and languages of instruction. The Arab sector faces disparities in resources and achievement.

Higher education is accessible through matriculation exams (bagrut) and the Psychometric Entrance Test, with many universities ranked globally in science and technology. Education is culturally valued and integral to economic development, especially in the high-tech sector. Challenges include inequalities, teacher strikes, and political-educational tensions. Gender disparities exist, with women outperforming men in matriculation and higher education enrollment.

# Education in England
England's education system features compulsory schooling from ages 5 to 18, with early years, primary, secondary, and post-16 education stages aligned to the National Curriculum. Schools include state-funded academies, community, free, foundation, voluntary aided/controlled, and independent (private) schools. Education is overseen by Ofsted with standardized testing at key stages.

Post-16 education includes sixth forms, further education colleges, apprenticeships, and T Levels. Higher education offers a broad range of vocational and academic qualifications, with tuition fees introduced in 1998 and capped since 2012. Adult education and lifelong learning opportunities exist. England participates actively in international assessments, maintaining high literacy and science rankings.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland mandates education from ages 6 to 16 or completion of three years of second-level education. The system includes early childhood, primary, and secondary education, with a focus on national language (Irish) promotion. Primary and secondary schooling features primarily church-run schools, though multi-denominational schools are growing.

Secondary education encompasses the Junior Cycle, optional Transition Year, and Senior Cycle culminating in the Leaving Certificate examination. Higher education is well-regarded internationally, with growing diversity and support for special educational needs. Programs address educational disadvantage through schemes like DEIS. The academic year includes standard holidays, with COVID-19 impacting schooling.

# Education in France
France's education system is centralized under the Ministry of National Education, offering free, secular, and compulsory education since the late 19th century. The structure includes preschool (maternelle), primary (école élémentaire), middle (collège), and high school (lycée) stages, culminating in the baccalauréat exam.

Higher education comprises public universities, elite grandes écoles, university technical institutes (IUTs), and private institutions regulated under various frameworks. The French system follows the Bologna Process with Licence, Master, and Doctorate degrees. Private schools operate under state contracts or independently with religious instruction. Preparatory classes serve as gateways to grandes écoles. Funding is primarily state-based with nominal fees. France emphasizes secular education and prohibits conspicuous religious symbols in schools.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education includes six years of primary, three years each of junior and senior secondary education, followed by tertiary education. The Universal Basic Education policy makes the first nine years compulsory and free. Secondary education is divided among federal, state, private, and religious schools, with a variety of curricula and teacher qualifications.

Examinations like the West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE) determine progression. Vocational education is overseen by national bodies but remains challenged by funding and infrastructure gaps. Informal education and literacy initiatives supplement formal education. Female education faces cultural and socio-political barriers, addressed by targeted programs. International schools and advanced level (A-level) programs like IJMB and JUPEB offer pathways to university.

# Education in Turkey
Turkey’s education system underwent major reforms after the Republic’s founding, emphasizing secularism and centralized control. Compulsory education expanded from 5 to 12 years, structured as 4+4+4 years covering primary to secondary levels, including general and vocational tracks.

Pre-primary is optional. Primary education lasts eight years featuring core subjects and foreign languages. Secondary education offers general, vocational, and technical schools, with tracks and electives from grade 10. Higher education includes universities, vocational schools, and conservatories, with a growing number of private and foundation universities. Religious education was removed and later reintroduced with Islamic content.

Foreign language instruction focuses on English, with recent additions of Kurdish and Arabic electives. The system promotes Turkish nationalism and secularism but faces criticism over frequent reforms, textbook bias (e.g., Armenian genocide denial), and a need for better teacher quality. Turkey is an active participant in international education programs and hosts many foreign students.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan’s education system includes compulsory 12-year education from elementary to junior high, followed by senior high or vocational education, and higher education with universities and junior colleges. The academic year runs from September to June, with a focus on Mandarin instruction and inclusion of native languages. Education involves rigorous entrance exams at multiple stages.

Higher education offers associate to doctoral degrees across public and private institutions, with a popular emphasis on engineering. Private supplemental cram schools are widespread due to academic pressure. Special education services, adult education, and international programs are available. Private kindergartens with English immersion have grown rapidly. Distance learning and teacher in-service education are supported by government initiatives.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s educational heritage dates to ancient Persia, evolving through Islamic and modern reforms. The system is highly centralized under the Ministry of Education, mixing secular and Islamic content, with compulsory education until grade 9 and optional upper-secondary education. The competitive national university entrance exam (Konkur) governs higher education access.

Primary to secondary education integrates religious studies; higher education features prominent universities with significant female enrollment, especially in STEM fields. The country spends about 5% of GDP on education. Challenges include teacher shortages, privatization of schooling, and politically influenced curricula, including Armenian genocide denial and nationalism. Recent reforms promote research, critical thinking, and entrepreneurship, while hybrid learning has expanded post-pandemic. Iran maintains special schools for gifted students and offers International Baccalaureate programs at select schools.
