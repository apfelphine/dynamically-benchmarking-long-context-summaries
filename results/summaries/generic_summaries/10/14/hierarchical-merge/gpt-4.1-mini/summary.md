# Education Systems Overview and Key National Profiles

## General Global Trends

Many countries provide compulsory education typically spanning 9 to 12 years, combining academic and vocational tracks to address diverse learner needs. Systems emphasize inclusivity through special education and support for disadvantaged groups, while higher education commonly aligns with frameworks like the Bologna Process to ensure quality and comparability. Challenges recurrently include regional disparities, resource constraints, teacher shortages, and equity concerns especially regarding access for marginalized populations. Private tutoring and supplementary education remain widespread in several nations, reflecting gaps in formal schooling.

## Europe and Neighboring Regions

**France** operates a centralized secular education system starting at age 3, culminating in the baccalauréat or vocational certifications. Public universities provide accessible higher education under the Licence-Master-Doctorat structure, complemented by selective Grandes écoles that train elite cohorts through competitive examinations. Teacher recruitment is highly competitive and stratified across education levels, ensuring quality. Research is nationally coordinated, balancing accessibility with historic elite preparation.

**England and Ireland** maintain strong state-funded systems with national curricula and diverse qualification pathways including vocational options. England mandates education until 18, supports special needs students via targeted funding, and benefits from city academies improving outcomes. Ireland offers eight years of primary education mostly through Catholic National Schools, followed by rigorous secondary cycles and significant investment in infrastructure and inclusion, achieving high international rankings.

**Armenia, Norway, Sweden, and Egypt** illustrate varied regional approaches: Armenia combines free compulsory education with growing English and Chinese instruction amid dropout issues; Norway emphasizes inclusivity with comprehensive academic and vocational tracks and highly qualified teachers; Sweden supports subsidized preschool and diverse upper secondary admission but faces equity and safety challenges; Egypt expands STEM-oriented basic education but battles quality, inequality, and heavy reliance on private tutoring, with reforms inspired by international vocational models.

## Middle East, Turkey, and Iran

**Israel** features diverse education sectors (state-secular, religious, Arab) with compulsory education through grade 12 and gatekeeping Bagrut exams. Tertiary attainment is high, especially in STEM, supported by affirmative action despite disparities. Issues include large classes, low pay, and content bias, with active government and nonprofit inclusion programs.

**Turkey** runs a modern secular 12-year compulsory system combining general, vocational, and religious schools. Secondary graduation relies on national exams amid debates over curriculum changes and religious content. Higher education expanded markedly with over 160 universities, balancing low public fees and private institution growth. Religious education is constitutionally mandated alongside secular subjects. English is the main foreign language, and minority schools comply with national standards despite past challenges.

**Iran** mandates nine years of compulsory education with optional upper secondary tracks and nearly universal primary enrollment. It allocates substantial resources to education, witnessing high female university participation, especially in STEM fields. Competitive exams shape higher education admissions; public universities are tuition-free, while private institutions manage alternative access routes. The system features gender-segregated schooling aligned with Islamic values, expanding distance learning, and faces teacher shortages and regional disparities.

## Asia and Africa

**Taiwan** provides 12 years of compulsory education primarily in Mandarin with academic and vocational options, achieving over 95% university entrance from senior high. It hosts diverse higher education programs and extensive private supplementary tutoring (“buxiban”), attracting many international Chinese language learners despite reports of corporal punishment.

**Indonesia** mandates 12 years of education focusing on sciences, social studies, language, and religion, with vocational training embedded in senior secondary school. It struggles with uneven teacher distribution and rural resource gaps. The national qualifications framework governs higher education, which includes support for students with disabilities.

**Hong Kong** requires education from ages 6 to 18, blending Cantonese and English across multiple school types. Vocational and adult education promote inclusivity, while reforms emphasize active learning, patriotism, and national security, influencing hiring of foreign teachers. Higher education growth integrates regional collaboration and AI amid demographic shifts.

**Sri Lanka** combines a long Buddhist educational heritage with compulsory schooling until grade 9 and competitive university entrance exams. A vibrant private tutoring market, including digital platforms, supplements formal education. Limited university capacity drives many students toward overseas or vocational education.

**Ghana** offers nearly 20 years of education with free compulsory basic schooling achieving gender parity and adult literacy improvements. Students choose academic or vocational tracks, though vocational education faces stigma and infrastructure challenges. Non-formal initiatives, scholarships, and ICT integration support expanding tertiary access.

**Nigeria** follows a 6-3-3 system with nine years of free compulsory education under Universal Basic Education. Secondary certification exams determine university eligibility, with alternative foundation programs allowing advanced entry. Tertiary education includes over 150 institutions. Challenges involve teacher shortages, outdated curricula, regional disparities, especially for girls, and high out-of-school rates. Digital literacy targets and NGO programs aim to address gaps.

---

This synthesis highlights the diversity and commonalities among global education systems, revealing persistent challenges in equity, quality, and resource allocation alongside ongoing efforts to broaden access and modernize curricula across primary, secondary, and tertiary levels.
