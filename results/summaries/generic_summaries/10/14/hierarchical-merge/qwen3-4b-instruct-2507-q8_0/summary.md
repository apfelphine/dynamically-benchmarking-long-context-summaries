# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in Armenia, Norway, Sweden, Hong Kong, Indonesia, Egypt, Israel, England, Ireland, France, Nigeria, Turkey, Taiwan, Iran**

---

## **High-Performing, Structured Systems with Equity and Reform Challenges**

**Armenia, Norway, Sweden, and Hong Kong** maintain high standards of access and equity through state-funded, compulsory education systems. Armenia offers free, 12-year schooling with 100% literacy since 1960, emphasizing national history, language, and culture, though high school completion lags due to absenteeism and child labor. Public schools dominate (97%), with private institutions facing cost barriers. University access is competitive, supported by European integration (e.g., Erasmus+ and Bologna Process). Norway provides a comprehensive, publicly funded system with compulsory education until age 16, enhanced by the 1994 reform and *Kunnskapsløftet* (2006), promoting IT integration and flexible pathways. It ranks well in PISA, though math performance lags Shanghai. Special education is fully integrated. Sweden follows a standardized, age-based system from age 6 to 16, with a national curriculum (updated 2011), over 85% of grade 9 students studying a third language, and a mandatory diploma project. Dropout rates have risen slightly, especially among boys and foreign-origin students. Higher education is free for EU/EEA/Swiss citizens, with ECTS-based grading and a three-cycle structure. Challenges include grade inflation, inequality, and the rise of profit-driven *friskolor*. Hong Kong features a two-tiered, free public system from age 6 to 18, transitioning to the HKDSE in 2012. Most schools operate on a full-day basis, with vocational and commerce streams managed by MDC and VTC. International schools offer GCSE, IBDP, or French IB curricula; the French International School is the only one accredited by the French Ministry. Criticisms include rote learning, heavy academic loads, and a shift toward nationalist content post-1997, leading to declining enrollment in elite schools and brain drain. Despite small class sizes, tertiary education has expanded, with eight universities ranked among the world’s top 100.

## **Systems with Structural Inequities and Performance Gaps**

**Indonesia, Egypt, and Israel** reflect deep structural and cultural divides. Indonesia, with over 50 million students, offers free primary and middle school and a compulsory 12-year structure, though provincial autonomy has weakened Pancasila values. Teacher shortages, especially in rural areas, and poor quality in remote regions persist. Tertiary participation remains low at 36.31%. Egypt provides free basic education (ages 4–14) with high net enrollment and low dropout rates, but quality is poor—marked by rote learning, weak international performance, and widespread private tutoring (over 50% of spending). Disparities between wealthy and disadvantaged students endure. Higher education is centralized and strained by rising enrollment. Israel achieves strong outcomes in science, engineering, and math (46% of Israeli Jews hold post-secondary degrees), ranking second in OECD for tertiary education. However, Arab and Druze students face systemic barriers: underfunding, unequal access, and lower STEM investment, despite a 171% increase in math teachers. Arab students have higher dropout rates (4.5% vs. 1.7%) and lower exam pass rates (51% vs. 62%). Textbooks promote negative Arab stereotypes, and gender gaps persist—men more likely to drop out, women dominate university enrollment. Government initiatives like the 5-Year Plan and Aluma aim to address inequities but fall short of systemic change.

## **Policy-Driven, Culturally Anchored Systems**

**France, England, Ireland, Nigeria, Turkey, and Taiwan** reflect distinct policy frameworks and cultural values. France maintains a centralized, secular system with mandatory schooling from age 3 to 18, culminating in the *baccalauréat*, CAP, or CFA. The *grandes écoles* offer elite access via rigorous *CPGE* preparatory programs, emphasizing oral exams (*colles*) and academic rigor. *Laïcité* bans religious instruction and conspicuous symbols, sparking ongoing debates on religious freedom. Public education is high-quality, with minimal tuition for EU/EEA students, though access to elite institutions remains highly competitive. England’s system is compulsory from age 5 to 18, with diverse school types (academies, free schools, UTCs) and strong international performance in literacy and numeracy. Ofsted inspections and national funding (reaching £7.1 billion by 2023) have improved quality, though disparities in support for students with special educational needs (SEND) persist. Ireland offers free upper second-level education since 1967, with strong performance in reading (PISA 2nd) and math (11th), and a highly regarded Leaving Certificate. Catholic schools remain dominant, though religious diversity is growing. The Transition Year supports experiential learning. Third-level education is among the world’s top 20. Nigeria operates a 12-year system with free primary and secondary education under the UBE policy (9 years), though implementation is uneven. Secondary admission is based on the Common Entrance Exam; university entry requires WAEC/NECO results and JAMB scores. Private and religious schools offer better facilities but face rising fees. Advanced entry programs (IJMB, JUPEB) expand access. Challenges include teacher shortages, outdated curricula, poor infrastructure, and gender disparities due to poverty and early marriage. National literacy is 69% (2024), with significant regional disparities. Turkey features a centralized, secular system under the 1924 Law of Integration, with 12 years of compulsory education (post-2012 reform). It offers general and vocational tracks, with mandatory Imam Hatip schools. University admissions are based on a national final exam. Vocational education is strong, with ABET-accredited technical universities. The system emphasizes Turkish nationalism, Atatürk’s ideology, and secularism, while downplaying the Armenian genocide. English is dominant; minority languages are offered as electives. Taiwan’s system is highly structured and state-led, with a full 12-year compulsory education fully implemented by 2014. Mandarin is primary; English introduced in Grade 3. National exams determine senior high and vocational admissions. High junior high graduation rates (94.7%) and strong vocational tracks support transition to employment or higher education. Private cram schools are widespread but vary in quality. Iran has a highly centralized system, with 6 years of elementary, 3 years of lower-secondary, and 3 years of upper-secondary education. A GPA-based grading system (A: 16–20, F: 0–9.99) requires a GPA above 10 to pass. Higher education excels in engineering and science, with women outnumbering men. Key reforms (2012) emphasize research, critical thinking, and teacher facilitation. Post-pandemic hybrid learning uses LMS platforms. Challenges include a teacher shortage (~60,000), inadequate infrastructure in remote regions, and only 30,000 counselors. Private schools are growing rapidly, with higher costs than public institutions. Foreign language education includes Arabic (grades 7–12), English (added in 2022), and over 5,000 schools. Internet access is expanding, but ideological restrictions and a 2024 law banning protests against education policies remain concerns.

## **Common Themes and Observations**

Across all systems, education reflects deep historical, cultural, and political influences. **Academic rigor and state oversight** are prevalent, particularly in Hong Kong, Taiwan, and France, though often at the cost of student well-being and critical thinking. **Rote learning, excessive workloads, and exam-driven culture** remain persistent, especially in systems like Hong Kong, Egypt, and Iran. **Structural inequities**—based on gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, or regional access—are widespread, with Arab students in Israel, rural students in Nigeria, and minority groups in Iran facing systemic disadvantages. **Teacher quality, infrastructure, and curriculum relevance** to labor markets remain major challenges. While **Ghana** (noted in Summary 1) has made gains in access and gender parity through FCUBE, its system still struggles with teacher training and vocational education. **Israel** stands out for strong outcomes in science and innovation, yet deep inequities persist. **England and Ireland** excel in equity and access, with strong international performance in literacy and numeracy. **France and Taiwan** demonstrate how cultural identity and state control can shape education, though both face tensions between tradition and modernization. Overall, all systems are undergoing reforms toward **modernization, equity, and holistic development**, but progress is uneven. The tension between **academic excellence and student-centered learning** continues to define policy debates, especially in systems emphasizing discipline and national identity.
