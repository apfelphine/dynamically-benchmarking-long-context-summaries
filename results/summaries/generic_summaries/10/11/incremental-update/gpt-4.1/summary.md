## Summary

### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka offers free, centralized education from Grades 1 to 13, yet faces exam pressure, private tutoring reliance, increased online learning, and high graduate unemployment outside select professions.

### Egypt
Egypt provides free, compulsory schooling (ages 4–14) but grapples with overcrowding, rote learning, low teacher pay, infrastructure deficits, a dependence on private tutoring, high youth unemployment, and ongoing reforms.

### Israel
Israel maintains compulsory, multi-track education, though pronounced inequalities affect Arab and Haredi students. STEM subjects are prioritized, but resource and gender disparities persist.

### Indonesia
Indonesia offers 12 years of compulsory education influenced by Islamic and colonial heritage, with the KKNI higher education framework. Rural areas face major resource and funding limitations.

### Republic of Ireland
Ireland mandates education from ages 6–16, supports preschool and homeschooling, features Catholic-dominated primary schools and diverse secondary schools, high literacy and completion rates, and provisions for special needs and international students.

### Armenia
Armenia’s K–12 system aligns with national and European higher education standards, delivers universal literacy, exam-based access, and tech innovation, but experiences occasional absenteeism.

### Nigeria
Nigeria addresses unemployment and skill gaps at primary, tertiary, and vocational levels, challenged by outdated curricula, poor infrastructure, teacher shortages, and regional disparities. Initiatives promote girls’ education, digital literacy, and international study.

### Norway
Norway offers public, compulsory education to age 16 with varied tracks, prioritizes teacher qualifications, IT integration, Bologna-aligned grading, and broad tertiary access.

### Taiwan
Taiwan provides 12 years of Mandarin-medium education with specialized secondary tracks, varied university entry routes, international partnerships, and robust special education.

### Sweden
Sweden delivers income-based preschool, nine years of compulsory education, two upper secondary tracks, Bologna-aligned higher education, financial aid, strong anti-discrimination policies, a female-majority teaching force, debate over independent schools, and limited homeschooling.

### Iran
Iran supplies free, compulsory education, competitive university entry, modernization and hybrid learning, entrepreneurship, gender parity promotion in STEM, but faces dropout rates, teacher shortages, privatization, and strict Islamic norms.

### Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s system covers kindergarten through tertiary, with selective, exam-driven university admissions. Reforms expand inclusivity for non-Cantonese speakers and adult/international education, and a demographic shift is reducing class sizes.

### Turkey
Turkey’s 12-year compulsory system includes general, religious, vocational, technical, and IB tracks. Key challenges are frequent curriculum reform, increased religious/nationalist content, textbook quality, minority language restrictions, and university entry via exam. Minority schools face struggles; internationalization is rising.

### Ghana
Ghana transitioned from colonial roots to universal, free 12-year basic education, offering academic/vocational senior high tracks and expanding higher education. Vocational training remains undervalued, completion rates low, and tertiary entry selective. Reforms and international aid broaden access; the Ministry oversees decentralized agencies with substantial, mostly domestic funding. Teacher training is standardized, adult literacy is improving, ICT use is limited, gender gaps persist, non-formal education and compulsory National Service exist, and the private sector is growing.

### France
France ensures state-regulated, secular education (ban on religious symbols since 2004), mandatory from age 3. With a national curriculum, both public and regulated private/international schools, the aging population is reducing youth numbers but school violence is rare. Higher education enrolls 2.9 million, including universities, selective grandes écoles, technical IUTs, and private colleges, all Bologna-aligned. Teacher recruitment/pay varies, and expanded 19th-century access benefited women.

### England
England mandates compulsory schooling until age 16 and continued participation until 18 via various pathways. The broad national curriculum encompasses arts, humanities, design/technology, business, and languages. State schools dominate, including comprehensive, grammar, faith, academy, and other models—largely tax-funded and regulated. Independent schools set their own curricula; home education is allowed but unfunded. The structure spans nursery, primary, secondary, and five Key Stages (KS1–KS5).

Higher education starts at 18, offering vocational and academic qualifications, with capped tuition fees for UK students, higher fees and bursaries for international students, and prestigious universities like Oxford and Cambridge attracting many. Regulation is provided by agencies such as the Office for Students and QAA, with alignment to the RQF and European standards. Adult education features diverse pathways, vocational and recreational courses, and distance learning opportunities. The introduction of tuition fees has improved funding and standards, narrowing participation gaps.

England's education system is internationally recognized for high performance, ranking above the OECD average for 15-year-olds and fourth globally in reading and literacy among primary students. The UK stands seventh on the UN Education Index. Reforms, including academy expansion in disadvantaged areas and increased school funding since 2018, have improved standards, with 88% of schools rated good or outstanding by 2015, up from 68% in 2010. Special needs provision has also benefited from increased investment.
