# Education in Sri Lanka, Egypt, Israel, Indonesia, the Republic of Ireland, Armenia, Nigeria, Norway, Taiwan, Sweden, Iran, Hong Kong, Turkey, Ghana, France, and England: Comprehensive Comparative Summary

## Sri Lanka Education System  
Sri Lanka offers free education from Kindergarten to Grade 13, with national exams controlling progression and university admission. High-stakes exams drive widespread private tutoring. Public universities provide free undergraduate degrees through district quotas, amidst governance fragmentation and resource disparities.

## Egypt Education System  
Egypt mandates free, compulsory education (ages 4–14) under centralized control. Reforms address governance, quality, and extracurricular activities amid low enrollment and underfunding. COVID-19 accelerated distance learning.

## Israel Education System  
Israel faces equity challenges amid a diverse population with growing Haredi and Arab enrollments. A tiered system culminates in critical matriculation exams tied to military and higher education access. Women outperform men academically but remain underrepresented in STEM and leadership. Despite underfunding, Christian Arab students excel. Bilingual Jewish-Arab schools promote integration amid ethnicity-related marginalization.

## Indonesia Education System  
Indonesia educates over 50 million students through secular and Islamic schools regulated by a National Qualification Framework, contending with funding disparities and teacher shortages.

## Republic of Ireland Education System  
Compulsory education spans ages 6–16, ending with Junior and Leaving Certificates. Primary education remains predominantly Catholic but with growing multi-denominational options. Secondary tracks cater to special needs and disadvantaged students, maintaining high literacy and science scores.

## Armenia Education System  
Armenia mandates free, compulsory education focusing on Armenian language and culture plus foreign languages. Post-Soviet reforms align with European standards. Child labor impacts attendance; after-school centers foster 21st-century skills.

## Nigeria Education System  
Nigeria’s 6-3-3-4 system offers nine years of free education emphasizing core and indigenous languages. Secondary education leads to exams that gate university entry. Vocational education faces staff shortages and outdated curricula. Female enrollment is rising despite socio-cultural barriers and urban-rural digital divides.

## Norway Education System  
Norway provides compulsory grunnskole and upper secondary education with general and vocational tracks including apprenticeships. Higher education quality is assured nationally, with inclusive special education.

## Taiwan Education System  
Taiwan mandates 12 years of compulsory education divided into academic and vocational tracks. Over 95% progress to university; private cram schools supplement formal education. Kindergarten enrollment and needs-based provisions expand.

## Sweden Education System  
Sweden’s 9-year compulsory education emphasizes inclusivity, with upper secondary offering preparatory and vocational paths aligned with the Bologna Process. Publicly funded independent schools generate quality debates. Early childhood education is strong, but declining test scores correlate with immigration.

## Iran Education System  
Iran delivers free, compulsory (grades 1–9) education with optional upper secondary emphasizing STEM and female participation. Over 4.5 million enroll in higher education via the Konkour exam. Challenges include teacher shortages, privatization, and politicized curricula post-1979. Distance education expanded through Payame Noor University.

## Hong Kong Education System  
Post-1997, Hong Kong shifted from English dominance to Cantonese and Putonghua bilingualism. Compulsory education concludes with the HKDSE, feeding into globally ranked universities selected via JUPAS. Reforms introduced national security education amid staff departures. Criticism of rote learning spurred interactive teaching reforms.

## Turkey Education System  
Turkey provides secular, centralized 12-year compulsory education with general, vocational, and religious Imam Hatip tracks. Government textbooks are free but often replaced by private ones due to quality concerns. Curricula emphasize Turkish nationalism and controversially deny the Armenian genocide. Minority language education declined despite treaty guarantees. Higher education expanded with international accreditations. Early English instruction and curriculum reforms spark secularism and minority rights debates.

## Ghana Education System  
Ghana rapidly expanded free basic education with strong budgetary investment, culminating in BECE exams. Senior high and vocational tracks lead to WASSCE. Tertiary education includes public and private institutions with mandatory National Service. Private schooling predominates regionally. Adult literacy has improved but female rates lag. TVET faces completion, quality, and infrastructure challenges, with informal apprenticeships common. Efforts in microcredit and qualifications frameworks face limited impact. ICT integration is a priority but constrained by access.

## France Education System  
France’s free, secular (laïcité) public system mandates education (ages 6–12), emphasizing social cohesion and banning conspicuous religious symbols except locally. Serving 18 million students including 3 million higher education attendees, pathways include preschool, primary, collège, and lycée, culminating in the baccalauréat. Private schools vary in contracts. Over 100 AEFE-regulated international schools offer English curricula. Higher education aligns with Bologna, featuring selective Grandes écoles. Teachers are recruited via competitive exams with distinct roles.

## England Education System  
Education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18, with voluntary early years education. The system entails Foundation Stage, four Key Stages, and post-16 education including GCSEs and A-levels. State-funded schools educate 93%, featuring diverse types: academies, community, free, foundation, faith, University Technical Colleges, and selective maths schools; private schools educate 7%. A national curriculum mandates core subjects plus arts, humanities, and languages, with Ofsted inspections and policies ensuring equity and uniform use.

Compulsory schooling ends at 16 but requires continued education/training until 18 through school sixth forms, further education colleges, apprenticeships, or traineeships. Post-16 options include A-levels, BTECs, NVQs, and T Levels with industry placements. Higher education comprises universities and colleges, offering degrees typically lasting three years, with tuition capped and loans available. Adult education includes vocational and distance learning via institutions like the Open University. Qualifications frameworks (RQF and FHEQ) align with European standards.

British 15-year-olds rank 13th globally in literacy, math, and science (PISA), with primary pupils ranking 4th in reading (PIRLS). The UK ranks 7th on the UN Education Index. Reforms since 1997 introduced city academies targeting deprived areas, raising school quality ratings from 68% "good/outstanding" in 2010 to 88% in 2023. Since 2018, a national funding formula delivers significant budget increases and includes substantial allocations (£780 million in 2020–21) for Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND).

## Comparative Insights  
Global education systems reflect diverse historical, religious, and political legacies shaping governance, curricula, inclusion, and secularism—for instance, France’s laïcité contrasts with Turkey’s religious tracks and Israel’s multiethnic dynamics. Demographic shifts drive equity challenges notably in Israel and Nigeria. Widespread issues include funding gaps, teacher quality, access inequalities, curriculum modernization, vocational relevance, and digitalization post-COVID. Political contexts influence academic freedom and curriculum content in Iran, Hong Kong, and Turkey. Variations in higher education expansion, STEM focus, female participation, and ICT integration mirror socio-economic trends. England exemplifies balancing access, choice, and standards via diverse school types, national curricula, inspection, and targeted funding.
