# **Merged Summary: Comparative Analysis of Education Systems in Sri Lanka, Egypt, Israel, Indonesia, Ireland, Armenia, Nigeria, Norway, Taiwan, Sweden, Iran, Hong Kong, Turkey, Ghana, and France**

## **Core Features and National Contexts**

The education systems across these 14 countries reflect a rich tapestry of historical legacies, cultural values, political ideologies, and socioeconomic priorities. In **Sri Lanka, Egypt, Israel, Indonesia, Ireland, Armenia, Nigeria, Ghana, and Turkey**, systems emphasize national identity, access, and equity—though they face persistent challenges in teacher quality, curriculum relevance, regional disparities, and graduate employability. **Sri Lanka** blends British colonial and Buddhist traditions, resulting in a competitive tertiary admissions system and limited graduate employment. **Egypt** maintains a strong Al-Azhar religious influence but suffers from poor public education quality, widespread private tutoring, and high youth unemployment. **Israel** offers a rigorous, track-based system promoting innovation and mobility, yet Arab students face underfunding and biased curricula. **Indonesia** operates a secular, anti-elitist system rooted in Pancasila, serving over 50 million students, yet rural access and infrastructure remain inadequate. **Ireland** stands out for strong performance in international assessments, inclusive special needs support, and free preschool access. **Armenia** provides free, compulsory 12-year education with strong STEM outcomes, though dropout rates and child labor remain pressing concerns. **Nigeria** follows a 6-3-3-4 structure with growing university numbers, yet suffers from underfunding, outdated curricula, and deep regional and gender disparities. **Ghana** offers free, compulsory 12-year basic education, with reforms emphasizing vocational training and gender equity, though TVET remains underdeveloped and low in prestige. **Turkey** features a centralized, nationalist curriculum with a "4+4+4" structure, promoting Turkish identity through history, Kemalism, and religious education—though controversial content (e.g., denial of the Armenian genocide, creationism) and reduced science emphasis raise concerns. **France** operates a highly centralized, secular system with strict academic gatekeeping, especially through the elite *CPGE* pathway, where students endure rigorous study and oral exams to gain entry to top universities.

In contrast, **Norway, Taiwan, Sweden, Iran, Hong Kong, and France** represent models shaped by strong public investment, political ideology, and national priorities. **Norway** is a global benchmark for equity and quality, with a publicly funded *folkeskole* system, strong teacher professionalism, and inclusive special education. **Taiwan** operates a centralized, discipline-driven model with near-universal university enrollment (over 95% of graduates), rigorous national exams, and robust vocational training—though academic stress and private cram schools are growing concerns. **Sweden** emphasizes access and equity, offering free higher education to EU/EEA citizens, though the expansion of *friskolor* (independent schools) has sparked debates over fairness and quality. **Iran** invests heavily in education (20% of government spending), with a strong focus on STEM and female enrollment, though its curriculum is ideologically rigid and subject to political control. **Hong Kong** evolved into a state-funded, meritocratic system with a strong emphasis on patriotism and national unity, especially post-2020, leading to declining enrollment and a teacher/student exodus amid political tensions. **France** maintains a centralized, secular structure with high academic selectivity and a rigorous *CPGE* system preparing students for elite institutions, though concerns about privatization and religious exclusion persist.

## **Key Trends and Common Themes**

Across all systems, **equity, access, and quality** remain central challenges. **Norway and Sweden** exemplify high public investment and inclusive access, while **Nigeria, Egypt, Ghana, and Turkey** face structural inequities in rural and marginalized communities. **Curriculum design and ideological influence** vary widely: **Taiwan and Hong Kong** emphasize academic rigor and national standards, while **Iran and Turkey** embed political and religious values into core instruction. **Private education** grows in **Iran, Taiwan, and France**, offering better resources and outcomes, but raises concerns in **Sweden and Hong Kong** over fairness and access. **Technology integration** is most advanced in **Taiwan and Hong Kong**, with AI tools like ChatGPT now embedded in coursework, while **Norway and Sweden** adopt digital tools more gradually, focusing on teacher training and inclusion. 

Despite structural differences, all systems are evolving in response to labor market demands, globalization, and political pressures. Sustainable reform requires investment in **teacher development**, **modernized curricula**, **inclusive policies**, and **infrastructure**, particularly in underserved regions. Cross-national cooperation—such as **Erasmus+ in Armenia**, **cross-Hong Kong–Guangdong agreements**, and **public-private partnerships in Ghana and France**—highlights growing recognition of the need for regional collaboration. Ultimately, education systems serve not only as institutions of learning but as mirrors of societal values—reflecting tensions between **tradition and modernity, equity and merit, national identity and global integration**, and **secularism and ideological control**.
