# Global Education Systems: Regional Overview and Key Challenges

**Southeast Asia, Africa, and the Middle East**
Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Nigeria, and Egypt share colonial legacies and structural inequalities that drive resource disparities and high dropout rates. Sri Lanka's competitive Z-Score exam fuels a booming private tutoring industry, while Indonesia navigates enduring gender literacy gaps and vocational tracks. Nigeria's transition from a "6-3-3-4" to a "9-3-4" system aims to balance free federal schools with expensive private institutions, yet political instability, funding shortages, and outdated curricula continue to disproportionately affect rural areas and girls. Similarly, Egypt and others suffer from rote memorization, high student-teacher ratios, and inadequate infrastructure, where private tutoring accounts for up to 60% of spending, deepening wealth-based inequities.

The Middle East presents divergent models: Iran maintains a centralized, free compulsory system with high female participation but struggles with teacher shortages and curriculum mandates, while Turkey faces political controversies regarding secularization, including the removal of evolution theory and the addition of Islamic law concepts. In contrast, Israel operates a four-tier compulsory system with significant structural disparities between secular and religious sectors, facing low spending per student and high dropout rates among Arab youth. Moving to Europe, Ireland sustains high performance through government initiatives like the DEIS scheme, though it lags in mathematics; Sweden utilizes a state-funded model with university eligibility based on elementary grades rather than a single national exam; and Armenia is modernizing its tertiary sector to align with European standards following the collapse of the Soviet model.

**Comparative Models and Policy Divergences**
Turkey is transitioning to a standardized 4+4+4 model where public universities offer low-cost tuition, while private institutions often achieve higher ratings, particularly in engineering. The curriculum emphasizes core subjects and mandates "Culture of Religion and Ethics" courses, reflecting Atatürk's secular nationalism, though the system faces significant controversy regarding historical narratives and minority rights. Ghana's education framework evolved from pre-colonial traditions into a structured national system following independence, featuring a 12-year basic education cycle culminating in the BECE, followed by a three-year Senior High School program. Tertiary education has expanded significantly through the private sector, with over 50 universities offering degrees up to the PhD level.

France operates under a centralized governance model rooted in late 19th-century laws designed to unify national culture, yet it grapples with a significant gap between ICT policy mandates and practical implementation. Its higher education landscape is fragmented, comprising public universities, specialized *grandes écoles*, and technical institutes aligned with European Bologna standards. Admission to elite *grandes écoles* is highly selective, requiring rigorous preparatory classes, whereas public universities offer broader access, creating a complex dual system that continues to face debates on integration and tuition policy. England's system evolved from church-linked provision to a predominantly state-funded model in 1870, offering diverse pathways from selective grammar schools to comprehensive institutions.

**Persistent Structural Challenges**
While both Ghana and France utilize centralized governance models, they face distinct structural challenges regarding equity, technological integration, and socioeconomic disparities. Ghana struggles with poor infrastructure in technical vocational training, low completion rates, and difficulties in maintaining skills among women and graduates. Similarly, France prioritizes device usage over pedagogical integration in its ICT policies, while both nations navigate the complexities of balancing elite specialization with broad public access.

Global trends show a persistent struggle to balance standardized testing with equitable access, particularly in regions grappling with post-conflict reconstruction or rapid political transformation. Specific regional innovations highlight varying approaches to modernization: Indonesia utilizes a 12-year compulsory framework with diverse academic, vocational, and religious tracks under a National Qualification Framework, though funding and equity remain concerns. Taiwan operates a highly regulated 12-year compulsory system featuring strict discipline and rigorous national entrance exams that separate students into liberal arts and science/engineering tracks, ensuring high standards through national assessments. Hong Kong, transitioning from British missionary influence to a government-funded model emphasizing bilingualism, faces student outflows due to geopolitical shifts but is integrating AI tools and expanding international enrollment.
