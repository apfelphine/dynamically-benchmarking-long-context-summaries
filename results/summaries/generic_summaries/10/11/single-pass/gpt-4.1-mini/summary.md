# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's education system is overseen primarily by multiple ministries covering general education, higher education, and vocational training, leading to administrative inefficiencies. Education, dating back over 2300 years with roots in Buddhist temple schools and colonial missionary schools, evolved into a free, universal system initiated in the 1930s. The system comprises primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate, and tertiary education stages, with compulsory education up to grade 9. State-funded public schools dominate, subdivided into national, provincial, and pirivenas (Buddhist monastic colleges), alongside private and international schools serving diverse communities including Buddhists, Hindus, Muslims, and Christians. University entry hinges on competitive exams, notably the GCE Advanced Level, with limited seats in state universities leading some students to seek education abroad or in private institutions. Vocational education follows a national qualification framework from basic to degree levels, while tuition centers and online platforms supplement formal education. Critics highlight rigidity, competitiveness, and limited private university development.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education traces back to antiquity, with significant medieval institutions like al-Azhar University and the Dar al-Ilm shaping Islamic and secular learning. Modern reforms began under Muhammad Ali in the 19th century, introducing dual systems of religious and secular education and expanding intellectual institutions. British colonial rule curtailed educational expansion, enforcing vocational focus and tuition fees. Since the 1952 revolution, free public education expanded, yet quality issues persist, marked by rote learning and inadequate infrastructure. The system encompasses compulsory basic education (kindergarten through preparatory), followed by secondary and tertiary education, with diverse public and private institutions. Higher education includes public universities and numerous private and technical institutions, with efforts underway for reforms emphasizing quality and access. Challenges include reliance on private tutoring, teacher quality, inequality in access (notably in the Arab minority), and youth unemployment. Egypt also maintains a distinct Al-Azhar system focused on Islamic education.

# Education in Israel
Israel's education is divided into primary, middle, and high school, with compulsory education through 12th grade. Schools are categorized into state-secular, state-religious, Haredi (ultra-Orthodox), and Arab sectors, alongside private schools. The Haredi and Arab sectors are growing but face challenges such as low matriculation rates and funding disparities. Israeli society highly values education as a pathway to socioeconomic advancement, with a strong emphasis on higher education, especially in science and technology fields. The matriculation certificate (Bagrut) is critical for university admission and employment. Higher education admission requires Bagrut and a psychometric test, with government subsidies and military service discounts. Israeli universities rank highly globally, especially in STEM disciplines. Despite high tertiary attainment rates, concerns exist over educational inequalities, teacher morale, and gaps between Jewish and Arab students. Women surpass men in academic enrollments, though male dominance persists in STEM fields.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia's education evolved from Islamic pesantren boarding schools and Dutch colonial stratified systems, which favored Europeans and elites while gradually expanding native education with limited funding. Early institutions emphasized religion and local languages, with gender disparities prominent historically. Post-independence efforts focused on establishing a national, secular, and inclusive education system underpinned by the 1945 constitution. The current system comprises early childhood education (PAUD), six years of primary, three years of junior high, and three years of senior high or vocational education, with compulsory education covering 12 years. Islamic schools offer pesantren, madrasah, and sekolah islam types, integrating religious and secular curricula. Higher education includes universities, institutes, polytechnics, and academies, structured under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI). Challenges include funding, quality, equity, and management, with ongoing teacher training reforms.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland's education features compulsory schooling from ages 6 to 16, encompassing primary and secondary levels, with free upper secondary education introduced in the late 1960s. Primary education spans junior infants through sixth class, often starting at age four or five, with a national curriculum emphasizing holistic child development. Secondary education includes junior and senior cycles, possibly with an optional transition year for experiential learning. Students take the Junior Certificate and Leaving Certificate exams, key to progression and university entry. Schools largely remain under religious patronage, primarily Catholic, although multi-denominational schools have grown. Special needs education is supported through statutory frameworks. Disadvantaged schools benefit from targeted programs like DEIS with additional funding and reduced class sizes. Ireland hosts many international students and offers a well-regarded education system.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia boasts near-universal literacy, with compulsory free primary and secondary education structured into primary (grades 1–4), middle (grades 5–9), and high school (grades 10–12), including general and specialized tracks. The system emphasizes Armenian language and culture, with Russian as a second language and growing English instruction. Public schools dominate, governed by national curriculum standards, while private schools operate under Ministry guidelines with potential curriculum flexibility. Higher education includes a wide range of state and private universities and specialized institutions, aligned with European frameworks such as the Bologna Process and Erasmus programs. Challenges include student absenteeism linked to child labor and education quality. Innovative technology-based education centers, like Tumo and COAF SMART, provide free skill development opportunities. Armenia participates in international higher education quality assurance and mobility agreements.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria's education system features six years of primary education starting around age five, followed by three years each in junior and senior secondary schools. Universal Basic Education aims for free, compulsory schooling for nine years. Secondary education includes federal-sponsored "unity schools," state schools, and private institutions varying in quality and resources. Students take standardized exams like the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination for advancement. Tertiary education includes public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges, with admissions based on exam scores and qualifications like IJMB or JUPEB allowing direct university entry. Vocational and informal education are widespread but face challenges like teacher shortages and outdated curricula. Literacy rates are improving but remain uneven, with gender and regional disparities. Female education is expanding but hindered by social and security issues. International schools and advanced level programs contribute to educational diversity.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education system comprises compulsory primary education (grades 1–7, ages 6–12), lower secondary school (grades 8–10), and upper secondary school (grades 11–13). Primary education emphasizes foundational skills and social learning without formal grades, while secondary levels are graded and influence upper secondary admission. Upper secondary offers general and vocational tracks, with apprenticeships integral to vocational studies. Higher education includes universities and university colleges offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, governed by national quality agencies. Private schooling is limited and must meet government standards. Special education is mainly integrated in public schools. The grading system has transitioned to align with the Bologna process. The academic year runs August to June with defined breaks and national holidays. Teacher roles vary by qualification levels.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan's education system includes compulsory 12-year basic education from kindergarten through junior high, followed by senior high or skill-based vocational high schools. Public education predominates, with public schools focusing on a rigorous national curriculum covering languages, sciences, and humanities. Senior high school tracks include liberal arts and sciences, culminating in national entrance exams. Higher education resembles the U.S. model with universities, junior colleges, and institutes of technology offering associate to doctoral degrees. Private schools, including preschools and cram schools, are common. Special education services exist for diverse needs. Taiwan encourages in-service teacher training with dedicated regional centers. University entrance is highly competitive, and vocational education is promoted as an alternative pathway.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden offers a comprehensive, compulsory nine-year grundskola followed by voluntary three-year upper secondary gymnasieskola with preparatory and vocational programs. Preschool is widely available and supports early development. The curriculum covers a broad set of subjects with national assessments in grades 3, 6, and 9. Grading starts in grade 6 using A–F scale. Upper secondary education requires passing core subjects for eligibility, with diploma projects included. Higher education follows the Bologna framework, offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees across universities and university colleges. Independent (friskolor) schools are publicly funded but privately operated, featuring diverse pedagogies and profiles. Sweden faces challenges in standardized testing integrity and educational equity, with immigrant populations impacting outcomes. The education system emphasizes inclusivity, anti-discrimination, and quality assurance.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education system, with ancient roots, underwent major reforms under the Pahlavi and post-1979 Islamic Republic eras, emphasizing Islamic values alongside modern curricula. Education is compulsory and free at primary and secondary levels, with university entrance highly competitive via the national Konkour exam. The system includes pre-primary, elementary, lower and upper secondary education, vocational schools, and higher education, which expanded significantly after the 1979 revolution. Women now comprise the majority of university students. Teacher education is centralized in dedicated universities. Foreign languages, primarily English and Arabic, are taught with French recently introduced. Private schooling has grown, often at high cost. The government invests heavily in education, though access and quality challenges remain. Specialized schools exist for gifted students (NODET), and lifelong learning is supported through various agencies.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong's education system evolved from early missionary and colonial influences to a bilingual (English and Chinese) curriculum. Compulsory education covers primary to secondary levels, culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE). The system includes publicly funded, aided, subsidized, and private international schools, many offering curricula such as IB, British GCSE/A-Level, and others. Mediums of instruction have shifted post-1997, with English-medium schools valued for prestige. Recent political changes impacted curriculum content and academic freedom. Higher education includes eight public universities and numerous private institutions, with growing international collaborations. The city supports adult education, special education integration, and programs for immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children. The system faces challenges related to academic pressure and political influences on education.

# Education in Turkey
Turkey’s education system has transitioned from Ottoman religious schools to a centralized, secular system with compulsory 12-year schooling divided into primary, middle, and secondary education. Early republican reforms introduced secular curricula and the Latin alphabet, with later expansions including vocational education and varied secondary school tracks. Religious education was initially abolished but reintroduced with state-regulated content. Foreign language instruction is mandatory, mainly English, with Kurdish and other minority languages recently added optionally. Higher education comprises numerous public and private universities, with a growing number of international students. The system features private and minority schools regulated by the state. Educational reforms and controversies include curriculum changes emphasizing nationalism, textbook quality debates, and challenges in teacher status and financing.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana's education evolved from informal indigenous learning and European missionary schools to a formalized system with compulsory basic education (ages 4–14), including kindergarten, primary, and junior high school levels. Secondary education offers academic and vocational tracks culminating in WASSCE exams. Tertiary education comprises universities, polytechnics, and colleges, experiencing growth with private sector involvement. Challenges include teacher shortages, infrastructure, quality disparities, and underdeveloped vocational training. Government and international aid support education, with significant private-public partnerships. Literacy rates and enrollment have improved, especially for girls, though inequity remains. ICT integration and adult education initiatives are underway. The system is governed by the Ministry of Education and related bodies coordinating policy, curriculum, and examinations.

# Education in France
France's education system, shaped by 19th-century reforms emphasizing secular, free, and compulsory schooling, features a centralized national curriculum spanning preschool to high school. Students progress through école maternelle, école élémentaire, collège (middle school), and lycée (high school), culminating in the baccalauréat examination. Private schools operate mostly under state contracts, following the national curriculum, with some religious or independent variants. Higher education includes universities and elite grandes écoles, with preparatory classes (CPGEs) preparing select students for top institutions. Degrees comply with the Bologna Process, with structured bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral levels. Teacher recruitment involves competitive exams and specialized training. The system emphasizes French culture and identity, with strict secularism in public institutions. Tuition is low for public institutions, with state-funded student aid programs.

# Education in England
England mandates education or training from ages 5 to 18, structured into foundation stage, four key stages, and post-16 education. The national curriculum covers core subjects with standardized testing at various stages. Schools include state-funded types (academies, community schools, free schools, voluntary aided/controlled) and independent fee-paying schools. Secondary education culminates in GCSEs and A-levels or vocational qualifications, with further education options available. Higher education is diverse with universities and colleges offering undergraduate and postgraduate degrees; tuition fees apply with financial support schemes. Ofsted inspects schools, and the system emphasizes inclusive education and equality. Adult and lifelong learning opportunities exist nationwide. National qualifications frameworks align with European standards, and the UK ranks favorably in international assessments.
