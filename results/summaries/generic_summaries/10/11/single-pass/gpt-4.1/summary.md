# Education Systems: Global Overview

---

## Sri Lanka

**Administration and Structure:**  
Education is overseen primarily by several ministries, with divisions for general, higher, vocational, and physical educational services. The National Institute of Education focuses on curriculum development. The structure comprises five stages: primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate, and tertiary education, with compulsory education through grade 9.

**History and Schools:**  
Education evolved from Buddhist temple schools and pirivenas, later influenced by colonial Christian missionaries and British reforms. Free education and national languages were institutionalized in the 20th century. Schools include government (national, provincial, pirivenas), private, international, madrasas, and semi-government types.

**Examinations and Tertiary:**  
Key exams include the Scholarship exam (Grade 5), GCE O/Ls (Grade 11), and GCE A/Ls (university entrance, Grades 12-13). State universities are free but highly competitive; fewer than 16% qualify for admission.

**Vocational and Criticisms:**  
Vocational education is coordinated by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, offering multi-level qualifications. Criticisms include the system's rigidity, competitiveness, and underdeveloped support for private institutions.

## Egypt

**Historical Evolution:**  
Ancient Egypt invented writing and pioneered schools ("House of Life"). Medieval centers like al-Azhar and Dar al-Ilm shaped Islamic education. Modern reforms started under Muhammad Ali, with British colonial neglect later impacting access and equity.

**Current System and Reforms:**  
Highly centralized, divided into three compulsory levels: basic (kindergarten, primary, preparatory), secondary (general/technical tracks), and higher education (public and private universities, technical institutes). International curricula are also popular among private schools.

**Challenges:**  
Quality remains uneven, with heavy reliance on private tutoring, inadequate teacher qualifications and infrastructure, wealth-based attainment gaps, and high youth unemployment.

## Israel

**Structure and Society:**  
Education spans primary, middle, and high school (grades 1–12); compulsory from kindergarten to age 18. Tracks include state secular, state religious, Haredi (ultra-Orthodox), and Arab, alongside private and integrated Jewish-Arab schools. The 2000 Pupils’ Rights Law prohibits sectarian discrimination.

**Higher Education and Economy:**  
Universities are partially government-subsidized; strong emphasis on science, engineering, and post-secondary attainment (46% of adults hold tertiary degrees). University admission requires the matriculation (bagrut) certificate and psychometric entrance test. Israeli universities rank highly worldwide.

**Inequality and Teacher Status:**  
Arabs and Haredi students face systemic resource disparities. Female students outperform males in schools, but fewer women are professors. Teachers contend with low wages and large classes.

## Indonesia

**Development and Structure:**  
Education evolved from Islamic pondok pesantrens, Dutch colonial stratified schools, and post-independence national reforms. The system now covers PAUD (early childhood), six years of primary, three years of junior high (SMP), and three years of senior high or vocational (SMA/SMK).

**Diversity and Access:**  
Education is compulsory for 12 years and free at lower levels. Islamic schools coexist with secular public schools. Higher education includes universities, polytechnics, and vocational institutions, organized under the National Qualification Framework.

**Challenges:**  
Despite high enrollment, disparities persist in rural areas concerning qualified teachers, school resources, and outcomes.

## Republic of Ireland

**Framework and Types:**  
Free upper second-level education since 1967. Education is compulsory from ages 6–16. Irish and English are core subjects; a growing number of schools are multi- or non-denominational.

**Structure:**  
Divided into primary (eight years), secondary (junior cycle, transition year, senior cycle), and tertiary levels, with high student performance and international recognition. DEIS schools target areas of disadvantage.

**Accessibility and Special Needs:**  
Special needs support is available, with inclusive policies. International students and adult learners are common.

---

## Armenia

**Structure and Reforms:**  
Literacy reached 100% by 1960; education transitioned from Soviet models to a system emphasizing Armenian culture. Compulsory 12-year education is free, spanning primary, basic (middle), and high school, with public schools dominating. Private schools exist but require licensing.

**Higher Education:**  
Numerous universities (state and private) and international partnerships (Lisbon Convention, Bologna Process) bolster quality assurance. Challenges include absenteeism and resource gaps.

---

## Nigeria

**Primary and Secondary:**  
Universal Basic Education mandates nine years (primary + junior secondary); senior secondary education follows. Types include federal, state, private (often expensive), and religious schools. Major entry exams: SSCE, BECE, WAEC, NECO.

**Tertiary and Vocational:**  
Numerous universities (federal, state, private), polytechnics, and colleges. Access is competitive (via JAMB, A-levels). Vocational and informal education are substantial; challenges include poor infrastructure, teacher shortages, and regional disparities.

**Gender and Literacy:**  
Efforts to boost female enrollment and literacy ongoing. Barriers include cultural factors, conflict, and poverty.

## Norway

**Structure:**  
Three levels: elementary (barneskole, 6–12), lower secondary (ungdomsskole, 13–16), and upper secondary (videregående skole, 16–19). Education is mandatory through lower secondary; upper secondary graduation rate is 73%.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities and university colleges offer general, vocational, and professional degrees. Access relies on grade point averages and entrance exams. Special education and inclusion are emphasized.

---

## Taiwan

**System and Tracks:**  
Public education is compulsory for 12 years (primary, junior high, senior high/vocational). Admission to higher levels is exam-driven. Senior high divides into academic tracks or skill-based schools. Higher education includes universities, technology institutes, and junior colleges.

**Private Education:**  
Private kindergartens, cram schools (buxiban), and international curricula are widespread.

**Challenges:**  
Academic pressure is significant; bridging vocational and academic tracks is ongoing.

---

## Sweden

**Stages and Reform:**  
A nine-year grundskola is mandatory; post-compulsory gymnasieskola offers vocational and preparatory tracks. National curricula are standardized. Grading uses A–F scale.

**Higher Education:**  
Bologna Process aligns degrees (bachelor, master, doctorate). Universities and colleges vary in specialization and grading systems. Independent (“friskolor”) and private schools exist, with policy debates on quality and equity.

---

## Iran

**Structure and History:**  
Highly centralized, spanning pre-primary, six years of elementary, three of lower secondary, three of upper secondary, followed by technical/vocational or university tracks. Education is compulsory until age 15.

**Reforms and Issues:**  
Recent reforms emphasize Islamic values, hybrid learning, teacher training, and privatization. Tuition inflation and resource disparities persist. Higher education is competitive, with entrance via the national Konkour exam.

**Special Populations:**  
Efforts support women’s education, gifted children, and religious minorities; literacy rates have improved, but regional gaps remain.

## Hong Kong

**Evolution and Structure:**  
Rooted in missionary schools, the system expanded under British and Chinese influences. Education is compulsory through secondary school (6 years primary, 6 secondary). Various types include government, aided, DSS, private, international, and ESF schools.

**Curriculum and Exams:**  
Recent curricular reforms promote nationalism and national security; HKDSE (Diploma of Secondary Education) is the secondary leaving exam.

**Higher Education:**  
Eight major UGC-funded universities; medium of instruction is primarily English. Numerous international schools offer global curricula.

---

## Turkey

**Structure and Reform:**  
Pre-primary (optional), primary (8 years, compulsory), and secondary (typically 4 years). Tracks include general, Anatolian (language-focused), vocational/technical, and religious high schools. Curriculum changes are frequent; religious content has been re-introduced.

**Higher Education:**  
Universities, faculties, vocational schools, and conservatories. Admission is via national exams (YÖS for international students). Technical and vocational education is available post-secondary.

**Private and Religious Schools:**  
Private schools entail Turkish, minority, foreign, and international institutions. Nationalism and secularism are pillars, but recent religious and nationalist content has increased.

---

## Ghana

**Development and Structure:**  
Transitioned from informal, mission-based to formal, centralized systems. Education is free and compulsory through basic (kindergarten, primary, junior high) and extends to senior high and tertiary levels. English is the medium of instruction.

**Enrollment and Outcomes:**  
Enrollment rates have risen; gender parity achieved in primary/secondary. Tertiary access is competitive, with universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education.

**Vocational and Private Education:**  
Vocational/technical education remains underdeveloped and informal. Private-public partnership is essential to system expansion. ICT and literacy programs target adult and rural populations.

---

## France

**Governance and Structure:**  
Administered by the Ministry of National Education, highly centralized with standardized curricula. School is mandatory from ages 3–16, divided into maternelle (preschool), école élémentaire (elementary), collège (middle), and lycée (high school). Religious instruction is excluded in public schools.

**Higher Education:**  
Split between public universities and elite grandes écoles. Admission to higher education often depends on the baccalauréat exam, with preparatory (CPGE) classes for selective pathways. Tuition is low for EU students; private and technical institutions have higher fees.

**Private and International Schools:**  
Private schools may follow state or independent curricula. International schools are expanding, overseen by AEFE.

---

## England

**Compulsory Education:**  
Mandatory from ages 5–18, structured as early years (nursery, reception), primary (key stages 1–2), secondary (key stages 3–4), and post-16 (sixth form, further education, apprenticeships). Core subjects are English, math, and science.

**Schools:**  
Types include state-funded (community, academy, free, faith, foundation), grammar, technical colleges, and private (independent/public/prep) schools.

**Examinations and Tertiary:**  
GCSEs are taken at age 16, with A-levels or vocational qualifications following. University admission is contingent on qualifications and often fees (£9,535/year cap for domestic students).

**Standards and Funding:**  
England ranks high on international assessments, focusing on equity and inclusion. National funding reforms aim to balance resources among schools.
