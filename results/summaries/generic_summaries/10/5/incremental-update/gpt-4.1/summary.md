## Summary

Education systems across Turkey, Iran, France, Taiwan, England, Nigeria, Ghana, Ireland, Hong Kong, Israel, Sweden, Sri Lanka, Norway, Egypt, Armenia, and Indonesia reveal diverse responses to historical, political, and cultural forces but share common aims: equitable access, modernization, technology integration, improved teacher quality, and inclusive provision. All confront challenges of resource allocation, inequities (social, gender, regional), religious influences, examination pressures, infrastructure limitations, and governance concerns. Global trends include ongoing reform, expansion of vocational options, support for special needs students, and transformation of examination systems.

### Country Highlights

#### Turkey
Turkey’s centralized 4+4+4 system balances academic and vocational tracks amid civic and religious tensions in an evolving secular-religious society.

#### Iran
Iran's centralized, Islamized, gender-segregated system backs women’s STEM participation but struggles with university access, teacher shortages, privatization, dropouts, and the expansion of distance learning.

#### France
France's secular, state-run system presents academic and vocational routes, competitive tertiary admissions, and faces issues with testing, student welfare, and privatization.

#### Taiwan
Taiwan features a twelve-year system emphasizing academic/vocational choice, private and English-immersion education, and robust international partnership.

#### England
England manages diverse school types through Ofsted, frequent reforms, and strong links to European higher education.

#### Nigeria
Nigeria provides free primary schooling, but secondary/vocational access is affected by gender, region, and security; private schools generally outperform state provision.

#### Ghana
Ghana's exam-driven public system is implementing equity-focused reforms, teacher training, ICT integration, and competitive higher education.

#### Ireland
Ireland’s system, largely denominational but becoming more secular, stresses inclusivity, stability, and standardized testing.

#### Hong Kong
Hong Kong mandates education from age 6–18, maintains rigorous curricula, supports adult/special needs learning, and expands international enrollment.

#### Israel
Israel’s secular, religious, Haredi, and Arab tracks foster innovation and inclusivity, but grapple with achievement gaps, STEM gender disparities, teacher shortages, and curriculum debates.

#### Sweden
Sweden offers free, compulsory, and post-compulsory schooling with academic/vocational choices, national exams, public funding, competitive admissions, and religious private friskolor controversies.

#### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka combines public, private, religious, and international education, with exam-based progression and limited university capacity driving private/foreign options.

#### Norway
Norway provides comprehensive public education from early years through upper secondary, with mainstreamed special needs support and expert teacher training.

#### Egypt
Egypt’s centralized system, under reform, offers compulsory basic education and varied secondary/higher tracks. Challenges include poor rankings, teacher pay, regional disparities, youth unemployment, tutoring costs, with reforms prioritizing access, curriculum, teacher development, and governance; distance learning and religious schooling remain substantial.

#### Armenia
Armenia boasts near-universal literacy, a largely public, compulsory system, specialized tracks, and a tertiary sector within the European higher education area. Modernization confronts absenteeism, child labor dropouts, rising quality expectations, and benefits from initiatives like the Tumo Center and COAF SMART for rural youth.

#### Indonesia
Indonesia’s vast system evolved from Islamic/colonial roots to a post-independence, inclusive framework. It is compulsory for twelve years and includes public, private, and religious schools, with instruction primarily in Indonesian and a curriculum focused on Pancasila and rote methods, alongside local autonomy. While primary enrollment is high, upper levels see drop-off due to disparities in teacher quality and resources, especially in remote regions. Vocational and special needs tracks exist and the semester system prevails. The 2013 curriculum for primary and secondary offers specialization in natural sciences, social sciences, languages, and religious studies, with subjects tailored to school type. Islamic schooling ranges from pesantren (traditional boarding schools) to madrasah and sekolah Islam, integrating religious and general curricula—most are moderate in theology and approach. Higher education comprises universities, institutes, colleges, polytechnics, and academies, with academic, vocational, and professional programs structured by the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (since 2012), spanning diplomas through doctorates.

---

In summary, these countries strive for inclusive, high-quality, and modern education while navigating persistent inequities, funding and infrastructure problems, religious and social divides, exam stress, and international trends in reform, vocational provision, and support for special needs learners.
