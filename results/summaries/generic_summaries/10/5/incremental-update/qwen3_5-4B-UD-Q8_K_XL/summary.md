# **Working Summary of Chunks 1 to 45: Global Education Systems (Sri Lanka, Norway, Egypt, Armenia, Indonesia)**

### **1. Sri Lanka: Structure, Inequality, and Brain Drain**
Formalized in 1836 and made free in 1938, Sri Lanka's system spans Kindergarten to Grade 13 (ages 3–17), aiming for equity through the National Institute of Education (NIE). The structure divides students into five stages, with schools split between elite government **National Schools** and under-resourced **Provincial Schools**, alongside unregulated private and international options. While tertiary education offers structured progression, it faces high graduate unemployment (outside medicine and IT), "brain drain," and a rigid, overly competitive environment. Mandatory military-style leadership training for undergraduates was abolished in 2015.

### **2. Norway: Balanced Academic and Vocational Pathways**
Evolving from cathedral schools to the *grunnskole*, Norway maintains a system with limited private schooling and an August–June academic year. Education is divided into Elementary, Lower Secondary, and Upper Secondary levels, with recent reforms emphasizing IT and career studies. The "2+2 model" (school followed by apprenticeship) is common, distinguishing higher education between theoretical universities and vocational colleges. Grading ranges from 1.0 to 6.0, and special education laws ensure most students receive support within local schools.

### **3. Egypt: Access vs. Quality and Systemic Corruption**
Egypt's history ranges from ancient libraries to a modern centralized system established after Muhammad Ali's reforms. While free compulsory basic education (ages 4–14) has expanded access, quality remains low due to high student-teacher ratios, rote memorization, and poor infrastructure. The sector is plagued by systemic corruption, low teacher salaries attracting underqualified staff, and a severe wealth gap. A critical challenge is the **over-reliance on expensive private tutoring**, which accounts for **over 60% of educational spending**. The system comprises international schools (British, American, IB), the state-run Al-Azhar system (religious studies), and specialized agricultural schools, yet it continues to struggle with high youth and graduate unemployment.

### **4. Armenia: Post-Soviet Transition and EU Integration**
Transitioning from a centralized Soviet model to an autonomous system prioritizing Armenian language and culture, Armenia maintains a free, compulsory 12-year K-12 system where Russian remains widely taught alongside English and Chinese. While primary and secondary enrollment rates exceed 90%, high school enrollment lags behind EU and OECD standards. The system is dominated by **public schools (97%)**, which face funding challenges, while **private schools** offer specialized programs with flexible curricula but limited access due to cost. **Higher education** is robust, featuring 26 state universities and 33 licensed private institutions, including the historic **Yerevan State University (1920)** and **Yerevan State Medical University (1930)**. The country boasts particular strength in Physics, Mathematics, Medicine, Engineering, and IT, with state exams serving as the critical gateway for university admission. Armenia has integrated into the **European Higher Education Area** via the Bologna Process and Lisbon Recognition Convention (1997), becoming a full member of the European University Association and associate member of Horizon Europe. Since 2004, it has participated in EU mobility programs like Erasmus Mundus and Erasmus+, while its quality assurance agency was incorporated into ENQA and EQAR in 2017. However, **student absenteeism and dropout rates have surged significantly since 2002**, driven by child labor and low educational quality, though new technology-based centers are emerging to provide youth with 21st-century skills.

### **5. Indonesia: Colonial Legacy, 12-Year Mandate, and Islamic Education**
Indonesian education history spans distinct colonial and post-colonial eras. During the **Dutch colonial period**, education was segregated by race and class, favoring Europeans and limited privileged natives, though the "Ethical Policy" of the 1870s gradually opened schools to natives. The Dutch introduced a tiered system (e.g., HIS, HBS) and village schools, but funding remained low, resulting in low literacy (7.4% in 1931) and a stark gender gap (only 0.5% of female natives were literate in 1920). Indigenous figures established institutions to empower local populations. By the 1930s, limited formal education existed across provinces, supplemented heavily by missionary schools in outer islands. During the **Japanese occupation (WWII)**, the Dutch system was consolidated for military and ideological indoctrination, leading to a sharp decline in enrollment, particularly for secondary education.

Following independence in 1945, Indonesia established a new, non-colonial system to promote nationalism and equality, with the 1945 Constitution guaranteeing education rights and integrating religious institutions like *Pesantren* and *Madrasah*. The current structure mandates **12 years of schooling**:
*   **Preschool (PAUD):** Non-compulsory (ages 2–6), 99.35% private.
*   **Primary (SD):** Compulsory, 6 years, shifting to Indonesian instruction.
*   **Junior High (SMP):** Compulsory, 3 years, with academic, vocational, and domestic science tracks.
*   **Senior High (SMA/SMK):** Optional academic track or 47 vocational fields.
*   **Special Needs:** Separate schools (SLB) for disabilities.

The system serves over 50 million students in 300,000 schools, with high net enrollment rates (93.5% for primary/middle) but low tertiary participation (36.31%). Pedagogy traditionally emphasizes rote memorization and teacher authority, though the curriculum's focus on *Pancasila* diminished after 1998 decentralization. Despite high access, challenges persist regarding funding, teacher shortages in remote areas, certification, and educational equity.

**Recent Curriculum and Higher Education Developments (2012–2013):**
*   **Curriculum (2013):** Specialized tracks focus on Natural sciences, Social sciences, Languages, and religious studies (Islamic, Christian, Catholic).
*   **Islamic Schools:** Three main types exist:
    *   *Pesantren:* Traditional boarding schools led by religious scholars, offering a mix of deep religious study and modern subjects; often moderate in theology.
    *   *Madrasah:* Integrates pesantren religious content with Western-style secular curricula.
    *   *Sekolah Islam:* Uses the national secular curriculum supplemented by Islamic content.
    *   **Equivalency:** Islamic schools correspond to standard Indonesian levels (e.g., MI = SD, MA = SMA).
*   **Higher Education:** Universities (*Universitas*), Institutes (*Institut*), Colleges (*Sekolah Tinggi*), Polytechnics (*Politeknik*), and Academies (*Akademi*) offer Academic, Vocational, and Professional education. Since 2012, the system operates under the **Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI)**, standardizing degrees from Diploma (Level 3–4) to Doctorate (Level 9).
