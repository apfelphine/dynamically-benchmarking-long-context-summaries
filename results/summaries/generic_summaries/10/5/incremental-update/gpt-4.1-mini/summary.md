# Updated Summary of Education in Turkey, Iran, France, Taiwan, England, Nigeria, Ghana, Republic of Ireland, Hong Kong, Israel, Sweden, Sri Lanka, Norway, Egypt, Armenia, and Indonesia

## Historical Development, Curriculum, and Educational Philosophy

### Turkey, Iran, France, Taiwan, England
- Turkey’s post-Republic reforms unified secular education, initially removing then reintroducing religious content.
- Iran’s system is shaped by historical reforms and post-1979 Islamization, with gender-segregated instruction primarily in Persian.
- France established free, secular schooling since the 19th century, emphasizing civic values and restricting religious symbols.
- Taiwan’s compulsory 12-year education primarily uses Mandarin, with strict discipline and national exams controlling progression.
- England moved from church-affiliated to state-funded compulsory education, integrating vocational and adult education.

### Nigeria, Ghana, Republic of Ireland, Hong Kong, Israel
- Nigeria offers 9 years of free Universal Basic Education split into stages, with expanding English-medium international schools and digital literacy ambitions for 2030.
- Ghana’s colonial missionary roots evolved into free, compulsory education with high literacy and informal, low-prestige vocational training.
- Ireland provides free upper secondary education with a bilingual approach and predominantly Catholic primary schools; special education legislation and international student inflows exist.
- Hong Kong’s curriculum reflects British and Chinese influences; recent National Security Law reforms promote mainland identity amid demographic shifts, vocational expansion, and diversity.
- Israel features four distinct K–12 streams (state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, Arab), with growing minority populations, high tertiary attainment, but funding and quality disparities.

### Sweden, Sri Lanka, Norway, Egypt
- Sweden’s comprehensive 9-year compulsory education includes standardized testing and a broad upper secondary with vocational and academic tracks; debates persist on independent schools and public funding.
- Sri Lanka’s educational tradition spans Buddhist origins, colonial missionary schools, and free education since 1938, with multilingual instruction and competitive exams.
- Norway’s system evolved from historic cathedral and folk schools to integrated academic-vocational pathways, inclusive education policies, and a structured grading system.
- Egypt’s millennia-old centers evolved through Islamic, colonial, and modern reforms, resulting in a centralized system with basic, secondary, Al-Azhar religious streams, and expanding higher education amid quality and equity challenges.

### Armenia and Indonesia
- Armenia boasts universal literacy since 1960, with K–12 free education divided into foundational, middle, and specialized secondary levels; higher education integrates European frameworks and features technology centers.
- Indonesia’s education reflects Islamic kingdoms, colonial stratification, and post-independence reforms, emphasizing nationalism and Pancasila values. Early childhood education is predominately private and non-compulsory; compulsory 12-year secular and religious schooling exists with distinct specialization groups in sciences, social sciences, languages, and Islamic/theological fields.

## Education Structure, Language, and School Types
- Compulsory education durations range from 9 to 13 years, structured into early childhood, primary, lower secondary, and upper secondary levels with vocational and special education options.
- Instruction languages align with national identities and sociolinguistic realities—such as Turkish, Persian, French, Mandarin, English, Irish, Cantonese, Hebrew, Swedish, Sinhala, Norwegian, Arabic, Armenian, and Bahasa Indonesia.
- Diverse institutional forms coexist: public, private, religious, international, and vocational schools shaped by historical, political, and religious contexts.
- Indonesia uniquely categorizes Islamic education into pesantren (boarding schools with varying theology), madrasah (blending religious and secular subjects), and sekolah Islam (secular curriculum with Islamic content).

## Assessment, Progression, and Examinations
- Countries deploy national assessment systems integral to certification and tertiary access, such as Sri Lanka’s Scholarship and GCE exams, Sweden’s standardized tests at key grades, Norway’s combined assessments, Armenia’s certificate system, and Egypt’s traditional but reforming exams.
- Broad pressures exist to move from rote memorization toward holistic, skill-based learning.

## Higher Education and Research
- Diverse higher education systems include Scandinavian integration with Bologna processes and subsidized access; others face challenges of funding, quality, and graduate employment.
- Indonesia’s universities, institutes, polytechnics, and academies follow the Indonesian National Qualification Framework, offering academic to specialist professional qualifications.
- Armenia’s sector engages European quality and mobility frameworks, with technological skill hubs like Tumo enhancing 21st-century competencies.
- Global trends show rising female participation but persistent STEM gender gaps, internationalization growth, and COVID-19–accelerated digital learning.

## Private Sector, Equity, Technology, and Social Dimensions
- Private and international schools contribute to socioeconomic stratification.
- Equity efforts harness legislation, funding schemes, and expansion of special education.
- ICT adoption varies widely, with rural and remote areas impacted; the pandemic spurred digital education uptake.
- Teacher remuneration, workload, and school climate, including issues like corporal punishment and violence, influence quality.
- Curricula and policies balance secularism, religious identity, multicultural integration, and national narratives.

## Performance, Outcomes, and Challenges
- Across nations, equity in access and quality, demographic changes affecting enrollment, infrastructure deficits, and reconciling tradition with modernization are key challenges.
- Early childhood education is robust in countries like Sweden but uneven elsewhere.
- Standardized test outcomes fluctuate amid social and policy shifts; textbook and funding controversies affect perceptions of fairness.
- Private tutoring is widespread in some contexts (e.g., Egypt, Sri Lanka).
- Youth unemployment correlates with educational system effectiveness, vocational relevance, and social mobility.
- Indonesia’s specialization in public education allocates balanced time across natural sciences, social sciences, language, and Islamic/theological studies, matching the structure of its Islamic schools with national equivalents aligned to preschool through vocational education.
