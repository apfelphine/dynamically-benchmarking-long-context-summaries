## Global Education Systems Comparison

Global education systems are highly diverse in structure and philosophy, universally facing challenges concerning funding and equity. Models range from strictly centralized state controls to hybrid systems influenced by the private sector, with varied priorities between economic growth and inclusion.

**System Structures and Overviews:**

*   **Developing Economies:** Nations like Ghana and Nigeria struggle with limited Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET), low literacy, funding deficits, and gender bias in vocational training.
*   **Developed/Advanced Systems:** Advanced systems exhibit varied approaches:
    *   **Centralized/Secular Models:** France utilizes a centralized, secular system featuring the *baccalauréat* and *grandes écoles*, while Turkey runs a centralized, secular 12-year system emphasizing nationalism via a national exam. Iran employs a centralized STEM focus via the Konkour exam.
    *   **Market/Hybrid Models:** Israel mandates K-12 education across four distinct tracks to support its high-tech sector despite internal inequities.
    *   **Comprehensive/Inclusion Models:** Norway mandates education (ages 6–16) with unified tracks and promotes special education inclusion. Sweden offers free preschool and compulsory *grundskola*, linking tertiary education to the Bologna Process with partial state subsidies.
    *   **Specific Models:** Taiwan emphasizes discipline through K-Senior High while depending on private cram schools. Ireland mandates education (age 6–16) and supports low-income families. Hong Kong provides free primary/compulsory education across secondary tracks but contends with rote learning. Sri Lanka's British-standard system requires education until Grade 9, balancing free public and private schools, where intense exam competition drives private tutoring, and state university access is limited.
*   **Regional System Profiles:**
    *   **Egypt:** Employs a complex, dual structure featuring religious ($\text{kuttab}$) and secular schools. This centralized system suffers from low quality, high student-to-teacher ratios, and infrastructure gaps, compounded by fund embezzlement, exam leakage, poor teacher conditions, and a major reliance on private tutoring (reported by 58% of families).
    *   **Armenia:** Achieved 100% literacy since 1960, offering free and compulsory primary and secondary education while modernizing from a Soviet model. Its structure involves 4-year primary, 5-year middle, and three-year high school, using state exams for university entry. While 97% of institutions are public, funding is a challenge, though it aligns with European standards via Bologna.
    *   **Indonesia:** The system has evolved from Islamic kingdoms to its current tiered structure, comprising non-compulsory early childhood care (PAUD) and 12 years of compulsory primary and secondary education. Public schooling is state-managed, but Islamic education manifests in three forms: *pesantren* (traditional boarding schools), *madrasah* (integrating religious and secular subjects), and *sekolah islam* (using the secular curriculum with Islamic additions). The higher education landscape is diverse, spanning academic, vocational, and professional tracks, and is structured by the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI), which defines qualifications up to the Doctorate. Despite high primary completion rates among its over 50 million students, participation drops at higher levels, and challenges persist regarding funding, equity, teacher shortages, and resource distribution, with a historical tendency toward rote learning.
