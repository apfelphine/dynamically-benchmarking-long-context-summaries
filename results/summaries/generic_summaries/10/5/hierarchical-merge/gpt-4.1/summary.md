# Comparative Overview of Global Education Systems

## Europe
European education systems, including those in Sweden, France, England, Ireland, and Norway, are characterized by robust public sector oversight, compulsory schooling lasting between 9 and 12 years, and an emphasis on secular, standardized curricula. Sweden offers comprehensive schooling with university-preparatory and vocational tracks, highlighting social equity and support for minorities; independent schools have sparked debates over grade inflation. France and England maintain high educational standards and inclusive policies, while Ireland implements equity initiatives and religious patronage reforms. Norway combines strong regulatory oversight and integrated special education, but faces critiques regarding academic pacing. Higher education across these nations is generally affordable or state-supported, and follows harmonized European frameworks.

## Middle East
Turkey and Iran feature highly centralized, state-directed education emphasizing national values—secularism in Turkey and Islamic principles in Iran. Both countries maintain extensive compulsory education but face challenges in curricular content, minority rights, and equity for marginalized groups. Israel provides parallel secular, religious, and Arab tracks, with disparities persisting in outcomes and resources. Educational quality is high at the university level, but persistent gaps remain in access and teacher support.

## Africa
Nigeria and Ghana have implemented universal compulsory education, focusing on expanding access and achieving gender parity. Despite public investment, these systems grapple with inadequate infrastructure, teacher shortages, and high dropout rates. In Egypt, broad reforms have improved access, but socioeconomic divides, underqualified teachers, and significant private tutoring undermine progress. Ghana and Nigeria continue to pursue digital skills development and adult literacy programs to close existing gaps.

## Asia
Taiwan, Hong Kong, Indonesia, and Armenia exhibit diverse models of compulsory education ranging from discipline-focused, exam-driven systems to those emphasizing national identity. Taiwan and Hong Kong are expanding internationalisation and integrating reforms for immigrant and special needs learners. Indonesia struggles with resource disparities and declining higher education participation despite improvements in teacher training. Armenia boasts 100% literacy and European alignment in higher education, but faces challenges due to child labor, absenteeism, and funding limits.

## South Asia
Sri Lanka’s multi-track system delivers public, private, and religious education, with progression heavily reliant on competitive exams. The tertiary sector is free at state universities but highly selective, with ongoing debates over privatization and curriculum relevance to labor market needs. Efforts to broaden access and modernize vocational education are continuing, though expansion is slow.

---

Across these countries, education systems strive to balance universal access, equity, quality, and national identity. Persistent issues include disparities in infrastructure, teacher shortages, and marginalization of minority groups, driving ongoing reform and increasing focus on inclusivity, digital skills, and reliable pathways into higher education and employment.
