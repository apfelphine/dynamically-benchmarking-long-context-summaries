**Education in Turkey**  
Turkey’s education system evolved from fragmented, religiously-based institutions to a unified, secular national system post-1923. Key reforms include the 1924 Law of Integration of Education, which merged pre-existing schools (district schools, madrasas, reform schools) into a secular, state-controlled system. The Ministry of National Education, established in 1933, oversees all education, with a focus on national identity and Turkish nationalism. The 1997 reforms extended compulsory education to eight years, later expanded to 12 years in 2012 under the "4+4+4" system.  

**Primary education** (ages 6–14) is compulsory, free, and covers core subjects including Turkish, mathematics, science, and foreign languages (commonly English). Students transition to middle school (ages 11–14), which includes a mix of academic and vocational tracks.  

**Secondary education** includes general and vocational tracks. General schools (lycées) offer a broad curriculum leading to a high school diploma, while vocational schools train students for specific careers. Students take national exams (e.g., TEOG) to enter higher education.  

**Higher education** includes public and private universities, with state-funded institutions offering degrees in arts, sciences, and engineering. The system emphasizes Turkish language and history, with a notable shift in 2017 to include religious content (e.g., jihad) in curricula.  

**Controversies** include curriculum changes (e.g., removal of evolution), textbook quality issues, and historical bias—particularly in the treatment of the Armenian genocide. Critics argue the system promotes nationalism and lacks academic diversity.  

**Private education** includes Turkish, minority, foreign, and international schools. Dershane schools were reformed into academic high schools in 2015.  

**Religious education** was banned in 1927 but reintroduced in 1956. It now includes 8 years of instruction, with content covering multiple world religions and ethical values.  

**Foreign languages** are taught from grade 2 onward, with English dominant. In 2011, the government introduced a plan to hire 40,000 language assistants. Kurdish, Abkhaz, and Arabic were added to the curriculum in 2012–2017.  

**Criticism** centers on frequent policy changes, curriculum bias, and inadequate textbook quality, with concerns over the suppression of secular and scientific content.
