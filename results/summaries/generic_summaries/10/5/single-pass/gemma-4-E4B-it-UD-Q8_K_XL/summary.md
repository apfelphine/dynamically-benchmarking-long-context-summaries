# Global Education Systems Summary

## Turkey
The Turkish education system evolved significantly since the foundation of the Republic, with the Ministry of Education reorganizing its structure. Initially, education was split into three distinct channels: district schools/Quranic madrasas, Reform schools, and minority colleges. The 1924 Law of Integration combined these channels to enforce secularism. Education has undergone recent changes, including the extension of compulsory education to 12 years and curriculum updates by the AKP government, notably removing evolution theory and incorporating jihad. Pre-primary education is optional, and primary school is now 8 years, with secondary education offering general, vocational, and technical tracks. The system recently shifted to a "4+4+4" structure in 2012.

Higher education features 167 public and private universities, with high quality varying significantly. The country is an international hub for students, supported by programs like the Türkiye Scholarships. Criticism has focused on constant systemic changes, the perceived bias in state-approved history textbooks, and the reliance on private tutoring due to low-quality state textbooks. Religious education, which was initially banned, has been re-established and is currently part of the curriculum.

## Iran
Iranian education has historical roots in ancient Persia and modern reform during the Safavid and Qajar eras. The Literacy Corps (1969–1979) aimed to improve literacy cheaply. Post-1979, the system underwent Islamization, including the segregation of students by sex and the mandatory observation of Islamic Law. Education is highly centralized under the Ministry of Education.

The grading system ranges from pre-primary (optional) to university (4-7 years). Public and private schools exist, though private sector growth is accelerating. Government spending on education is high, at 20% of government spending and 5% of GDP. Challenges include a reported dropout rate of 4 million children in K-12 education and issues related to teacher shortages and privatization trends.

## France
French education is governed by the Ministry of National Education and Youth, upholding the principles of secularism (*laïcité*). The modern system was shaped by Jules Ferry in the late 19th century, mandating free, compulsory, and secular instruction for all children aged 6 to 12.

The curriculum is standardized across public and subsidized private schools, although specialized sections exist. Post-primary education is divided into *collège* (middle school, 11–15) and *lycée* (high school, 15–18), preparing students for the *baccalauréat*. Higher education is divided between public universities and elite *grandes écoles*. Tuition is low for EU/EEA citizens in public universities, while private education ranges from moderate to very high cost.

## Taiwan
Taiwan’s education system mandates 12 years of compulsory education, implemented by 2014, which replaced the former 9-year requirement. The system is highly centralized and structured across distinct levels: elementary (grades 1–6), junior high (grades 7–9), and senior high (grades 10–12).

Public schools are the norm, emphasizing Mandarin as the medium of instruction. Discipline is generally strict, with required daily cleanup rituals. Higher education is similar to the American model, with high enrollment rates; NTU is a leading public institution. Private schooling and extensive cram schools (*buxiban*) are prevalent, driven by parental pressure for high academic achievement.

## England
Education is compulsory from age 5 to 18, with options for training or work after age 16. The system is structured using Key Stages (KS1-KS5), leading to GCSEs and A-levels/IBDP qualifications. Most state-funded schools are free, though independent schools exist.

State-funded schools are subject to rigorous inspection by Ofsted, and many are specialist schools. Funding is administered via a national formula. Higher education is diverse, with public universities and private colleges. The system emphasizes broad development, and there is a focus on addressing educational disadvantage through schemes like Deis.

## Nigeria
The system is structured around a 9-year Universal Basic Education (UBE), comprising 6 years of primary school and 3 years of Junior Secondary School (JSS). Secondary education then includes 3 years of Senior Secondary School (SSS).

Education is decentralized, with Federal and State governments managing different school types. Federal schools are often viewed as elite due to merit-based admissions via the National Common Entrance Examination. Vocational training is crucial, managed by the National Board for Technical Education, though it suffers from issues like outdated curricula and teacher shortages. Literacy efforts are ongoing, with a stated goal of 95% digital literacy by 2030.

## Ghana
Ghana's education is divided into three parts: basic education (12 years, ages 4–15), secondary education, and tertiary education. Basic education is free and compulsory, ending with the Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE).

The system has undergone numerous reforms, including the 1987 Education Act. The system is a shared effort between the government and the private sector, which significantly outnumbers public schools. Tertiary education is expanding, with a growing private sector presence. Challenges include inequality, as higher education is disproportionately consumed by the wealthy, and the poor quality of vocational training.

## Republic of Ireland
Education is compulsory from age 6 to 16, or completion of three years of second-level education. English is primary, though Irish is taught as a core subject, and Gaelscoileanna use Irish as the main medium.

The structure includes Junior Cycle (3 years) leading to the Junior Certificate, followed by the Senior Cycle (2 years) leading to the Leaving Certificate. State-funded schools cover the majority of pupils, with a strong historical link to religious patronage (Catholic control is dominant in primary). Higher education is globally ranked highly, and students can opt for integrated master's degrees.

## Hong Kong
The system mandates primary and secondary schooling, with public education being free. Secondary education is divided into junior and senior years, culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) or the IBDP.

Medium of instruction has shifted from English to Chinese (Cantonese) in most secondary schools. International schools offer alternative curricula (IBDP, A-levels). There is a strong emphasis on academic rigor, though recent political changes have shifted the curriculum toward patriotism and national security, leading to increased student departures.

## Israel
The system is compulsory from kindergarten through 12th grade, structured into primary, middle, and high school. There are four distinct tracks: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (*Haredi*), and Arab.

Societally, education is viewed as a key to mobility, leading parents to sacrifice for high standards. However, significant disparities exist, particularly regarding the under-resourced Arab sector and the lack of mainstream education for *Haredi* students. Higher education is highly ranked internationally, and while the military conscription is a factor, students can defer it to study.

## Sweden
The system is comprehensive, based on a nine-year compulsory school (*grundskola*) for ages 6–15, which covers 16 mandatory subjects. Upper secondary school (*gymnasieskola*) is elective, lasting three years and offering both preparatory and vocational programs.

Preschool is widely available and free from age three. The system utilizes a point-based grading scale, and national tests are administered in grades 3, 6, and 9 to ensure equivalent assessment. While public schools dominate, a growing number of publicly funded independent schools (*friskolor*) operate under public funding.

## Sri Lanka
The system is divided into primary (5 years), junior secondary (4 years), senior secondary (2 years), collegiate, and tertiary levels. Primary education is compulsory until Grade 9.

The system is heavily influenced by historical religious institutions (Buddhist *Pirivenas*). Government schools are broadly categorized as National (direct central control), Provincial (local control), or Pirivenas. Tertiary education is competitive; state university admission is based on high scores in the G.C.E Advanced Level exam. Tuition classes are a lucrative, yet controversial, supplementary market.

## Norway
Education is compulsory for ages 6–16, comprising elementary (ages 6–12) and lower secondary (ages 13–16), which together form the *grunnskole*. Upper secondary school (*videregående skole*) is elective, lasting three years.

The system is heavily public, though private schools exist under strict guidelines. The 1994 reform merged vocational and general studies, allowing for credit transfer. Students can specialize in general studies or vocational tracks. Higher education is state-run, and quality is assured by NOKUT. Special education emphasizes inclusion within ordinary schools.

## Egypt
The system is highly centralized, with basic education (ages 4–14) being compulsory and free in government institutions. Basic education includes kindergarten (2 years), primary (6 years), and preparatory (3 years). Secondary education has general (3 years) and technical (3–5 years) tracks.

Higher education is extensive but centralized, with limited institutional autonomy. Despite significant expansion in access, quality is hampered by systemic issues like high student-to-teacher ratios, low teacher wages, and over-reliance on private tutoring to overcome public education deficiencies.

## Armenia
The compulsory system spans 12 years and is split into primary (4 years), middle (5 years), and high school (3 years). Public schools form 97% of the sector and adhere to a national curriculum blending global trends with local culture.

Private schools must meet state licensing guidelines despite operating on tuition fees. Higher education institutions are numerous, with Yerevan State University being the largest. The system is increasingly integrating with European standards (Bologna Process) and has a focus on international cooperation.

## Indonesia
Education is compulsory for twelve years. The system features state-run, nonsectarian public schools, alongside religious private schools (*pesantren* and *madrasah*). The curriculum traditionally emphasized secular wisdom, though Pancasila’s role is diminishing.

The structure includes primary (6 years), junior high (3 years), and high school (3 years). Higher education is diverse, offering academic, vocational, and professional tracks under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI). Private schooling is pervasive, and the disparity between well-resourced and under-resourced areas is significant.

## International Comparisons
Various countries demonstrate different philosophical approaches: Turkey emphasizes secular nationalism; Iran emphasizes Islamic values; France prioritizes secular, common culture transmission; England balances state funding with academic selectivity; and Israel heavily values education as a pathway to socioeconomic mobility.
International student presence and international curriculum adoption (e.g., IB) are seen in nearly all nations. While many systems are centralized (e.g., Egypt, Taiwan), others exhibit significant decentralized or private sector influence (e.g., Ghana, Ireland).
Disparities between affluent and poor students, whether through private tutoring (Egypt, Sri Lanka) or unequal resource distribution (Nigeria, Israel), remain a consistent challenge across the summarized systems.
