# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education system has a rich history dating to antiquity, with early schools like the "Bar Ankh" and the university in Alexandria (c. 300 BCE). Islamic education flourished through institutions such as al-Azhar (est. 970–972 CE) and Dar al-Ilm, evolving over Fatimid, Ayyubid, and Mamluk periods. Muhammad Ali’s 19th-century reforms introduced modern and religious schools and sent students abroad for education, while British occupation (1882–1952) saw educational neglect favoring elites. Post-1952 reforms offered free public education, expanding access but still grappling with quality issues highlighted by low international test rankings and reliance on private tutoring. The system includes a centralized structure with basic (ages 4–14), secondary (general and technical tracks), and higher education (public and private universities). The Al-Azhar system operates in parallel, focusing on religious education. Agricultural education supports practical skills through secondary schools. Challenges remain in teacher quality, infrastructure, equity, and youth unemployment.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education evolved from Islamic pesantren boarding schools through Dutch colonial systems stratified by social class, with limited access for natives and significant gender literacy gaps. Post-independence reforms created a national, secular system promoting Pancasila values, administered by the Ministry of Education and Ministry of Religious Affairs. Education is compulsory for 12 years, divided into early childhood (PAUD), primary, junior and senior secondary, with both academic and vocational tracks. Islamic education operates via pesantren, madrasah (integrating secular and religious curricula), and Islamic schools. Higher education includes universities, institutes, and vocational institutions aligned with the Indonesian National Qualifications Framework. Challenges include teacher shortages and resource disparities, especially in remote areas. 

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education spans over 2300 years, initially based on Buddhist monastic schools (pirivenas), followed by colonial missionary education. Free education was formalized in 1938 with expansion of vernacular medium instruction. The system consists of five stages: primary (5 years), junior secondary (4 years), senior secondary (2 years), collegiate (2 years), culminating in tertiary education. Most schools are government-run, categorized into national, provincial, and specialized (pirivenas for Buddhist clergy). Private, international, Muslim, and madrasas also supplement education. University admission is highly competitive based on GCE Advanced Level exams, with limited private university options. The Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission oversees vocational training. Tuition and exam-related private tutoring are prevalent due to competitiveness. Compulsory leadership training for undergraduates was instituted but later discontinued amid criticism.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia has near-universal literacy and follows a Soviet-influenced, centralized education system with preschool, 12 years of compulsory secondary education split into primary, middle, and high school levels, and higher education institutions including universities and specialized schools. Public schools dominate, with private schools following state guidelines. Higher education comprises many universities offering degrees from bachelor to PhD levels, with Armenia participating in European educational frameworks such as the Bologna Process. Student absenteeism linked to child labor is a concern. Non-formal education centers provide technological and civic skills in rural areas in line with modern demands.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system comprises primary, middle, and high school tiers, divided into four main tracks—state secular, state religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab schools—each reflecting cultural and religious identities. Education is compulsory through 12th grade with matriculation (Bagrut) exams determining higher education and military opportunities. Israel achieves high tertiary education rates (46%) but faces challenges in disparities, especially in the Haredi and Arab sectors. Arab education emphasizes cultural heritage but suffers from underfunding and infrastructure gaps, despite government and nonprofit efforts to improve access. Female students outperform males in matriculation and higher education enrollment, though gender disparities remain in faculty positions and STEM fields. Teacher wages are modest, and the system has experienced strikes over reforms. Israeli universities rank highly worldwide, especially in science and technology disciplines.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education includes mandatory elementary (grades 1–7), lower secondary (8–10), and optional upper secondary school (11–13). The system transitioned from folkeskole to comprehensive grunnskole and videregående skole, with recent reforms emphasizing inclusivity, elective electives, and IT integration. Teachers are categorized by qualification level, requiring university education. Higher education is widespread and state-run, with universities, university colleges, and private institutions offering bachelor to PhD degrees. Special education focuses on inclusion in mainstream schools, supported by psychological services. Grading uses a 1–6 scale in lower levels and a letter-based Bologna system in higher education. The academic year runs from August to June with several national holidays. Private schooling is limited and must align with national curricula.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland provides compulsory education from ages 6 to 16, spanning primary, junior cycle (3 years), optional transition year, and senior cycle (2 years) culminating in Junior and Leaving Certificate exams. Primary education is predominantly under church patronage but shifting toward more multidenominational schools. Secondary education occurs in voluntary secondary, community, comprehensive, or private grind schools. The curriculum includes a wide range of subjects and emphasizes preparation for state exams. Third-level education features universities and colleges with a strong international reputation. Special needs education and disadvantaged school programs (DEIS) aim to improve equity. School days typically run 8:20 am to 3:00–4:00 pm with various holidays; homeschooling is permitted. Ireland ranks highly in international assessments.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden’s system features preschool, nine years of compulsory comprehensive school (grundskola), and elective three-year upper secondary school (gymnasieskola) with preparatory and vocational tracks. National curricula prescribe core subjects with grading from A to F starting in year 6. Upper secondary offers 18 specialized national programs. Higher education aligns with Bologna, offering bachelor, master, and doctoral degrees, alongside higher vocational education. Independent “friskolor” schools, publicly funded but privately run, constitute over 10% of enrollment, sparking debate over privatization and educational equality. National tests determine assessment equivalency. Sweden’s international test scores have fluctuated, partly attributed to immigration and testing irregularities. Special education emphasizes inclusion, supported by government services.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education follows a 9-3-4 system: 6 years primary, 3 years junior secondary, 3 years senior secondary, followed by tertiary education including universities and technical colleges. Primary education includes core subjects plus local languages; secondary education offers academic and vocational tracks. Federal and state governments manage public schools, with private schools providing often better-resourced alternatives. Promotional exams include SSCE and GCE. Nigeria hosts many international and advanced level (A-level) programs like IJMB and JUPEB. Tertiary education is expansive but challenged by teacher shortages, funding issues, illiteracy, and regional disparities, especially affecting female education. Vocational and informal apprenticeship training are important but underdeveloped. Literacy rates remain low at about 69%, with ongoing government efforts to improve general and digital literacy.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education system evolved from bilingual Christian missionary roots to a modern model with kindergarten, primary, and secondary education culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE). Education is compulsory from age 6 to secondary level (Form 3 or age 18). The curriculum emphasizes bilingualism (Cantonese and English), with a gradual push for Putonghua. Public schools are free; international schools are numerous, costly, and provide foreign curricula (British, IB, etc.). University education includes several well-ranked public universities, with English as the medium of instruction. Recent political changes have affected enrollment and curriculum content, emphasizing patriotism and national security. Adult and special education options exist, along with services for immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking students. Class sizes decreased amid demographic changes, and discipline is enforced school-wide.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan’s education encompasses 12 years compulsory basic education (6 years elementary, 3 years junior high, 3 years senior high or vocational school), followed by extensive tertiary options from universities to junior colleges. Education is bilingual with Mandarin as the main medium and English taught from grade 3 upwards. Private schools and cram schools are widespread due to high academic pressure. Junior colleges and institutes of technology offer vocational degrees; universities provide bachelor to doctoral programs, with graduates increasingly numerous but facing employment challenges. Military education is integrated into senior high schooling. Special education, teacher training, and adult education systems are well structured. International student programs and English immersion preschools have grown, reflecting Taiwan’s global educational links.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education system comprises 12 years of basic education (kindergarten through junior high), 3 years of senior high school or vocational education, and tertiary education including universities and polytechnics. Historically influenced by missionary and colonial systems, the government now emphasizes free and compulsory basic education with gender equity initiatives. Challenges include infrastructure deficits, teacher shortages, and low vocational education uptake. Private education is significant, often bridging gaps in public provision. Governance involves multiple agencies coordinating policy and exams. Adult literacy and ICT integration efforts exist but face resource constraints. University admission is competitive, with disparities in access. The government emphasizes universal enrollment, technical skills development, and scholarship systems.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education traces back to ancient Persia with modern reforms initiated in the 19th century. Post-1979 Islamic Revolution led to Islamization of curricula and segregation by sex in schools. The system is centralized under the Ministry of Education, encompassing kindergarten through university, with compulsory education up to lower secondary. High school offers theoretical and vocational tracks, culminating in a National Entrance Examination (Konkur) for higher education admission. Higher education includes many public universities and private Islamic Azad University, with significant female enrollment surpassing males. Teachers receive training via Farhangian University. Foreign languages, especially English, are widely taught, though recent reforms affected English instruction in primary schools. Challenges include teacher shortages, disparities in rural areas, and debates over quality and privatization. Iran embraces distance education and promotes entrepreneurship in universities.

# Education in England
England’s education system includes compulsory schooling from ages 5 to 18, divided into early years, primary (Key Stages 1-2), secondary (Key Stages 3-4), and post-16 education including sixth form and apprenticeships. Schools encompass various types: community, academy, grammar, free, foundation, and independent schools, largely state-funded but with significant private sectors. The National Curriculum defines compulsory subjects. Secondary education culminates in GCSE exams, followed by A-levels or equivalent vocational qualifications. Higher education is highly regarded, with a mixture of universities and colleges offering undergraduate to doctoral degrees. Tuition fees apply, with loans available. Education quality is monitored by Ofsted. Adult education and home schooling are recognized. England’s students perform well internationally, with a broad national qualifications framework.

# Education in Turkey
Turkey’s education system evolved from Ottoman-era madrasas to a unified, secular system after the foundation of the Republic. Education is compulsory for 12 years (4+4+4 system): two stages of primary and one secondary. The Ministry of National Education oversees centralized curricula including religious education, reintroduced in schools alongside secular subjects. Primary and secondary education equip students for vocational or university paths, guided by nationwide exams. Higher education includes numerous public and private universities, with increasing international student presence and a focus on science and technology. Challenges include frequent reforms, curriculum controversies, underfunded public schools, and political influences, including denialism in history textbooks and a strong emphasis on Turkish nationalism. The system includes private, minority, foreign, and international schools.

# Education in France
France's education is centralized and secular, mandated by the Ministry of National Education. It includes preschool (maternelle), five years of elementary school, four years of middle school (collège), and three years of high school (lycée) preparing for the Baccalauréat. The National Curriculum covers core subjects uniformly. The system balances public universities and prestigious grandes écoles, which are highly selective specialist institutions. Private education includes contracted schools following the national curriculum and independent religious schools. Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, awarding Licence, Master, and Doctorate degrees. Vocational training is integrated via IUTs and CFAs. Teacher recruitment requires competitive exams and specialized training. France emphasizes cultural integration through education, with a strong focus on literature and history in curricula. International schools and private institutions complement the public system.
