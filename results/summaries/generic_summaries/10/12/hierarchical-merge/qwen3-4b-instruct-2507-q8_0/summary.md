# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in Egypt, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Armenia, Israel, Norway, Ireland, Sweden, Nigeria, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Ghana, Iran, England, Turkey, and France**

## **High-Performing, Equitable Models**
**Norway** and **Ireland** exemplify inclusive, well-resourced, and equitable education systems. Norway’s three-tiered structure—lower secondary, upper secondary, and higher education—offers flexible pathways, including vocational and language tracks, with a fully integrated special education system supported by psychologists and social workers. Grading is standardized (1–6 scale), and the system prioritizes equity, accessibility, and student well-being. Ireland ranks 2nd globally in reading and 11th in math (PISA 2022), thanks to a holistic curriculum and strong support for students with special educational needs. The *Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools (DEIS)* program targets disadvantaged students, while government investment improves infrastructure and teacher conditions. Ireland also promotes diversity through Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools), Muslim National Schools, and inclusive governance.

## **Academic Rigor and Systemic Challenges**
**Israel** and **Taiwan** maintain high academic performance in science and engineering, supported by elite institutions such as Tel Aviv, Hebrew, and Technion (Israel), and National Taiwan University (Taiwan). However, significant equity gaps persist: Arab students in Israel face systemic disadvantages, including lower funding, higher dropout rates, and marginalized curricula. Taiwan’s high-pressure academic environment is reflected in widespread private tutoring, large class sizes, and historical use of corporal punishment (now banned in theory). The **3+3 system** enables direct university entry, and national exams drive intense competition. **Hong Kong** follows a selective senior secondary model with the HKDSE and IB Diploma, emphasizing English-medium instruction and vocational streams, while providing support for immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking students. Despite strong performance, concerns remain over test integrity, excessive homework, and student well-being.

## **Structural and Governance Challenges**
Several countries face deep-rooted governance fragmentation, underfunding, and socioeconomic disparities. **Egypt**, **Sri Lanka**, **Nigeria**, and **Ghana** struggle with centralized or fragmented systems, low teacher quality, and poor infrastructure. Egypt’s system is marked by low primary quality, high student-to-teacher ratios, rote learning, and widespread private tutoring (used by 58% of families), with religious and secular tracks coexisting but unevenly accessible. Sri Lanka’s multi-ministerial structure leads to inefficiencies; only 16% of A/L graduates gain university admission, fueling intense competition and reliance on overseas education. Nigeria’s *Universal Basic Education (UBE)* mandates nine years of free schooling, but state schools suffer from teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and poor facilities. Private and religious schools offer better access but remain unaffordable. Ghana faces underdeveloped vocational training, inconsistent grading (transitioning from numeric to point-based systems), and limited ICT infrastructure. Teacher training is managed through public Colleges of Education, though donor funding has declined.

**Iran** has a long-standing tradition rooted in Persian scholarship, with major reforms like the 1969–1979 Literacy Corps, which trained over 200,000 volunteers to educate 2.2 million people. Post-revolution, the system became highly centralized and Islamic-oriented, featuring gender segregation, mandatory religious practices, and a curriculum emphasizing Islamic values. A credit-based structure includes a three-year high school and technical-vocational tracks aligned with labor needs. University admission is determined by the national *Konkur* exam. Despite challenges—such as high dropout rates (4 million children in 2014), religious content dominating over secular or scientific topics, and teacher shortages in rural areas—Iran achieves notable gender parity in higher education: women now constitute over 70% of university students and 70% of engineering graduates. The country ranks among global leaders in STEM graduates and maintains high literacy and near-universal primary enrollment.

## **Innovation, Reform, and Equity Initiatives**
Multiple countries are advancing reform through innovation and targeted equity programs. **Armenia** has strengthened academic standards through EU partnerships (Erasmus+, Bologna Process) and established innovation hubs like Tumo and SMART to promote digital literacy and 21st-century skills. It also expands language education, including a new Chinese language school. Egypt launched a Japan-inspired engineering diploma (2025) and the *Higher Education Enhancement Project* to improve quality. **Sweden** has expanded access to upper secondary education and introduced free higher education for EU/EEA citizens. **Ireland** and **Norway** have invested in inclusive policies and infrastructure. **Taiwan** promotes vocational pathways, and **Ghana** launched the *National Functional Literacy Programme (FNLP)* to reduce illiteracy, especially among women and rural populations.

## **Modern State Systems: England, France, and Turkey**
**England** operates a state-funded, compulsory system from age 5 to 16, with post-16 education via apprenticeships, sixth forms, or further education. The curriculum is standardized, including RSE and PSHE, and the academy system—expanded into disadvantaged areas—has improved school quality (88% rated good or outstanding). Ofsted inspections and increased funding (e.g., £7.1 billion in 2022–23) support equity, with tuition capped at £9,535/year. England ranks 7th in the UN Education Index and 13th globally in literacy, math, and science.

**France** maintains a secular, mandatory system from age 6 to 18, with free, compulsory schooling and a structured three-tier higher education system (*Licence*, *Master*, *Doctorat*). The system is highly competitive, especially through **preparatory classes (CPGEs)**—only 5% of applicants gain entry to elite *grandes écoles*. Scientific, business, and humanities CPGEs offer rigorous training, with oral exams (*colle*) central to progression. Teacher recruitment is highly structured, with **Agrégés** (top-tier educators) earning higher salaries and teaching in high schools. Public universities are city-based, while elite *grande écoles* specialize in engineering, business, and humanities. French higher education is accessible to EU/EEA students at low cost (€170–380/year), though international students face high fees. The system enforces **laïcité**, banning religious symbols, sparking ongoing debates on religious freedom.

**Turkey** features a centralized, secular system established in 1924, with compulsory primary education (ages 6–14) and a structured secondary stage offering general, vocational, and technical tracks. A 2012 "4+4+4" reform streamlined the curriculum, with track options introduced in 11th grade. All students receive a standard diploma and must pass a national final exam for university entry. Higher education includes around 167 public and private universities, with public institutions offering affordable access and strong industry partnerships—especially in engineering. The curriculum emphasizes Turkish nationalism, Atatürk’s ideals, and secular democratic values, with religious education introduced from age 4 and covering major world religions. English is widely taught, especially in private schools. However, the system faces criticism for nationalist bias—particularly in history (e.g., downplaying the Armenian genocide)—and poor textbook quality, leading to strong demand for private alternatives. Turkey attracts growing numbers of international students due to low tuition and accessible entry, with notable research in agriculture, health, IT, and defense.

## **Common Trends and Global Shifts**
Across all systems, there is a growing emphasis on vocational training, inclusive education, and digital learning. Hong Kong and Taiwan lead in academic rigor and global university rankings, while Ghana, Nigeria, and Iran face systemic constraints in access, quality, and infrastructure. Despite diverse contexts, all systems are adapting to global trends: promoting student-centered learning, integrating special needs, and expanding access through public-private partnerships. However, persistent challenges—such as achievement gaps, teacher shortages, poor digital infrastructure, rigid curricula, and ideological bias—highlight the need for sustained investment, equitable policy design, and inclusive reform strategies to ensure high-quality, equitable education for all.
