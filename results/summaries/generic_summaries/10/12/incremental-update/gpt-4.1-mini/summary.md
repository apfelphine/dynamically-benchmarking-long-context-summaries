# Updated Working Summary of Education Systems: Egypt, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Armenia, Israel, Norway, Republic of Ireland, Sweden, Nigeria, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Ghana, Iran, England, Turkey, and France

## Education in Egypt  
Egypt’s centralized system provides free compulsory education (ages 4–14) with general and vocational tracks supported by historic institutions like al-Azhar University. Challenges include uneven quality, reliance on private tutoring, corruption, low teacher pay and training, corporal punishment, socioeconomic disparities, incomplete funding, and inadequate vocational training contributing to youth unemployment, despite digital and agricultural advances.

## Education in Indonesia  
Indonesia’s 2013 curriculum integrates sciences, social studies, languages, and religion from preschool through tertiary levels, including Islamic education via pesantren, madrasah, and sekolah islam. Higher education aligns with the National Qualification Framework, offering academic and vocational degrees. High enrollment and survival rates contrast with funding, decentralization, uneven teacher quality, teacher shortages in remote areas, and persistent rote learning.

## Education in Sri Lanka  
Free since 1938 and compulsory to Grade 9, education culminates in G.C.E. exams across government, private (including madrasas), semi-government, and British-curriculum schools, with widespread tutoring. Free state university admission is highly competitive (<16%). Challenges include rigid structures, fierce competition, debates over private universities, and unregulated foreign providers.

## Education in Armenia  
With universal literacy by 1960 and 12 years compulsory education including preschool from age 2, Armenia’s mostly public schools reflect EU-supported reforms aligning with European standards. Innovative centers like Tumo foster 21st-century skills. Higher education specializes in sciences, engineering, medicine, IT, and Armenian studies and participates in Erasmus+.

## Education in Israel  
Compulsory through grade 12, education divides into state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, Arab, and private sectors. Arab and Haredi populations (to be 60% of elementary pupils by 2030) face curriculum and integration challenges. Funded at 7.3% of GDP, education supports socioeconomic mobility and the tech sector. Despite higher female academic achievement, male dominance persists in STEM. Arab schools face underfunding and dropout issues; textbook content remains politically sensitive.

## Education in Norway  
Compulsory elementary (6–12) and lower secondary (13–16) education are supported by optional upper secondary and higher education. Few private schools receive funding. Upper secondary offers general, mercantile, and vocational tracks with apprenticeships. Norwegian 15-year-olds perform well on OECD tests but require better support for slower learners. Educators hold bachelor to master degrees. Special education uses tailored psychological-aligned support.

## Education in the Republic of Ireland  
Preschool to tertiary education features compulsory schooling ages 6–16 with a national curriculum emphasizing holistic development. Most schools are mixed-sex, state-funded under Catholic patronage, with Irish-language and multidenominational options. Secondary ends with the Leaving Certificate (~90% participation). Recent reforms include a €200 million student loan scheme and DEIS expansion. Ireland attracts many international students.

## Education in Sweden  
From preschool through compulsory Grundskola (grades 1–9), upper secondary (Gymnasieskola, 16–19), and higher education, Sweden emphasizes play-based early learning, equality, and diverse pedagogy. National tests and grading accompany diverse upper secondary programs. Independent publicly funded friskolor serve ~10% pupils, sparking debates on inequality and grading inflation. PISA and PIRLS scores have slightly declined amid immigration and testing controversies.

## Education in Nigeria  
Nigeria’s UBE program offers nine years free compulsory education starting at age ~5, with core and elective curricula, technical colleges, and elite federal secondary schools admitting via exams. Key exams are SSCE and GCE; advanced programs allow direct university entry. With 153 universities, vocational studies face teacher shortages and outdated curricula. Female education rises despite gender bias and insecurity. The government aims for 95% digital literacy by 2030 amid infrastructure challenges.

## Education in Hong Kong  
With origins in village and missionary schools, Hong Kong’s bilingual system includes optional kindergarten, 6 years compulsory primary, 3 compulsory junior secondary, 3 selective senior secondary (HKDSE), and tertiary education. The system emphasizes discipline but faces critiques of rote learning and exam focus. Instruction is mainly in English or Cantonese, with increasing Putonghua. Eight UGC-funded universities and multiple school types serve a diverse student body, including immigrants.

## Education in Taiwan  
Taiwan mandates 12 years compulsory schooling with academic and vocational tracks emphasizing Mandarin, math, sciences, English, native languages, social studies, and arts. University entrance is highly competitive, with >95% enrolling in higher education modeled on the U.S. system. Private education includes cram schools and immersion kindergartens amid debates on native teacher employment. Special education serves ages 2–22; nursing education offers multiple paths to licensure. Taiwan attracts many Chinese language learners and international students.

## Education in Ghana  
Ghana’s formal education is free and compulsory (ages 4–15), covering basic to tertiary levels with academic and vocational tracks culminating in WASSCE. Colleges of Education train teachers; National Service is compulsory. Literacy rose significantly but disparities persist. Informal TVET faces challenges; reforms and ICT integration improve access and pedagogy. Financing is multi-source, with decentralization enhancing governance.

## Education in Iran  
Iran’s centrally governed system features optional pre-primary, compulsory elementary and lower-secondary, and non-compulsory upper-secondary education with diverse tracks and national Konkur exams. Public tertiary education is mostly free with notable universities, and private schooling grows despite fees and teacher shortages. Curriculum aligns with Islamic values; English is restricted but French grows. Female university enrollment exceeds 70%. Gifted education and post-pandemic hybrid learning expand. Dropout rates remain high; reforms seek improved vocational training and critical thinking amidst political-social controls in schools.

## Education in England  
Compulsory education spans ages 5–16 with Foundation Stage and four Key Stages emphasizing a National Curriculum including RSE and PSHE. Post-16 options include sixth forms, further education, apprenticeships, A-levels, BTEC, NVQs, and T Levels. Higher education ranges from undergraduate to doctoral levels, regulated and financially supported, with prestigious institutions and alignment to European qualifications frameworks. England performs well on PISA and the Education Index. City academies have boosted school quality; substantial funding increases support SEND students.

## Education in Turkey  
Turkey’s centralized system expanded compulsory education to 12 years. The 2017 curriculum removed evolution and emphasized Islamic concepts amid political shifts. Pre-primary is optional (3–6); compulsory primary and secondary (up to 17) include general, vocational, and religious (Imam Hatip) schools. University admission requires national exams. Universities number around 820, with English preparatory classes common. Nationalist biases and curriculum changes affect minority language education negatively despite legal guarantees. Preparatory dershane schools were replaced by academic high schools by 2015.

## Education in France  
France mandates free, secular, compulsory education (ages 3–16) managed by the Ministry of National Education and Youth with a standardized national curriculum emphasizing national identity and immigrant integration. Its stages include preschool (from age 3), elementary, middle school, and lycée preparing for the baccalauréat and vocational certificates. Private schools under contract follow state curricula; others are independent. 

Higher education follows the Bologna Process with Licence (Bachelor), Master, and Doctorat degrees aligned with ECTS credits. Enrollment reached 1.65 million (2021). Public universities offer broad accessibility with low fees for EU/EEA students and higher for non-EU; elite grandes écoles provide highly selective, prestigious training in sciences, engineering, humanities, business, and politics (e.g., École Polytechnique, HEC Paris, Sciences Po). Engineering and private business schools may have high tuition fees. Private higher education includes not-for-profit "Facultés Libres" (e.g., Catholic universities with EESPIG status) and mainly Grandes Écoles, some for-profit, some under state contracts. 

Preparatory classes (CPGEs) are two-year intensive courses preparing ~5% of students for Grandes Écoles entrance exams, divided into scientific, business, and humanities streams with rigorous oral exams (colles). Teacher recruitment is tiered: INSPE graduates for primary/kindergarten, CAPES-certified for middle schools, and agrégés (passing the Agrégation) for high schools with higher pay.

University teaching staff include:
- **Teachers-researchers** with doctorates conducting research and teaching, advancing via HDR theses; earning €2,300–€8,800 monthly.
- **Secondary school teachers assigned to universities** (PRAG, PRCE) focusing on teaching, earning €1,400–€3,900 monthly.
- **CPGE teachers**, mostly agrégés, teaching fewer hours but earning €2,000–€7,500 monthly with extras.

Since 2018, experimental public-private partnerships integrate private colleges with EESPIG status into public universities, raising concerns about privatization and tuition increases.

French secularism (laïcité) strictly separates religion from public school instruction, except in Alsace-Moselle due to the Concordat. A 2004 law bans conspicuous religious symbols in schools, upholding neutrality but sparking opposition.

France has a population of 67 million, with a declining youth proportion (under 25 years) from 40% in the 1970s to 32% now. About 18 million students (25% of the population) are in the education system, including over 2.9 million in higher education. Incidents of school violence are low, with few schools classified as seriously or somewhat violent.
