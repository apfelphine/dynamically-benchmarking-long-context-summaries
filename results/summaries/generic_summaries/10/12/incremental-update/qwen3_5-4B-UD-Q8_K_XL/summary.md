### Comparative Overview of Education Systems: Sweden, Nigeria, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Ghana, Iran, England, Turkey, and France

**Sweden, Nigeria, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Ghana, Iran, England, Turkey, and France: Systemic Structures and Challenges**
The analysis covers nine distinct educational systems, highlighting their unique historical foundations, structural frameworks, and contemporary challenges.
*   **Sweden:** Operates a hierarchical system anchored by the *grundskola*, offering free higher education to citizens but facing issues with inequality in upper secondary access, physical violence, and security concerns.
*   **Nigeria:** Driven by the 1999 Universal Basic Education (UBE) program, the system features a diverse tertiary landscape (Federal, State, Private) and faces hurdles like teacher shortages, political instability, and regional disparities in literacy and infrastructure.
*   **Hong Kong:** Evolved from missionary roots to a system emphasizing project-based learning and patriotism post-1997. However, the National Security Law has triggered a significant exodus of students and educators, while the culture remains heavily influenced by cram schools and high-stakes exams.
*   **Taiwan:** Expanded to a 12-year system with a strong emphasis on academic achievement, characterized by intense senior high preparation, a mix of public and private institutions, and a vibrant but competitive sector of cram schools.
*   **Ghana:** Transitioned from a decentralized colonial model to a centralized system under the 1987 Education Act. While literacy has improved, the Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) sector struggles with low prestige and poor infrastructure, and equity gaps persist between urban and rural areas.
*   **Iran:** Features a centralized, Islamized system with high female enrollment but faces severe teacher shortages, high dropout rates, and ideological constraints on curricula (e.g., bans on English in primary schools, mandatory prayers).
*   **England:** Ranks well globally in literacy and science, with a diverse school landscape including state, academy, and independent sectors. The system emphasizes a broad National Curriculum and offers extensive post-16 pathways, though disparities between disadvantaged and advantaged students remain a focus.
*   **Turkey:** Established with secular principles, the system underwent major reforms (e.g., the 4+4+4 structure) but faces controversies over curriculum content (e.g., evolution theory, history textbooks) and political influence on education standards.
*   **France:** Built on the Napoleonic and Jules Ferry legacy of secular (*laïque*) public education. The system is highly structured from preschool to the *baccalauréat*, with a distinct higher education sector dominated by elite *Grande Écoles* and intense preparatory classes (CPGE).

**Deep Dive: France's Higher Education and Social Context**
France's educational model is defined by its strict secularism (*Laïcité*) and a complex higher education hierarchy.
*   **Higher Education & Recruitment:** University staff are categorized into researchers (*Maître de conférences*, *Professeurs*), secondary teachers assigned to universities, and highly qualified CPGE teachers. Salaries vary significantly, with most researchers earning below €4,000 net, while CPGE teachers command higher wages due to their specialized, intensive role.
*   **Secularism and Religion:** Governed by *Laïcité*, public schools generally prohibit religious symbols (with a 2004 ban on conspicuous symbols), aiming to foster tolerance, though exceptions exist in Alsace and Moselle.
*   **Demographics and Statistics:** With a population of 67 million, the system enrolls approximately 18 million students (25% of the population). The society is aging, with the youth population (under 25) declining from 40% in the 1970s to under 32%. Despite this, over 2.9 million students pursue higher education. The system has historically maintained a focus on socialization and national identity, with relatively low violence in state schools compared to other regions.
