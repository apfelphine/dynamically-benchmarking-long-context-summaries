## Comparative Summary of Education Systems

### Egypt
Egypt’s centralized education faces resource disparities, overcrowding, staff shortages, corruption, outdated infrastructure, and rote learning traditions. Rankings and graduate employment outcomes remain low, though distance learning and university standards have improved.

### Indonesia
Indonesia offers secular and religious education tracks, compulsory high school, and early privatized childhood education. Despite a KKNI framework, higher education enrollment is low.

### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka offers free and compulsory education up to age 14, with fierce competition for university places and reliance on tutoring. Significant vocational, state, open, and overseas university options exist.

### Armenia
Armenia’s European-style K–12 system boasts high enrollment, but dropouts risk persists due to child labor and inconsistent quality. Innovations include centers like Tumo and SMART.

### Israel
Israel has secular, Haredi, and Arab schools, with notable achievement gaps, especially in Arab-Christian communities. Women are prominent in most university fields, except STEM, and increasingly study abroad. Teacher pay is rising.

### Norway
Norway mandates upper secondary education with academic/vocational tracks and obligatory IT skills. Higher education meets NOKUT standards and requires high teacher qualifications.

### Republic of Ireland
Ireland provides co-educational, religion-influenced education culminating in the Leaving Certificate. Marginalized group support, admissions issues, and compliance with NFQ/EU standards are priorities, attracting international students.

### Sweden
Sweden offers free preschool, compulsory basic education, university-prep upper secondary, support for boys and immigrant students, and tuition-free higher education for Swedish/EU citizens. Key issues include grade inflation, homeschooling restrictions, specialized adult/Sámi education, and immigration.

### Nigeria
Nigeria’s 6-3-3 system enables competitive tertiary access but vocational sectors are underfunded. Efforts focus on digital/AI learning to address inequality and improve literacy rates.

### Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s system features voluntary kindergarten, compulsory primary/junior secondary, selective senior secondary/tertiary tracks, and 100+ international schools. Major challenges include declining enrollment, staff attrition, strict exam culture, discipline issues, political tensions, and AI adoption in tertiary education.

### Taiwan
Taiwan’s 12-year system includes academic/vocational tracks, strong engineering/medical fields, and specialty options. English-immersion and private vocational schools proliferate, attracting mainland Chinese students.

### Ghana
Ghana delivers free, compulsory 12-year education, achieving high enrollment and gender parity. Instruction is in English, ICT is promoted despite resource constraints, and TVET completion is low. A National Service year follows graduation.

### Iran
Iran’s centralized, Islamized, state-funded system improves access for women, modernizes education, expands vocational/professional pathways, and encourages research and entrepreneurship. Challenges include religious requirements and urban-rural disparities.

### England
England’s highly ranked education system features diverse school types, compulsory schooling from age 3–18, strong special needs support, varied post-16 options, and prestigious higher education with substantial international enrollment and high fees.

### Turkey
Turkey’s centralized, secular structure includes mandatory primary/secondary, general, religious, and vocational tracks, periodic reforms, minority language limitations, and expanding higher education autonomy, international recruitment, and technical accreditation.

### France

France’s centrally regulated, secular education emphasizes national identity through French literature and history, progressing through preschool, lycée, and culminating in the baccalauréat or vocational qualifications. Most private schools follow state curriculum, some provide religious instruction; over 100 international schools and the AEFE network serve additional needs. Universities are city-based, public, and universities and research institutes like CNRS drive research. Highly selective Grandes Écoles feature preparatory classes (CPGEs). Private colleges may partner with public institutions for accreditation.

Teacher recruitment requires advanced degrees and competitive exams: INSPEs train primary teachers; CAPES or Agrégation recruit secondary teachers, with agrégés earning higher pay. CPGE teachers have distinct roles and pay scales. At universities, teachers are "teachers-researchers" who must hold doctorates and complete an HDR thesis to become professors, or secondary school teachers (PRAG/PRCE) assigned to universities, who teach more but do not conduct research; salaries range between €3,760–€3,900/month. In 2021, over 1.65 million studied in higher education and over 2.9 million overall. France’s education expanded greatly in the 1800s and continues to uphold secularism, with religious symbols banned in public institutions (except Alsace-Moselle). School violence is reported as rare. France’s population stands at 67 million with a declining youth proportion; about 18 million are enrolled in education.

This summary outlines key characteristics, challenges, and recent reforms across diverse national education systems, focusing on structure, enrollment, prominent issues, and teacher education, with France highlighted as a model of centralized, secular, and highly selective education.
