### Education in Sri Lanka: Structure, History, and Reforms

Rooted in a 2,300-year Buddhist tradition and standardized during the British colonial era (Colebrooke Commission, 1836), Sri Lanka's education system is administered by three ministries and progresses through five stages: compulsory primary (Grades 1–5), junior secondary (Grades 6–9), senior secondary culminating in the G.C.E. Ordinary Level (O/L) exam, collegiate years (Grades 12–13) preparing for the university entrance G.C.E. Advanced Level (A/L), and tertiary education. The landscape encompasses government, private, and international institutions, alongside a diverse array of religious schools (Buddhist, Hindu, Christian, Muslim, Catholic) established post-independence to preserve cultural values, often utilizing English as a medium of instruction.

**Institutional Structure and Funding**
Government schools are bifurcated into National Schools—historically elite and directly funded by the Ministry with superior facilities—and Provincial Schools, which constitute the majority but frequently suffer from resource shortages due to local council funding. Non-government options include private schools operating in local languages (Sinhala, Tamil, English) on assisted or autonomous fee-levying models, and international schools serving expatriates with British or Cambridge curricula. The public sector remains dominant, with limited private alternatives, though a lucrative but controversial tuition industry exists in major cities to assist students in competing for high-stakes exams.

**Higher and Vocational Education**
Higher education originated in the 19th century, evolving from the Ceylon Medical School to the University of Ceylon (1942) and the University of Sri Lanka (1972). The 1978 Universities Act established state universities under direct government control; while reforms post-1999 allowed them to grant undergraduate degrees, they remain free but extremely competitive, admitting fewer than 16% of qualifiers via rank-based Z Scores, a process that often favors urban students. Despite state university dominance, graduates frequently face unemployment or "brain drain" abroad due to perceived rigidity and a lack of practical exposure. Vocational education is managed by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, offering structured qualifications (NVQSL) and non-formal courses for adults and dropouts in fields like hospitality and skills training.

**Reforms, Challenges, and Controversies**
Recent initiatives include shifting to national languages as the primary medium of instruction, the nationalization of private schools, and the establishment of the Mahapola Fund in the 1980s to support higher education. However, the system faces significant criticism for being overly rigid and competitive. Notable controversies include the 2011–2015 mandatory compulsory leadership training for undergraduates at military bases, which was terminated after substantial public and political backlash. While efforts to establish private universities have been blocked by opposition groups, some foreign-affiliated private institutes continue to operate.
