# Summary of Education Systems in England, France, Ghana, Turkey, Hong Kong, Iran, Sweden, Taiwan, Norway, Nigeria, Armenia, Ireland, Indonesia, Israel, Egypt, and Sri Lanka

## England  
Education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18, following a National Curriculum emphasizing Relationship and Sex Education and PSHE. State schools predominate, with independent and homeschooling options available. Post-16 pathways include academic and vocational options, and higher education fees are regulated. City academies and equitable funding support quality and special needs provision. England ranks strongly internationally (PISA 13th; UK 7th UN Education Index).

## France  
Free, secular education spans ages 3 or 6 to 18, based on Jules Ferry laws emphasizing laïcité and core national subjects. The system culminates in the baccalauréat, with private schools following national or independent curricula. Grandes écoles offer selective higher education at low tuition. Religious symbols are banned under laïcité except in Alsace-Moselle; nationwide, 18 million students are enrolled.

## Ghana  
Nearly 20 years of English-medium education cover kindergarten to tertiary levels. Free basic education, decentralized in administration, has improved literacy (~79% in 2018) and near gender parity. Vocational education remains underdeveloped. The Ministry of Education and Ghana Education Service supervise the sector; teacher training occurs at Colleges of Education. Adult literacy is ~71.5%, bolstered by ICT and scholarships amid equity challenges.

## Turkey  
Twelve years of secular education include general, vocational, technical, and Imam Hatip religious tracks. The 2017 curriculum removed evolution theory and increased Islamic content while retaining compulsory religious education alongside ethics classes. English begins in grade 2; minority languages are optional. Secondary education ends with national or IB exams. Türkiye Scholarships support international students. The system faces curriculum controversies and marginalized minority languages.

## Hong Kong  
Bilingual education evolved from British colonial times to post-1997 governance, with schooling from kindergarten to senior secondary ending in HKDSE or IBDP exams. Vocational training is provided by the Vocational Training Council. About 20–30% of schools teach in English, facilitating university entry. Curriculum reforms encourage active learning and national security education, removing sensitive topics to align with Mainland China. Eight UGC-funded universities rank internationally. Challenges include exam pressure, rote learning, and contested history teaching.

## Iran  
Rooted in ancient traditions, Iran’s education became Islamized after 1979, with gender segregation and compulsory religious practice. It includes optional pre-primary, compulsory elementary and lower secondary, and multiple upper secondary academic and vocational tracks. Higher education is free via national exams; over 70% of university students were women in 2015. Reforms emphasize Islamic values, vocational expansion, and entrepreneurial skills. Persian is official; Arabic, English, and French are secondary languages.

## Sweden  
Inclusive education starts from preschool (age one), with nine years of compulsory comprehensive schooling across 16 subjects. Grading begins in grade 6, influencing upper secondary admission, which features academic and vocational tracks. Higher education follows the Bologna Process; tuition is free for domestic/EU/EEA students. About 10% attend independent “friskolor,” which face scrutiny. International assessments reflect policy and demographic influences.

## Taiwan  
Twelve years of compulsory education comprise kindergarten, elementary, junior high, and senior high. Mandarin is the sole instructional language; corporal punishment is banned though discipline remains strict. Curriculum focuses on core academics and university exam preparation. About 95% proceed to senior high or vocational schools, including military-style academies. Higher education resembles American models. Private cram schools emphasize English and math. Taiwan hosts many mainland Chinese language learners.

## Norway  
Schooling is compulsory from ages 6 to 16, divided into elementary, lower secondary, and upper secondary levels. The 2006 "Kunnskapsløftet" reform integrated IT and elective courses. Vocational education combines school and apprenticeships. University admission depends on upper secondary grades. Inclusive special education is the norm. Independent schools receive public funding and offer alternative curricula. The school year runs August–June.

## Nigeria  
The "6-3-3-4" system includes free compulsory primary education and diverse federal, state, and private secondary schools. Core secondary subjects include mathematics, English, and civic education. The Senior Secondary Certificate Examination is required for university entry; alternatives exist. Vocational education has expanded to address unemployment. Challenges persist from gender bias, poverty, insecurity, and regional disparities. Literacy stands at ~69% (2022); goals target 95% digital literacy by 2030.

## Armenia  
Benefiting from Soviet legacy, Armenia sustains near-universal literacy with emphasis on Armenian language and culture alongside Russian, English, and Chinese. Free compulsory K-12 education offers general and specialized tracks, with state exams for university and vocational admission. Higher education aligns with Bologna Process and Erasmus+, overseen by a national quality agency. Challenges include absenteeism and dropouts linked to child labor. Innovative centers like Tumo support youth digital skills development.

## Republic of Ireland  
Schooling is compulsory from ages 6 to 16 or three secondary years, spanning preschool, 8 years of primary, and secondary education. Most primary schools (~88%) are church-controlled (mainly Roman Catholic), impacting admissions. Secondary education includes Junior Cycle, optional Transition Year, and Senior Cycle ending in the Leaving Certificate. Ireland ranks in the global education top 20, legislates special needs education, and implements DEIS for disadvantaged students. The academic year is August–June.

## Indonesia  
Education is shaped by Islamic, Dutch, and Japanese legacies, mandating 12 years with public secular schools and private religious institutions (pesantren and madrassas). Islamic education parallels general education, integrating Quranic and secular studies. Curriculum emphasizes secular knowledge, national ideology, and Indonesian language. Secondary tracks include academic, vocational, and domestic science. Teacher qualifications and resources have improved, though disparities persist. Higher education follows a national framework with diverse institutions.

## Israel  
Compulsory education spans kindergarten through 12th grade, divided into state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab systems. Haredi schools often lack secular subjects, affecting matriculation and employment. Jewish communities emphasize higher education in sciences and technology; about 61.5% earn matriculation certificates. Arab schools face funding and resource disparities despite affirmative actions. Females outperform males in dropout and matriculation rates. Teacher strikes over pay and reforms are frequent. Textbooks spark controversy, particularly regarding Arab portrayals. Universities hold high regional and global rankings.

## Egypt  
Ancient educational roots encompass al-Azhar and colonial influences. Post-independence reforms guarantee free public education, with compulsory basic schooling (ages 4–14), national exams, and distinct secondary academic and technical tracks including Applied Technology Schools aligned with labor market needs. Agricultural education cooperates with the Ministry of Agriculture. Higher education is centralized with limited autonomy; university enrollment is about 30%, with only half graduating. Private international schools offer foreign curricula. The parallel Al-Azhar system focuses on religious education. COVID-19 accelerated distance learning adoption.

Despite reforms, public education quality is low and uneven, driving widespread private tutoring (58% of families). Issues include low teacher salaries, poor qualifications, corruption, exam leakage, corporal punishment, and regional disparities. Egypt ranked lowest globally in primary education quality (2013 WEF). Public schools often levy fees despite nominal free education, increasing household expenses. Youth unemployment, especially among graduates (36.1% overall; 53.2% female), remains high.

## Sri Lanka  
Education reflects a 2300-year history rooted in Buddhist temple schools and colonial missionary institutions. Free education since 1938 emphasizes national languages, religious instruction, and teacher protections, overseen by multiple ministries and the National Institute of Education to enhance quality and equity. The system includes primary (Grades 1–5), junior secondary (6–9), senior secondary (10–11), collegiate (12–13), and tertiary levels, with compulsory schooling up to Grade 9 (age 14). Key exams—the Scholarship exam (end primary), G.C.E. Ordinary Level (Grade 11), and Advanced Level (Grade 13)—serve as university entrance gateways.

Over 10,000 government schools educate 4.2 million students and are divided into elite centrally funded National Schools with colonial origins; Provincial Schools with fewer resources; and Buddhist monastic Pirivenas for priestly and general education. Non-government options include longstanding private schools following the national curriculum and international schools serving expatriates and affluent locals.

Religious communities maintain educational contributions: 205 registered madrasas provide Islamic education along with 749 Muslim schools; Hindu Colleges founded by the Hindu Educational Society; historic Catholic and Methodist schools; and secular multi-religious institutions. Semi-government schools operate through government-private partnerships offering government support (e.g., Zahira College, Wesley College).

Private tuition is widespread due to competitive exams (Year 5, GCE O/L & A/L), especially in urban centers, with online platforms enhancing access. State undergraduate education is free but highly competitive; fewer than 16% of qualified students gain university admission, which favors district representation. Many students pursue education abroad or professional/vocational pathways. Fifteen state universities and some degree-granting institutes exist, with limited private universities due to political resistance. Private providers offer local, foreign, or professional qualifications with degree classifications from certificates to doctorates.

Vocational education and training are managed by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, with the National Vocational Qualifications Sri Lanka (NVQSL) providing structured Levels 1–7 certifications. Programs operate in technical colleges, universities, and community settings targeting dropouts and adults.

The education system faces criticism for being overly competitive and rigid, with controversies over private university opposition and compulsory military-style leadership training for undergraduates (2011–2015), which was later abolished.

---

This overview demonstrates extensive global diversity in education influenced by cultural, historical, religious, and political factors. It highlights varying compulsory schooling durations, academic and vocational tracks, multilingual and religious pluralism, and ongoing challenges in equity, quality, curriculum content, and modernization. Sri Lanka exemplifies a historically rich, multi-religious educational landscape integrated with contemporary universal access and tiered progression, while Egypt typifies enduring traditional systems facing significant socioeconomic and quality disparities.
