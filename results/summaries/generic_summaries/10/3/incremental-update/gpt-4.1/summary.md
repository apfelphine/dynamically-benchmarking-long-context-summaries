## Comparative Overview of National Education Systems

Education systems in England, France, Ghana, Turkey, Hong Kong, Iran, Sweden, Taiwan, Norway, Nigeria, Armenia, Ireland, Indonesia, Israel, Egypt, and Sri Lanka reflect diverse historical, cultural, and administrative contexts, while addressing issues of modernization, equity, access, and quality.

### England
Historically religious roots inform state-funded, compulsory education (ages 5–18), which accommodates academic, vocational, technical, SEN, home, and adult education, with capped university fees.

### France
A centralized, secular system (compulsory ages 6–12) offers academic and vocational tracks, strongly promotes national identity, boasts low violence, and subsidizes higher education.

### Ghana
Ministry-led, twelve-year compulsory education features national exams, gender equity policies, and some social service requirements, yet faces challenges in infrastructure and vocational pathways.

### Turkey
Twelve years of compulsory, secular education (4+4+4) includes academic/vocational tracks impacted by Islamic/nationalist influences. Exams determine progression; minority access is limited.

### Hong Kong
Free, compulsory education (ages 6–18) is provided mainly in Cantonese, with English/international options. Reforms support project-based learning, though competitive university entry and politicized curricula remain concerns.

### Iran
Centralized, religious, and gender-segregated schooling prioritizes Persian, Arabic, Islamic studies, and STEM, but faces disparities, privatization, and rigorous exams. Female STEM participation is high.

### Sweden
A nine-year compulsory ‘grundskola’ with academic/vocational secondary options features curriculum reforms and equity challenges, especially for boys and immigrants, with independent schools influencing disparities.

### Taiwan
Mandated twelve-year, exam-focused education in Mandarin incorporates English and local languages, increased vocational/special education, and strong engineering emphasis in higher education.

### Norway
Offers progressive, ten-year compulsory schooling with academic/vocational upper secondary, minimal private provision, Bologna-aligned universities, and emphasis on academic and social skills.

### Nigeria
Federal/state/private 6-3-3-4 system faces literacy, disparities, infrastructure, poverty, and security challenges; reforms address vocational training, digital literacy, female education, and adult learning.

### Armenia
K-12 system with high literacy, free compulsory education, focus on Armenian culture and multilingualism, European alignment, STEM and language emphasis, and exam-based university entry.

### Republic of Ireland
Eight-year primary/secondary education (ages 4–12), historically Catholic, now secular, with Junior/Senior Cycles and an optional Transition Year. Features high completion rates, special needs support, and a national qualifications framework.

### Indonesia
Compulsory twelve-year secular and Islamic education (in Indonesian) relies on rote learning and covers academic, vocational, and professional higher education, but suffers from fees, disparities, and resource gaps.

### Israel
Pluralistic tracks serve state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, Arab, and private groups; Arab schools face disparities, contested curricula, and gender gaps. Technology and the bagrut exam are central.

### Egypt
Centralized, compulsory education with free basic schooling, academic/vocational tracks, and specialized or international higher education. The sector faces quality, inequality, corruption, and exam/security challenges.

### Sri Lanka
Education is delivered through government, semi-government, and non-government schools, plus specialized Muslim institutions. The system encompasses colonial-era elite schools, multi-religious institutions, and a five-stage structure (primary through tertiary), with compulsory schooling to age 14. Over 10,000 government schools include national, provincial, and Buddhist pirivenas. Expansion of private and international schools has led to varied curricula and quality.

Intense national exam competition has driven a robust private tuition market, increasingly regulated and supplemented online. Higher education is free but highly competitive—under 16% of qualified students are admitted to state universities (15 in total), with admission based on district-level Advanced Level exam rankings. Many students opt for overseas study, Open University, or vocational/professional institutions, as few private universities exist (amid political resistance). Vocational education is coordinated by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission and includes formal technical colleges, private providers, and non-formal programs for adults and drop-outs. Criticisms of the system focus on its rigidity and competitiveness. Leadership training for undergraduates was briefly required (2011–2015) before being phased out due to public dissent.

This synthesis highlights key structures, curriculum models, governance, reforms, examination regimes, funding, minority and adult education, international alignment, modernization initiatives, persistent disparities, and responses to current challenges across national systems.
