## Global Education Systems Overview

**System Comparisons & Governance:**
Global education systems show wide diversity in governance (centralized/decentralized), curriculum (secular/religious), and tertiary structure, with a general trend toward digital literacy. Common challenges across these systems include poor teacher quality, insufficient compensation, and union resistance to change.

**Regional System Deep Dives:**

*   **Sri Lanka:** The system is highly diverse, influenced by historical and religious factors, featuring five years of primary, four years of junior secondary, and two years of senior secondary (G.C.E O/Ls), with compulsory education up to Grade 9. Education is delivered through Government (National, Provincial, Pirivenas), Non-Government Private, and international schools, with significant religious presence (Catholic, Muslim via 205 madrasas, Buddhist). While tertiary education is offered by state universities (free but highly competitive based on GCE Advanced Level scores), limited admission drives many qualified students abroad. Vocational Education and Training (VET) is managed by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, offering structured and non-formal programs. Critics note the system is overly competitive and rigid, leading to high graduate unemployment and unregulated private providers.
*   **Egypt:** A centralized system grappling with low quality, embezzlement, and exam leakage, where wealth strongly dictates educational outcomes, fueling a large private tutoring sector amid ongoing reforms.
*   **Israel:** Features a multi-tracked K-12 system (state-secular, religious, *Haredi*, Arab) crucial to its tech sector, culminating in mandatory *bagrut* exams, though Arab sector inequalities persist.
*   **Indonesia:** Educates over 50 million students across twelve years of compulsory education, balancing secular state schools with religious institutions (*pesantren*, *madrasah*) while addressing equity concerns.
*   **Ireland:** Structured with primary (4-12), junior secondary, optional Transition Year (TY), and senior cycle (Leaving Certificate), supported by the DEIS program for disadvantaged schools.
*   **Armenia:** Provides 12 years of free, compulsory secondary education aligned with European standards.
*   **Nigeria:** Requires nine years of free schooling under the UBE but struggles with low literacy, poor rural infrastructure, funding deficits, and barriers for female students.
*   **Taiwan:** Implements a 12-year Mandarin curriculum, introducing English in Grade 3 and allowing senior high specialization in academics or skills.
*   **Norway:** Has actively promoted inclusive education since the 1970s, offering compulsory schooling through elementary and lower secondary, with choices between general or vocational upper secondary paths.
*   **Sweden:** Offers free education from age three to nine (*grundskola*) and provides free tertiary education to EU/EEA/Swiss citizens via the Bologna Process since 2011.
*   **Iran:** Maintains a centralized, Islamized system mandating daily prayer and featuring segregated schooling, yet offering free education up to the Doctoral level.
*   **Hong Kong:** Provides free primary and secondary education until age 18, predominantly in English, offering pathways like the HKDSE or IBDP.
*   **English:** Mandatory schooling runs from age 5 to 18, governed by the National Curriculum leading to A-levels or BTECs, overseen by the OfS/QAA.
*   **French:** Operates a secular public system based on *laïcité* and "common culture," with progression from Licence to Master and Doctorat via the *baccalauréat*.
*   **Ghana:** Uses a decentralized 12-year basic education framework but faces obstacles in improving TVET and tertiary access, requiring national service for graduates.
*   **Turkish:** Implements a centralized, secular "4+4+4" structure integrating general and vocational tracks while emphasizing nationalism and English proficiency.
*   **International Context:** The diverse developmental trajectories, such as Indonesia's blend of religious and colonial legacies, highlight the complexity of worldwide educational landscapes.
