# Summary of Education Systems Worldwide

---

## Education in England
Education in England evolved from medieval religious schooling to a comprehensive system with compulsory education from ages 5 to 18. It features a national curriculum across primary and secondary stages, state-funded schools including academies, free schools, grammar schools, and private schools. Post-16 education offers various pathways such as sixth form colleges, apprenticeships, and further education. Higher education includes universities with tuition fees capped at around £9,535 per year (from 2024-25). Quality assurance is ensured through Ofsted inspections and national qualifications frameworks align with European standards.

---

## Education in France
French education, strongly shaped by secularism since the 19th century, is centrally governed by the Ministry of National Education with standardized national curricula. It comprises preschool, elementary, middle (collège), and high school (lycée), culminating in the baccalauréat exams. Higher education is divided between universities and elite grandes écoles, with structured preparatory classes (CPGEs) preparing students for entrance exams. Tuition fees are low due to state funding, with private institutions supplementing. Recent reforms emphasize international programs and maintaining high educational standards.

---

## Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education system includes 12 years of compulsory basic education (kindergarten to junior high), followed by secondary education (senior high school or vocational paths), and tertiary institutions including universities and polytechnics. The system experienced expansions and reforms post-independence but faces challenges like teacher shortages and uneven vocational education quality. Private sector participation is significant in both basic and tertiary education. The government emphasizes equity, digital literacy, and public-private partnerships to improve access and outcomes.

---

## Education in Turkey
Turkey provides compulsory 12-year education divided into pre-primary, primary (8 years), and secondary education, which includes general and vocational schools. Higher education consists of public and private universities offering a variety of programs with increasing autonomy. Education reforms have included curriculum changes emphasizing secularism, nationalism, and recently added religious content. Private and minority schools exist alongside public ones. The government actively supports international student enrollment and research development.

---

## Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education system is bilingual (English and Chinese) with 12 years of compulsory education consisting of kindergarten, primary, and secondary levels. Public and aided schools dominate, supplemented by Direct Subsidy Scheme and private international schools offering diverse curricula (UK, IB, American). The territory’s universities are well-ranked globally, with English as the primary medium of instruction. Recent political changes have influenced curriculum content and teacher policies. Adult education, special education, and services for non-Cantonese speakers are also emphasized.

---

## Education in Iran
Iran’s education system dates back to ancient times, with modern reforms after the 1979 Islamic Revolution emphasizing Islamic values and gender-segregated education. It offers pre-primary to university education, including specialized schools for gifted students (NODET). Higher education includes a large university network with growing female enrollment exceeding males. Tuition is mostly state-funded with some private schools. Challenges include teacher shortages and educational inequality. Instruction includes Persian as the national language, with foreign languages such as Arabic, English, and French taught.

---

## Education in Sweden
Sweden has a nine-year compulsory comprehensive school followed by elective three-year upper secondary education (gymnasieskola). Education includes preschool, primary, secondary, and higher education with broad vocational and preparatory programs. Independent schools receive public funding but cannot charge fees. Grading starts in year 6, supported by national tests. Higher education is structured within the Bologna framework offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Swedish universities emphasize inclusivity, grading fairness, and provide student financial aid.

---

## Education in Taiwan
Taiwan offers 12 years compulsory education, spanning kindergarten, elementary, junior and senior high school, followed by higher education including universities and vocational institutions. Mandarin is the primary language of instruction; English is taught from grade 3 onward. University admissions rely on national exams or recommendations. Vocational education is promoted through junior colleges and universities of science and technology. Private schools and cram schools are widespread. Teacher in-service training and special education services exist to support system improvements.

---

## Education in Norway
Norway mandates 10 years of compulsory education divided into primary (grades 1–7) and lower secondary (grades 8–10). Upper secondary education (grades 11–13) is elective with vocational and general paths. Higher education includes universities and university colleges with diverse fields. Grading systems vary but follow criteria-referenced models. Education emphasizes inclusivity, with special education integrated into mainstream schools. National curricula and exams support quality assurance, and government funding sustains mostly public education with limited private schools.

---

## Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system follows a 9-year Universal Basic Education (6 years primary, 3 years junior secondary), followed by senior secondary and tertiary education. State, federal, private, and religious schools exist with disparities in quality and access. Secondary education culminates in the West African Examinations Council (WAEC) SSCE and GCE exams. Vocational education remains underdeveloped, with informal apprenticeships common. Female education faces challenges including societal barriers and conflict. Polytechnic and university systems are expanding with federal support, alongside growing private higher education. Literacy improvement and digital skills development are national priorities.

---

## Education in Armenia
Armenia has free and compulsory 12-year education structured into primary, middle, and high school, with public and private schools following national curricula. Higher education includes numerous universities and specialized institutions adhering to the Bologna Process. Private schools provide diverse curricula but can lead to disparities in quality. Armenian education emphasizes national identity, with increasing language programs (English, Russian, Armenian) and expanding technology-based learning centers. Student absenteeism and dropout rates have risen, linked to socioeconomic factors.

---

## Education in Ireland
Ireland provides compulsory education from ages 6 to 16 (or three years of second-level education). The structure includes preschool, primary (eight years), junior cycle (3 years), optional Transition Year, and senior cycle (2 years) leading to Junior and Leaving Certificate exams. Most schools are run by religious bodies, with increasing numbers of multidenominational and gaelscoileanna (Irish-language schools). Higher education is internationally recognized. Special needs education and programs addressing educational disadvantage exist. Private tutoring is prevalent due to exam competitiveness.

---

## Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education system is 12 years compulsory education covering pre-school, primary, junior and senior secondary education, alongside vocational and religious schools (pesantren, madrasah). The system is decentralized with a national curriculum influenced by secular and religious values. Higher education includes universities and polytechnics with academic and vocational tracks aligned to the Indonesian National Qualification Framework. Islamic schools vary from traditional pesantren to integrated madrasahs. Challenges include teacher shortages and disparities in rural education quality.

---

## Education in Israel
Israeli education has three tiers: primary, middle, and high school, with compulsory education through grade 12. Schools are divided into state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab sectors, with rising enrollment in Haredi and Arab schools. Higher education is highly valued and subsidized, with strong focus on STEM fields. Entrance requires matriculation certificates and psychometric tests. Disparities exist in resources and outcomes, especially for Arab and Haredi populations. Special programs aim to increase inclusion. Israel ranks highly in tertiary education attainment among OECD countries.

---

## Education in Egypt
Egypt has a centralized education system with free compulsory basic education (ages 4-14) followed by secondary and tertiary education. The system comprises general, technical, and Al-Azhar religious schools. Despite high enrollment, educational quality challenges exist, including rote learning, teacher qualifications, and resource disparities. The higher education system includes many public and private institutions, facing overcrowding and funding issues. International curricula operate alongside the national system. Education reforms have focused on access, quality, and integrating technology, though private tutoring is widespread.

---

## Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education system is administered primarily by the Ministry of Education, with free education available from primary through tertiary levels. The structure includes primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate (pre-university), and tertiary education. National examinations govern progression. The system includes national, provincial, private, and international schools, as well as religious institutions like pirivenas and madrasas. Higher education is competitive and largely state-funded with limited private universities. Vocational education operates under dedicated ministries. Challenges include rigidity and calls for private university expansion.
