# Global Education Systems: A Comparative Overview

## England
The English education system has evolved from medieval religious institutions to a modern, state-funded framework where education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18. The system is predominantly state-funded (93%), comprising community schools, academies, free schools, and voluntary aided schools, all overseen by Ofsted. Compulsory schooling is divided into Foundation Stages, four Key Stages, and post-16 options like sixth forms, A-levels, or apprenticeships. Private schools, while smaller, operate independently of the National Curriculum. Higher education is accessible via the Russell Group universities, with tuition fees ranging up to £9,535 per annum for domestic students.

## France
France operates a centralized, secular (*laïque*) system regulated by the Ministry of National Education, which employs all public school teachers. The curriculum emphasizes a "common culture" to foster national identity, with mandatory education from age 6 to 18. The structure includes *maternelle* (preschool), *école élémentaire*, *collège* (middle school), and *lycée* (high school), culminating in the *baccalauréat*. Higher education is divided between public universities and prestigious *grandes écoles*. Tuition is extremely low for EU citizens (€170–380/year), while international students pay significantly more.

## Ghana
Ghana's education system, established post-independence, aims for free, compulsory, and universal basic education (ages 4–14). The structure includes kindergarten, primary, and junior high school, followed by senior high school and tertiary education. While enrollment rates are high, challenges persist regarding teacher shortages, curriculum quality, and the stigma surrounding vocational education. The system relies heavily on public funding supplemented by private initiatives and international donors to address financial inefficiencies and expand access.

## Turkey
Turkey's system, following the establishment of the Republic in 1923, is highly centralized under the Ministry of National Education to enforce secularism and Turkish nationalism. Compulsory education lasts 12 years (primary and middle school). Secondary education includes general and vocational tracks, leading to the *YKS* (University Entrance Exam). Religious instruction is mandatory in schools, and the curriculum has historically faced criticism for evolution denial and nationalist indoctrination. Higher education is dominated by public universities, though private institutions charge significantly higher fees.

## Hong Kong
Hong Kong's system, transitioning from British colonial rule to Chinese sovereignty, features a mix of public, subsidized, and private international schools. Compulsory education covers primary and secondary levels, leading to the HKDSE or IGCSE. Public education is free, but international schools command high fees. The system emphasizes academic achievement and has recently faced challenges regarding national security laws and student outflows. Higher education is competitive, with top universities like HKU and CUHK ranking globally.

## Iran
Iran's education system is characterized by centralization and the Islamization of curricula following the 1979 Revolution. Education is free and compulsory through secondary school, with a strong emphasis on religious studies and gender segregation in public institutions. The system faces challenges including a shortage of teachers, high dropout rates, and a widening gap between public and private education quality. Vocational training is growing to address labor market needs, though informal apprenticeships remain common.

## Sweden
Sweden's system is based on a nine-year comprehensive (*grundskola*) school that is free and secular, with a strong focus on equality and student well-being. Grading is non-punitive in early years, with national tests in grades 3, 6, and 9. Higher education is free for EU citizens and follows the Bologna Process, offering bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. The system includes independent (*friskolor*) schools that receive public funding but operate autonomously, sparking debate over standards and equity.

## Taiwan
Taiwan's system is highly competitive, driven by a culture of academic excellence and the pressure to score high on national entrance exams. Education is compulsory for 12 years, followed by a rigorous senior high school curriculum leading to the College Entrance Examination (CSE). Public universities are subsidized, but admission is fiercely competitive. The system is noted for its high enrollment rates and strong engineering focus, though it faces criticism for over-emphasis on rote learning.

## Norway
Norway's system is characterized by a long, comprehensive *grunnskole* (foundation school) where grades are not assigned until age 13. Upper secondary education (*videregående skole*) is elective, with students choosing between general and vocational paths. Higher education is free and offers both university and university college degrees. The system prioritizes inclusivity and has high international performance in science and mathematics, though it faces criticism regarding the slow pace of instruction.

## Nigeria
Nigeria's system follows a 6-3-3-4 structure (Primary, Junior Secondary, Senior Secondary, Tertiary). Education is free and compulsory at the basic level, managed by the Universal Basic Education Commission. The system is fragmented between federal, state, and private institutions, with significant disparities in quality and infrastructure. Challenges include teacher shortages, political instability, and a growing reliance on private tutoring to improve academic performance for university entrance.

## Armenia
Armenia's system was reformed in the 1990s to move away from the Soviet model, emphasizing Armenian language and culture. Education is free and compulsory through secondary school. The system faces issues with high student absenteeism, low high school enrollment compared to EU averages, and a need for increased funding. Higher education is strong in fields like medicine and engineering, with universities participating in European integration programs.

## Republic of Ireland
Ireland's system offers free second-level education since 1967. It is divided into the Junior Cycle, Transition Year (optional), and Senior Cycle, culminating in the Leaving Certificate. The system is heavily influenced by religious patronage, with the majority of schools under Catholic control. Recent reforms aim to reduce the dominance of Catholic patronage and improve equity through the Deis scheme for disadvantaged students.

## Indonesia
Indonesia's system is 12 years of compulsory education, divided into primary (*SD*), junior high (*SMP*), and high school (*SMA*). Public schools are secular, while Islamic schools (*pesantren* and *madrasah*) are also widely attended. Challenges include low literacy rates in outer islands, a large teacher-to-student ratio, and disparities in funding between Java and other regions. Higher education is expanding rapidly to meet the growing demand for university places.

## Israel
Israel's education system is highly valued and centralized, with a strong focus on science, technology, and military service. Compulsory education runs through 12th grade, leading to the *Bagrut* exam. The system is divided into state-secular, state-religious, and Arab tracks, with significant disparities in resources and outcomes. Higher education is accessible and subsidized, with a high percentage of the population holding tertiary degrees.

## Egypt
Egypt's system is highly centralized, with free compulsory education from age 4 to 14. While enrollment rates are high, quality remains low, leading to a massive reliance on private tutoring. The system faces challenges such as low teacher status, poor infrastructure, and a gap between the public and private sectors. Higher education is expanding but suffers from overcrowding and a lack of autonomy for universities.

## Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's system is competitive and rigid, with a focus on national languages and religious instruction. Primary education lasts five years, followed by junior and senior secondary levels leading to the GCE O/L and A/L exams. Higher education is free but extremely competitive, with low graduation rates. The system is supported by a robust network of private schools and vocational training centers.
