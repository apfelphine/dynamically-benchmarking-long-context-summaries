# Educational Systems Overview

## England
English education has evolved significantly since its medieval roots, initially focused on clerical training at Oxford and Cambridge. Nineteenth-century reforms expanded provision, culminating in the Elementary Education Act 1870 and the Education Act 1902. Currently, education is compulsory for ages 5 to 18, with state-funded schooling being the norm for 93% of children.

Schools are diverse, ranging from comprehensive to selective grammar schools, and various state-funded models exist, including Academies, Free Schools, and Foundation Schools. The curriculum, governed by the National Curriculum for primary and secondary levels, covers thirteen core subjects. Post-16 education mandates continuation until age 18 via further education, apprenticeships, or work.

## France
The modern French education system was established by Jules Ferry in the late 19th century, making public instruction mandatory, free, and secular ($l'école\ républicaine$). All educational programs are regulated by the Ministry of National Education and Youth.

The system features distinct levels: primary school (ages 3-11), middle school (*collège*, ages 11-15), and high school (*lycée*, ages 15-18), preparing students for the *baccalauréat*. Higher education is split between public universities and elite, specialized *grandes écoles*. Education is largely state-funded, resulting in very low tuition fees for EU/EEA citizens.

## Ghana
Ghana's education history spans from oral, informal knowledge transmission to formal schooling introduced by European settlers. Post-independence, the government prioritized universal basic education, leading to significant enrollment increases, though quality has been a persistent challenge.

Basic education is compulsory and free for 12 years (ages 4-15), divided into Kindergarten, Primary School, and Junior High School. Secondary education branches into academic (Senior High School, leading to WASSCE) or vocational streams (TVET). Tertiary education sees growth in both public and private institutions.

## Turkey
The Turkish education system was secularized and nationalized following the foundation of the Turkish Republic, consolidating previously separate religious and reform channels under the Ministry of National Education. Compulsory education was extended to 12 years in 2012.

The structure includes pre-primary, 8-year primary education (compulsory and free), and secondary education, which offers both general and vocational tracks. Higher education is state-funded for public universities, though private institutions exist. The system heavily emphasizes Turkish nationalism in Atatürk's thought.

## Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education developed from early missionary and village schools to a bilingual and bicultural system championed by Frederick Stewart. Public education is free, and compulsory attendance is required until age 18.

The system features stages from Kindergarten through to Secondary 6, culminating in the HKDSE. While the public sector is free, private and international schools offer alternative curricula. Recent reforms have emphasized experiential learning, and the curriculum now incorporates Citizenship and Social Development.

## Iran
Iranian education has historical roots dating back to ancient Persia, with modern development occurring during the Safavid and Qajar eras. The 1979 Islamic Revolution led to the Islamization of the system, resulting in gender segregation and a focus on Islamic values in textbooks.

Education is highly centralized under the Ministry of Education. The system includes K-12 education, with upper-secondary education not being compulsory. Higher education is state-funded for public universities, and while enrollment of women in universities is high, the system faces challenges related to privatization and teacher shortages.

## Sri Lanka
Education in Sri Lanka has evolved from ancient Buddhist traditions, influenced heavily by colonial Christian missions. The government established a free education system in 1938, aiming to provide universal, vernacular instruction.

The structure involves five stages: primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate, and tertiary. The GCE A/L is the university entrance exam. While government schools are the majority, private, international, and religious schools are also prominent, and private tutoring is widely used due to high exam competitiveness.

## Nigeria
The Nigerian system is divided into basic, secondary, and tertiary education. Basic education is 9 years (free and compulsory), transitioning to 6-3-3-4 secondary schooling. Federal government colleges are often viewed as elite due to merit-based admission through national exams.

Vocational training (TVET) is a key focus, though it remains largely informal (apprenticeships). The system faces challenges related to infrastructure, teacher qualifications, and significant regional disparities in educational access and literacy rates.

## Armenia
Armenian education is characterized by near-universal state control, with 97% of schools being public and free. The system is structured into three compulsory levels: primary (1-4), middle school (5-9), and high school (10-12).

Public schools adhere to a national curriculum that blends global trends with local culture. Private schools must adhere to licensing guidelines while having more funding flexibility. The country is a member of the Bologna Process, promoting international comparability in higher education.

## Ireland
Education is compulsory from age 6 to 16 (or three years of second-level education). English is the primary language, though *Gaelscoileanna* use Irish. The system comprises Junior Cycle (three years) and Senior Cycle (two years) leading to the Leaving Certificate.

The system is varied, with the majority of state-funded schools under religious control (primarily Catholic). Educational disadvantage is addressed through the Deis scheme, which provides additional resources to low-income schools. University education is highly regarded globally.

## Indonesia
Education in Indonesia transitioned from colonial-era segregation (Dutch system) to a post-independence national focus emphasizing nationalism. The system mandates 12 years of schooling.

Basic education (K-9) is compulsory and free, taught in Indonesian. The curriculum historically emphasized rote learning and the Pancasila. The system integrates formal, private religious (*pesantren*), and modern secular schooling options, with vocational training being a major pathway.

## Israel
The Israeli system spans three tiers: primary (1-6), middle school (7-9), and high school (10-12), with compulsory education through Grade 12. Schools are divided into four tracks: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (*Haredi*), and Arab.

Societally, education is highly valued as a path to socioeconomic mobility. While the system is highly ranked internationally, disparities exist between sectors, and challenges persist regarding the integration and education of Haredi students.

## Egypt
Egyptian education is highly centralized across three levels: basic (ages 4-14), secondary (ages 15-17), and tertiary. Basic education is free and compulsory in government institutions.

The system features a dual track: general and technical secondary education. Despite major reforms aimed at quality improvement and access, low teacher status, high student-to-teacher ratios, and reliance on private tutoring due to public education inadequacies remain significant challenges.

## Sweden
The Swedish system is built around a nine-year comprehensive school (*grundskola*), mandatory for ages 6–16. The curriculum is broad, covering 16 compulsory subjects, with mandatory national tests in Grades 3, 6, and 9.

Upper secondary education (*gymnasieskola*) is elective, divided into preparatory and vocational paths. Higher education follows the Bologna Process structure. Independent schools (*friskolor*) receive public funding, though the system faces public debate regarding standards and the profit motive of these private options.

## Taiwan
Taiwan enforces a 12-year compulsory education. Public schools operate with tight discipline, and instruction is primarily in Mandarin. Access to high school and university is governed by intense national exams.

The structure includes primary, junior high, and senior high school, with vocational/skill-based options available. While public education is largely free, the intense academic pressure drives a massive private cram school industry (*buxiban*). Tertiary education offers diverse paths, with engineering being a popular field.

## Norway
The Norwegian system is comprehensive, comprising mandatory elementary (ages 6-12), lower secondary (ages 13-16), and upper secondary (ages 16-19). The system is state-funded, with private schools being rare and heavily regulated.

The curriculum is broad, requiring 16 compulsory subjects in the primary stages. Higher education is structured around the Bologna Process. The system emphasizes inclusivity, and special education integrates students into ordinary schools.
