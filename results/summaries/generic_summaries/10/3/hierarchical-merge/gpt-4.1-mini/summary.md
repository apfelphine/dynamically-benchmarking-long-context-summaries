# Education Systems Across Selected Countries

## England and France
England mandates compulsory education from ages 5 to 18, structured into primary and secondary phases culminating in national exams like GCSEs and A-levels. The system emphasizes technical and vocational pathways (e.g., BTECs, T Levels), with about 93% attending state-funded schools subject to inspections and funded through national formulas. Higher education is mainly public, with capped tuition fees supported by income-contingent loans. Ongoing reforms seek greater equity and expansion of technical education.

France offers affordable public higher education within the European Bologna framework and prestigious Grandes Écoles requiring competitive preparatory classes. The public system stresses strict secularism, banning visible religious symbols, and maintains high academic standards through collaboration with national research bodies. Internationally, France extends its influence via AEFE schools offering bilingual and English-language curricula.

## Ghana and Nigeria
Ghana provides basic education (ages 4–15) advancing to Senior High School with academic and vocational tracks, culminating in the WASSCE exam. Vocational and apprenticeship training complement formal schooling, while tertiary access relies on WASSCE results followed by national service. Decentralized governance and substantial government spending coexist with private sector involvement addressing infrastructural gaps. Literacy has improved but equity challenges persist, notably in tertiary access and gender balance.

Nigeria’s "6-3-3-4" system includes six years primary, three years junior and senior secondary, and four years tertiary education with national exams at key stages. Vocational training and apprenticeships exist alongside formal education. Despite Universal Basic Education efforts, challenges include outdated curricula, teacher shortages, rural and gender disparities, and security issues. Plans to boost digital literacy by 2030 involve international cooperation.

## Turkey and Armenia
Turkey requires 12 years in a "4+4+4" model covering primary through secondary education with general, vocational, and specialized tracks including the International Baccalaureate. Religious education focused chiefly on Islam is constitutionally mandated from grade 4 and integrated with nationalist curricula. Higher education features over 160 universities with competitive national exam admissions; fees are low in public institutions but higher in private ones. Minority language education faces restrictions, and private schooling demand grows amid frequent curricular changes and textbook controversies.

Armenia offers free, compulsory K-12 education emphasizing STEM and national studies, sustaining near-universal literacy since 1960. Higher education aligns with European standards through the Bologna Process and EU-supported reforms. Challenges include student absenteeism linked to child labor and education quality. Efforts focus on raising high school completion to meet EU and OECD benchmarks, supported by initiatives like technology centers fostering 21st-century skills.

## Hong Kong, Iran, Sweden, Taiwan, and Norway
Hong Kong’s educational system mandates primary and junior secondary education ending with the HKDSE exam, features bilingual instruction (Cantonese and English), and offers diverse curricula including IB and A-levels. Political changes since 2020 have affected enrollment and teacher availability. Higher education is English-medium with centralized admissions and ethical adoption of AI tools.

Iran has centralized compulsory elementary and lower-secondary education with optional upper-secondary tracks, integrating mandatory religious instruction since 1979. University entrance depends on competitive exams, while challenges include teacher shortages, rising private schooling linked to inequality, and political-religious influences. Female university enrollment surpasses males amid substantial education budgets.

Sweden mandates nine years of compulsory schooling plus nearly universal upper secondary education with academic and vocational options, including publicly funded independent schools. Higher education spans bachelor to doctoral levels with standardized admissions. Despite high regard for early childhood education, recent international assessment declines have prompted quality-focused reforms.

Taiwan’s 12-year compulsory education includes academic and vocational senior high tracks, fostering high university enrollment rates but raising concerns about graduate unemployment. Supplementary cram schools ("buxiban") are widespread. Corporal punishment is officially banned but reportedly persists in some settings. Taiwan is a regional hub for Chinese language learners.

Norway requires compulsory education until age 16, with upper secondary general or vocational tracks and apprenticeships. University admission is GPA-based and aligned with the Bologna Process. The system emphasizes inclusive special education and professional support, operating on a semester calendar starting mid-August.

## Armenia, Ireland, Indonesia, Israel, Egypt, and Sri Lanka
Armenia’s higher education incorporates the Lisbon Recognition Convention and Bologna Process, backed by EU programs that improve quality assurance. Challenges include dropout rates linked to economic factors, with innovative after-school centers promoting digital skills.

Ireland’s system provides primary to tertiary education with compulsory schooling until age 16, culminating in the Leaving Certificate. Though historically dominated by religious schools, recent governance reforms promote inclusivity. Irish students achieve strong international results, supported by special needs and disadvantaged student programs. Infrastructure investments attract international students.

Indonesia mandates 12 years of education with free primary and junior secondary schooling; senior secondary fees are nominal. Education is offered via secular public schools and religious madrasah. The 2013 curriculum emphasizes discipline specialization. Challenges include uneven quality, teacher shortages in remote areas, and low tertiary enrollment (~36%). Early childhood education is mostly private and non-compulsory.

Israel enforces compulsory education from grades 1–12 across diverse school types including state-secular, state-religious, Arab, and private institutions. Nearly half of adults hold tertiary degrees, supporting a knowledge economy. Persistent issues include large class sizes, low teacher pay, and inequalities especially in underfunded Arab schools. Affirmative action and multilingual education aim to address disparities. Israeli universities hold international recognition amid labor challenges like frequent teacher strikes.

Egypt’s centralized system covers basic, secondary, and higher education with compulsory schooling for nine years. Islamic education is prominent through institutions like al-Azhar. Despite reforms and growth, public education quality suffers from outdated methods, large classes, underqualified teachers, exam corruption, and socio-economic gaps. Private tutoring is widespread to compensate for deficiencies. Vocational training is insufficient, contributing to youth unemployment; COVID-19 accelerated distance learning adoption.

Sri Lanka’s free, compulsory education (up to grade 9) is delivered through diverse institutions reflecting ethnic and religious diversity. Progression is exam-based, with heavy reliance on private tutoring due to intense competition for limited university spots. University education is free but selective; alternative pathways include foreign/open universities and vocational training overseen by a national commission. The system faces criticism for rigidity and competitiveness, with controversial initiatives like compulsory military-style training for undergraduates having been discontinued.
