## Education in Israel

Education is highly valued in Israel, central to socioeconomic mobility and Jewish culture. The system features four main tracks: state-secular, state-religious (Jewish studies-intensive), independent Haredi religious (largely Torah-focused), and Arab (teaching in Arabic, emphasizing Arab history and culture). Private, international, and integrated Jewish-Arab schools reflect population diversity. The Israeli Pupils’ Rights Law (2000) prohibits sectarian discrimination.

Key challenges persist, notably the limited core curriculum in Haredi schools, resulting in low workforce participation and lower matriculation (bagrut) rates. Bagrut exam eligibility, critical for higher education and elite military roles, varies greatly by city and sector. Most students are conscripted into the IDF post-secondary, with academic deferments possible. Higher education admission relies on bagrut scores and a psychometric test, except for Israel’s Open University. Despite high educational attainment (46% of Israeli Jews hold degrees), public investment per student is comparatively low.

Higher education receives substantial government subsidies, especially for post-military students, and Arab university enrollment is sharply rising. However, the Arab sector has fewer resources, higher dropout rates, and infrastructural deficits; recent government and nonprofit initiatives have aimed to increase access and inclusivity. Girls surpass boys in matriculation and university enrollment, but top academic ranks—and STEM fields—remain male-dominated. Teachers face low salary, large classes, low morale, and union resistance to reform.

Textbooks remain contentious: scholars cite negative portrayals of Arabs and limited Arab culture, while monitoring organizations report improvements and efforts at coexistence. Arabic textbooks often contain errors and minimize Arab-Palestinian presence. Strikes by faculty and students, often concerning pay, tuition fees, and working conditions, have affected the school calendar and prompted policy negotiations. The Ministry of Education annually rewards municipalities for innovation and progress, including immigrant absorption and student empowerment.

Israel’s education system blends strong achievement and international rankings with persistent gaps in resources, equity, and workforce readiness.

## Education in Other Countries

### Ghana
Ghana offers free, compulsory education for 12 years, ending with the WASSCE. Despite boosted enrollment from FCUBE, teacher shortages, poor facilities, and gender/geographic disparities persist.

### England
England mandates school through age 18, culminating in GCSEs. Ofsted regulates standards with support for disadvantaged/special needs students. Higher education charges tuition but provides aid.

### Hong Kong
Hong Kong gives free education (6–18), ending with the HKDSE. Recent reforms cover patriotic education, AI integration, and preschool vouchers.

### France
France’s centralized, secular system runs preschool to baccalauréat, with rigorous teacher recruitment and affordable higher education amid ongoing diversity and religious debates.

### Sweden
Sweden offers free, compulsory education to age 16, academic/vocational tracks, and debated friskolor. Reforms address early childhood, curriculum, and safety.

### Turkey
Turkey’s “4+4+4” structure provides academic/vocational/apprenticeship paths, religious and English instruction, and centralized higher education facing access issues.

### Norway
Norway emphasizes high-quality, inclusive education to age 16, early English, strong teacher training, and special education.

### Iran
Iran’s centralized system ensures compulsory education through elementary/lower-secondary, academic/vocational options, and strict teacher training; the Konkour exam determines university entry amid teacher shortages and inequality.

### Armenia
Armenia transitioned from Soviet to national curriculum, expanded language instruction, and adopted European standards. Child labor and poor school quality drive absenteeism.

### Taiwan
Taiwan mandates twelve years of exam-heavy education, offering academic/vocational tracks, early English, high university enrollment, and graduate oversupply.

### Indonesia
Indonesia offers compulsory twelve-year education via academic, technical, and Islamic tracks but struggles with equity, rural access, and rote learning emphasis.

### Nigeria
Nigeria’s 9-3-4 model supports varied education types, emphasizing adult literacy, digital education, teacher improvements, amid regional/gender disparities.

### Egypt
Egypt’s centralized education includes public, private, Al-Azhar (religious), and agricultural schools, facing challenges of facilities, inequality, private tutoring reliance, and graduate unemployment.

### Ireland
Ireland mandates free schooling (6–16), predominantly Catholic, with varied secondary options, special needs supports, and extra aid for disadvantaged schools.

### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s multi-track system (national, provincial, Buddhist, private/international) relies on competitive exams and private tuition; limited free tertiary spots push students to private/local/overseas options.

---

This summary distills educational structures, challenges, reforms, and outcomes across Israel and major world systems, emphasizing issues of equity, resource allocation, teacher status, access, and the complex interplay of curriculum, sociopolitical factors, and inclusion.
