# Comparative Overview of Global Education Systems

## Africa: Nigeria, Ghana, Egypt
Nigeria’s education system features structured pathways to tertiary institutions, comprising universal basic education, vocational, informal, private, and international schools. Challenges include regional and gender disparities, funding shortages, and high out-of-school rates. Recent efforts focus on digital innovation and literacy, while access, equity, and resource allocation remain significant hurdles. Ghana has shifted toward a formal, compulsory system, with increased ICT integration and inclusive policies, but faces persistent gaps in funding and gender and regional participation. Egypt’s system is rooted in centralization and tradition, with diverse school types and globally recognized higher education. However, overcrowding, outdated practices, teacher shortages, and rural-urban disparities impede quality and equity, despite ongoing reforms and technical education initiatives.

## Asia: Hong Kong, Taiwan, Iran, Turkey, Indonesia, Armenia, Sri Lanka, Israel
Hong Kong and Taiwan both offer mandatory, competitive, and multilingual education; Hong Kong emphasizes global readiness, AI integration, and a robust higher education sector, while Taiwan stresses Mandarin, English, STEM, arts, discipline, and government-backed teacher training. Indonesia operates one of the world’s largest systems, balancing secular and Islamic schools, with literacy and female participation rising despite access and funding challenges, particularly in remote areas. Iran’s centralized system focuses on competitive university entry, strong female STEM representation, modernization, and hybrid learning efforts, although regional inequalities persist. Turkey and Armenia blend general, vocational, and religious tracks, with Turkey debating religious content and increasing international outreach, and Armenia progressing toward European alignment, but facing absenteeism issues. Sri Lanka’s system, marked by universal state schooling and high literacy, struggles with exam-driven competition, constrained university access, and the pressures of tuition centers. Israel’s sector-divided schools produce variable graduation rates, especially in the Arab sector, amid resource gaps and ongoing equity reforms targeting representation and inclusivity.

## Europe: England, France, Sweden, Norway, Ireland
England enforces compulsory educational stages, blending academic and vocational routes, inclusivity, and financial support; recent reforms focus on school autonomy and special needs. France’s centralized, secular system offers free schooling with academic/vocational tracks and selective grandes écoles, with reforms for gender equality and academic rigor. Sweden and Norway provide universal, publicly funded education with strong support for special needs, anti-discrimination initiatives, and Bologna-aligned higher education. Sweden debates equity in privately-managed schools, and Norway emphasizes play-based, IT-integrated learning and informal assessment. Ireland’s compulsory system, transitioning away from religious tradition, supports multi-denominational schools, integrates personal development, maintains low dropout rates, and prioritizes European standards and Irish-language instruction.
