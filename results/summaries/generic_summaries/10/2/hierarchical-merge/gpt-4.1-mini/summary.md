# Education Systems Overview: Selected Countries

## Ghana, England, Hong Kong, France, Sweden, Turkey, Norway, Iran, Armenia, Taiwan, and Indonesia

These countries exhibit diverse educational structures balancing compulsory schooling, academic and vocational tracks, and higher education frameworks. Ghana emphasizes free basic education up to age 15, facing challenges in vocational training quality and digital access. England mandates schooling from ages 5 to 16 with strong quality assurance and inclusive policies, while Hong Kong shifted to a six-year secondary cycle promoting project-based learning with English as the main instruction language.

France provides free, secular education from age 3, emphasizing literature and social cohesion, with affordable higher education aligned to the Bologna Process. Sweden offers free early childhood education and universal access through upper secondary levels, focusing on multilingualism and inclusion amidst debates on privatization and fluctuating international rankings. Turkey's centralized 12-year system integrates religious and core subjects but faces reform challenges. Norway combines academic and vocational education with strong teacher qualifications and limited private schools. Iran emphasizes Islamic curricula and recent expansions in vocational and distance learning. Armenia follows a 12-year mandate with focus on national language and multiple foreign languages, though quality and attendance pose challenges. Taiwan features 12 years of education primarily in Mandarin, with extensive vocational options and special education, supported by private cram schools. Indonesia runs a vast system balancing secular and Islamic curricula, contending with regional disparities and ongoing decentralization reforms.

## Nigeria and Egypt

Nigeria’s education encompasses secondary, tertiary, vocational, and informal sectors, facing infrastructural and staffing challenges, especially in rural northern regions. Efforts target increasing literacy through multilingual programs and digital initiatives, aiming for 95% youth literacy by 2030. Its tertiary sector includes numerous universities requiring standardized exams for admission, while vocational education grapples with outdated curricula.

Egypt's system features basic, secondary, and higher levels with general, technical, and religious tracks, marked by disparities among urban, rural, and Al-Azhar schools. Challenges include rote learning, poor teacher qualifications, and widespread private tutoring. Reforms seek to modernize pedagogy and governance, aligning with labor market needs, supported by national strategic plans and innovative programs including international partnerships.

## Ireland, Sri Lanka, and Israel

Ireland mandates education from ages 5/6 to 16, combining primary and diversified secondary schooling with reforms reducing religious influence. It ranks highly in international assessments with strong disability support and evolving integration within European higher education.

Sri Lanka offers free education in Sinhala and Tamil through multiple ministries, with a strong history rooted in Buddhism and colonial legacy. It administers national exams for school progression and university entry, balancing cultural heritage and modern demands amid challenges like administrative inefficiencies and competitiveness. Expanding online education and scholarships improve accessibility.

Israel focuses on literacy and high-tech workforce preparation, with a matriculation exam critical for higher education and service. Despite strong universities and significant Arab and female student participation, disparities persist among minority groups. Ongoing reforms address resource gaps, teacher shortages, and curricular biases, supported by notable government investment and recognition programs promoting innovation and integration.
