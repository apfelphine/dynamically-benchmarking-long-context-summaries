# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in Ghana, England, Hong Kong, France, Sweden, Turkey, Iran, Taiwan, Indonesia, Armenia, Nigeria, Egypt, Ireland, Sri Lanka, and Israel**

## **Core Features and Comparative Trends**

The education systems across Ghana, England, Hong Kong, France, Sweden, Turkey, Iran, Taiwan, Indonesia, Armenia, Nigeria, Egypt, Ireland, Sri Lanka, and Israel reflect a global diversity of models shaped by historical legacies, cultural values, political contexts, and socio-economic priorities. While all systems emphasize foundational education and access, they differ significantly in structure, governance, curriculum design, and equity outcomes.

In **Ghana**, a 20-year system from kindergarten to undergraduate level provides universal access, with compulsory education from age 6 to 14. Despite strong enrollment (84% primary net enrollment, 79.04% literacy), challenges include underdeveloped vocational training, low completion rates, and socioeconomic disparities. Private education dominates, teacher training occurs through public Colleges of Education, and ICT integration remains limited. Reforms under Rawlings and the Ghana Scholarship Secretariat aim to improve equity, though teacher shortages and informal TVET persist.

**England** operates a decentralized, state-funded system with 93% of children aged 3–18 in free schools. Education spans four Key Stages (KS1–KS4), culminating in GCSEs, with post-16 options including A-levels, BTECs, or apprenticeships. Academies, especially in disadvantaged areas, have improved outcomes, with 88% rated "good" or "outstanding" by 2015. Strong performance in literacy (13th globally) and rising access for marginalized students reflect a balance between academic rigor and equity. The RQF/FHEQ framework aligns with European standards.

**Hong Kong** maintains a compulsory, free system from age 6 to 18, culminating in the HKDSE. English Medium Instruction and international curricula (e.g., IB) yield high university acceptance. However, political shifts since 1997—including the national security law—have led to declining enrolment (1,474 students left in 2022), teacher resignations (3,500), and student exits (28,000). Textbook revisions emphasize patriotism, and the Citizenship and Social Development subject replaces Liberal Studies. While academic pressure is intense, reforms promote group work and innovation. Tertiary education is globally strong, with HKU and CUHK ranked among the world’s top institutions.

**France** features a centralized curriculum under the Ministry of National Education, emphasizing national identity through history and civic education. The system includes preschool, primary (3–11), middle school (11–15), and high school (15–18), culminating in the baccalauréat or vocational CAP. A selective *grandes écoles* system (e.g., engineering, business) requires intense preparation via *CPGEs*, with rigorous oral exams. Teacher recruitment is tiered, with *agrégés* receiving better pay and reduced workloads. The secular model (*laïcité*) prohibits religious instruction, though exceptions exist in Alsace and Moselle. Tuition is low for EU students but high for international applicants.

**Sweden** offers a comprehensive, student-centered model beginning with preschool and a nine-year *grundskola* (ages 6–16). The curriculum is standardized, with a diploma project graded E or F. At the secondary level, students attend *gymnasieskola* (three years), with 18 national programs—two-thirds leading to university. Admission is based on grades, and a rise in non-qualifiers (from 12.3% to 15.6% between 2011–2018) prompted the introduction of introductory programs. Higher education follows the Bologna Process, with structured cycles (bachelor’s, master’s, doctoral). The system prioritizes inclusion, well-being, and student autonomy, with strong support for special education and anti-violence policies.

**Turkey** introduced a secular, centralized system in 1933, expanding compulsory education to 12 years (2012). The curriculum promotes Turkish nationalism, with the 2017 reform introducing jihad and removing evolution theory. Students receive a uniform diploma, with pathways determined by high school track. Vocational education includes apprenticeships. Public universities offer affordable access, while private institutions are more expensive. English is taught from grade 2, with greater emphasis in private schools. Religious education begins at age 10, with Islam as a major component. Criticisms include ideological bias, poor textbooks, and curriculum instability.

**Iran** operates a highly centralized system under the Ministry of Education, allocating 20% of government spending and 5% of GDP—50% to secondary, 21% to tertiary. Mandatory Islamic prayer (30 minutes) and study of General Soleimani’s will (2023) reinforce religious values. Admission to university requires the national *Konkur* exam. English is dominant in foreign language instruction (banned in primary schools in 2018), with French added in 2022. Teacher shortages (60,000) and lack of counselors persist. Engineering and science are strong, with over 70% of university students being women. A 2024 law criminalized protests against educational policies, indicating increasing state control.

**Taiwan** delivers a rigorous 12-year compulsory education (ages 3–17), emphasizing Mandarin, math, science, and standardized exams. Students take national exams to determine entry into academic or vocational senior high. Over 95% of graduates attend university. Top institutions like NTU and NTCH offer strong programs in engineering and medicine. Teacher training includes internships and certification. The 3+3 system allows students to bypass the CAP test via a competitive exam. Private education is widespread, including English immersion and cram schools. Special education supports gifted and special needs students.

**Indonesia** has a large, publicly funded system with 12 years of compulsory primary and secondary education (free for primary and middle school, modest fees for high school). Instruction is primarily in Indonesian from grade 3 onward. Religious education is prominent through *pesantren* and formal *madrasah* systems. Three types exist: *pesantren* (religious), *madrasah* (balanced), and *sekolah islam* (state-aligned). Vocational and pre-professional tracks are available. Higher education follows the KKNI framework. Despite high enrollment and survival rates, challenges remain in teacher quality, equity, funding, and access—especially in remote areas.

**Armenia** provides free, compulsory 12-year K–12 education with nearly 100% literacy and public schools dominating (97%). The system follows national curriculum standards, though high school enrollment lags OECD benchmarks. University access is determined by entrance exams. Armenia is a full Bologna Process member, participates in Erasmus+, and has EU-aligned quality assurance. Challenges include rising absenteeism and dropout rates, linked to child labor and declining quality. Post-school initiatives like Tumo and COAF SMART foster 21st-century skills.

**Nigeria** follows a 9-3-4 structure: nine years of compulsory basic education, followed by three years of senior secondary. Students take the SSCE (WAEC/NECO), with results valid for one year. Alternative qualifications (GCE, IJMB, JUPEB) allow direct university entry—JUPEB bypassing UTME. Tertiary education includes 153 institutions (40 federal, 45 state, 68 private). Entry requires 5 O/L credits, a JAMB score of 180+, or direct entry. Rural education suffers from poor infrastructure, staff shortages, insecurity, and unpaid salaries. Vocational education is underfunded and politically resisted. Female education has improved, but poverty, teen pregnancy, and insecurity remain barriers. Literacy rose from 62.02% (2018) to 69% (2022), but regional disparities persist. The government aims for 95% digital literacy by 2030.

**Egypt** includes public, private, and specialized tracks: the Al-Azhar system (six-year primary, three-year preparatory, three-year secondary), international schools (British, American, IB), and agricultural secondary schools. In 2025, a Japan-inspired engineering diploma was introduced, and university rankings improved (Cairo University at 350th, AUC at 410th). However, systemic issues persist: low-quality public education, widespread private tutoring (58% of families), underqualified teachers, low salaries, corporal punishment, regional inequities, and high youth unemployment (36.1%). Corruption, including exam leakage, undermines integrity.

**Ireland** spans primary (ages 4–12) and secondary levels, ending with the mandatory Junior Certificate. An optional Transition Year is followed by a two-year Senior Cycle culminating in the Leaving Certificate. Most state schools are Catholic (88.4%), though multidenominational and independent schools are growing. English is the primary language, with Irish taught in Gaelscoileanna and as a core subject. The system emphasizes equity, access, and linguistic diversity, supported by EHEA and NFQ frameworks. Ireland performs strongly in reading, math, and science—with only 12.3% of students leaving after the Junior Certificate (below EU average). The DEIS program supports disadvantaged students in 1,200 schools. Government investment (€200 million from EIB) improves infrastructure. Irish higher education ranks among the world’s top 20, attracting international students who become global leaders.

**Sri Lanka** has deep historical roots in Buddhist monastic traditions. The formal system began with British reforms in 1836, with free education established in 1938. Sinhala and Tamil are official mediums. Post-independence, literacy and enrollment increased. The system includes five levels: primary (1–5), junior secondary (6–9), senior secondary (10–11), collegiate (12–13), and tertiary. Key exams include the Scholarship Exam (Grade 5), G.C.E. O/Ls (Grade 11), and G.C.E. A/Ls (university entrance). Three government types: national schools (elite, centrally funded), provincial schools (under-resourced), and pirivenas (Buddhist monastic institutions). Private and international schools serve upper-middle-class and expatriate communities. Higher education evolved from colonial institutions to a decentralized system under the University Grants Commission. Since 1999, private institutions can grant degrees, supported by the Mahapola Fund. Despite progress, the system is fragmented, inefficient across three ministries, and rigidly exam-driven. Only ~16% of qualified students gain university admission. The 2011–2015 military-style leadership training policy has drawn criticism for authoritarianism.

**Israel** features compulsory education up to Grade 12, with four main tracks: state-secular, state-religious (Orthodox), independent religious (Haredi/Chinuch Atzmai), and Arab schools (Arabic-medium). The Bagrut exam is critical for university admission, military service, and employment. Israel ranks among OECD leaders in tertiary education attainment and spending, with universities ranked in the top 100 in Asia and top 200 globally—especially in STEM. Government support through subsidies, military service discounts (*atuda*), and financial aid enhances accessibility. However, disparities persist: the Haredi community faces low matriculation rates, while Arab students suffer from underfunding, teacher shortages, unequal access, and higher dropout rates. Gender disparities exist: women dominate social sciences, men dominate STEM (81% of professors). Teacher wages are low, and teaching reforms have been blocked by strikes. Controversies include textbook content—some studies claim Arab stereotypes are promoted in Arabic textbooks, while others argue for cultural accuracy and coexistence. Schools and universities have faced repeated strikes over pay, class size, and school days.

## **Key Trends and Challenges Across Systems**

Common challenges across all systems include **demographic decline**, **rising student well-being concerns**, **equity gaps**, and **marginalization of vulnerable groups**. In response, reforms increasingly focus on **inclusion**, **curriculum modernization**, and **student-centered learning**.

- **Performance vs. Equity**: Hong Kong and France emphasize high-stakes exams and national identity; Sweden and Ireland prioritize equity, holistic development, and well-being.  
- **Vocational and Tertiary Pathways**: Vocational education remains underdeveloped in Ghana, Nigeria, and Turkey, while Sweden, Taiwan, and Israel offer structured, accessible pathways.  
- **Political Influence**: Hong Kong, Iran, Turkey, and Egypt show how political shifts disrupt curricula, reduce enrolment, and alter educational values.  
- **Technology and Innovation**: Sweden and Hong Kong lead in digital integration, though implementation remains uneven. Ireland and Israel demonstrate institutional innovation in policy and delivery.  
- **Teacher Quality and Training**: Teacher shortages and underfunding persist in Iran, Indonesia, Nigeria, and Egypt. Teacher training is centralized in Iran and Turkey but under-resourced elsewhere.  
- **Inclusion and Diversity**: Sweden, Ireland, and Israel promote inclusive curricula and support for special needs and minority groups. Systems in Turkey, Iran, and Sri Lanka emphasize religious or national identity, sometimes at the expense of diversity.

Overall, while structural diversity is evident, a shared global trend is the move toward **more equitable, inclusive, and student-centered models**—driven by demographic changes, technological advancement, and growing recognition of mental health and social development in education. These systems collectively illustrate a spectrum of governance, cultural values, and performance, highlighting both the potential and persistent challenges in achieving universal quality, equity, and relevance in education.
