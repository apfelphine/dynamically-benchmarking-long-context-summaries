**Education in Ghana** began with informal, oral traditions and later evolved through colonial-era mission schools. Key milestones include the founding of the Philip Quaque Boys School in 1765 and the expansion of mission schools by the Wesleyan and Basel missions. Post-independence, under Kwame Nkrumah, education was made free and compulsory, with a focus on universal basic education and a national university. Reforms in the 1980s under Jerry Rawlings introduced a 12-year basic education system, reduced school duration, and emphasized vocational training. The system is now structured into basic, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a 20-year duration from kindergarten to undergraduate level. The government spends around 25% of its annual budget on education, with a high enrollment rate and gender parity in primary and secondary schools. Challenges include teacher shortages, low vocational education quality, and regional disparities. Despite progress, access remains unequal, with rural and marginalized groups facing barriers. The system has seen significant growth in enrollment and infrastructure, though quality and equity remain ongoing concerns.  

**Education in England** has a long history rooted in religious institutions, evolving into a state-funded, secular system. The 19th century saw the establishment of a national curriculum and compulsory education. Today, the system is structured into early years, primary, secondary, and higher education, with a strong emphasis on the National Curriculum. Schools are categorized into academies, community, foundation, and private institutions, with inspections by Ofsted. Education is compulsory until age 18, with a focus on core subjects and a wide range of curricula. The system is highly regulated, with a strong emphasis on standardized testing and teacher accountability. While access is generally equitable, challenges remain in terms of class size, teacher quality, and educational inequality.  

**Education in Hong Kong** is characterized by a highly structured, bilingual system with a strong emphasis on academic excellence. The curriculum is standardized, with a focus on Chinese and English languages, and a strong emphasis on exam performance. The system has seen a shift toward more experiential and project-based learning, with the introduction of the Citizenship and Social Development subject. The education system is free and compulsory until age 18, with a strong focus on academic performance and a high degree of centralization. However, concerns remain over the high pressure on students, the prevalence of cram schools, and the quality of teaching. The system has seen significant growth in enrollment, but challenges persist in terms of equity, teacher training, and student well-being.
