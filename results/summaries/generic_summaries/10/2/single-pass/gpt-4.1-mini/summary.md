# Education in Ghana
Ghana's education evolved from early informal oral traditions and apprenticeships to formal institutions influenced by European missionaries and colonial powers. Beginning with learning centers like the University of Moliyili in the 1700s and castle schools established by Europeans, formal education expanded notably in the 19th century, particularly through mission schools. Post-independence reforms aimed to provide free, compulsory education, though challenges persisted with quality and access. Recent reforms introduced a 12-year basic education system, free universal basic education, and efforts to improve vocational training (TVET), despite ongoing obstacles like limited resources and lower vocational school enrollment.

The education system is structured into basic education (kindergarten, primary, junior high), secondary (senior high school with academic and vocational paths), and tertiary education (universities, polytechnics, colleges of education). English is the primary medium, with curricula covering a range of core subjects. Ghana has seen increasing enrollment and school numbers, though disparities and quality issues remain. Private education plays a significant role alongside government efforts, with public-private partnerships addressing financing and access. Governance involves the Ministry of Education and agencies like Ghana Education Service, with considerable donor support. Adult literacy and non-formal education projects focus on reducing gender and geographic disparities.

# Education in England
England’s education system originated in religious institutions, evolving into a broad state-funded system after 19th-century reforms. Education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18, with structured stages including early years, primary (Key Stages 1 and 2), secondary (Key Stages 3 and 4), and post-16 education (sixth-form colleges, apprenticeships). State-funded schools are predominantly comprehensive, with specialized academies, grammar schools, faith schools, and free schools offering diverse curricula.

Private education accounts for about 7% of students and includes fee-paying independent schools with their own curricula. Home education is legally permitted under parental responsibility. The National Curriculum defines teaching requirements and subjects, complemented by compulsory Relationship and Sex Education and Personal, Social, Health and Economic education. Higher education is diverse, with public and private institutions, tuition fees (capped currently at £9,535 per annum), and financial aid. Universities offer undergraduate to doctoral degrees, with quality assured by external agencies. England ranks well in international assessments but faces challenges related to educational inequality and funding.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education started with traditional village schools and missionary efforts under British rule, evolving into a bilingual and bicultural system. Post-handover reforms introduced active and experiential learning methods. Recent political changes have influenced curriculum content and school enrolments. Pre-school education is partly subsidized; primary and secondary education is compulsory at ages 6–18, ending with the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE).

Secondary education includes junior and senior levels, with some offering International Baccalaureate diplomas. English-medium instruction is prevalent, especially in elite EMI schools, though Cantonese and Putonghua are also mediums of instruction. Tertiary education consists of eight publicly funded universities and several private institutions, generally using English as the medium. The higher education sector has grown with diverse programs, including distance learning and continuing education. Special education is trending towards integration, with services for immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children. The private sector and international schools offer education along global curricula with varied fees.

# Education in France
French education is centralized and secular, shaped by 19th-century reforms emphasizing free, compulsory education. The system includes preschool (maternelle), elementary, middle (collège), and high school (lycée), culminating in the baccalauréat, which can lead to higher education or vocational paths. Private schools mostly follow state curricula, with some autonomy.

Higher education features public universities and prestigious grandes écoles, with degrees aligned under the Bologna process (Licence, Master, Doctorat). The grandes écoles are selective elite institutions focusing on specific fields like engineering or business, supported by preparatory classes (CPGEs). Teacher recruitment involves competitive exams and university training. Religious instruction is banned in public schools except in limited regions, and religious symbols are prohibited. France maintains low tuition fees, with higher costs in private and business schools. The education system emphasizes literature and history for cultural identity, facing challenges from demographic shifts.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden offers nine years of compulsory comprehensive schooling (grundskola) from ages 6–16, followed by elective three-year upper secondary education (gymnasieskola) divided into preparatory and vocational programs. Preschool is widely accessible, focusing on foundational skills through play. Education emphasizes equality, multilingualism, and comprehensive student development.

The grading system includes letter grades from year 6 and national tests at grades 3, 6, and 9 to ensure standardized assessment. Higher education is structured around bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, following the Bologna process. Independent “free schools” funded publicly have risen since 1992, causing debate on standards and equity. Sweden participates actively in quality assurance and student aid. Challenges include concerns over declining PISA/PIRLS scores and increasing educational inequality linked partly to immigration.

# Education in Turkey
Following the foundation of the Turkish Republic, Turkey streamlined its education under a centralized Ministry of National Education. Pre-primary and primary education lasts 8 years, compulsory and free in public schools. Secondary education spans at least 3 years, with differentiated general and vocational tracks. Since 2012, a 4+4+4 system divides primary and secondary education into three four-year stages, including religious Imam Hatip schools.

Higher education comprises public and private universities with growing international student populations, admission via national exams, and varying quality. Vocational education and apprenticeships support workforce needs. Religious education was excluded after 1927 but reintroduced variably since 1949. Foreign language education prioritizes English, with recent additions of other languages. Education incorporates strong nationalist and secular values, though criticized for politicization and bias, including Armenian genocide denial in curricula. Private schools include Turkish, minority, foreign, and international institutions.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education system is divided into compulsory primary school (grades 1–7), lower secondary (grades 8–10), and upper secondary school (grades 11–13). Preschool is widely available and partly free from age three. Education reforms standardized curricula and grading systems, emphasizing equal access and quality. Upper secondary education offers general and vocational paths, with most students enrolled.

Higher education includes universities and university colleges offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, supported by financial aid and quality assurance bodies. Private “free schools” funded publicly have increased since 1992, fostering diversity amid debates on inequality. Grades from A to F are used, with national tests at key grades. Norway ranks near OECD averages on PISA, with concerns about violence and integration of immigrants in schools.

# Education in Iran
Iran's educational history traces back to ancient Persia with mixed Islamic and modern reforms. The current system features compulsory elementary and lower secondary education, with upper secondary schooling optional. Education is highly centralized under the Ministry of Education, which oversees curriculum and teacher training. After the 1979 revolution, Islamic values were incorporated, with gender segregation and Islamized textbooks.

The system is structured into pre-primary, elementary (grades 1–6), lower secondary (7–9), upper secondary (10–12), technical-vocational, and tertiary education including bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. Literacy programs have improved enrollment and female participation, with women comprising a majority of university students. Private school growth and teacher shortages are significant issues. English is the primary foreign language taught, with recent inclusion of French and Arabic. Distance education and language programs expand access. Controversies involve political indoctrination, curricular bias, and unequal educational access.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia maintains a compulsory free 12-year education system divided into primary, middle, and high school phases, ending with national certification exams. Schools include public (majority) and private, both regulated by the Ministry of Education. The curriculum blends global trends with Armenian culture, and private schools provide varied educational programs subject to licensing standards.

Higher education institutions include state universities and private universities, many aligned with European frameworks like the Bologna Process and Erasmus programs. Armenia faces challenges with student absenteeism linked to socioeconomic factors. Technology-based educational centers and international schools have been developed to enhance skills and access. Armenian education emphasizes culture, language, and scientific disciplines, with ongoing reforms to improve quality and equity.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan’s education system features compulsory 12-year education from elementary through junior high, followed by senior high or vocational tracks, with national exams determining progression. Kindergarten is optional, and schools operate on a semester system. Mandarin is the primary medium, with English taught from grade 3; schools also offer Taiwanese, Hakka, and Putonghua classes.

Senior high schools differentiate liberal arts and science tracks, including skill-based vocational education. Higher education comprises public and private universities, junior colleges, and institutes of technology, offering degrees from associate to doctoral levels. Private cram schools and English immersion preschools are popular. International programs and teacher in-service training promote educational quality. The schooling system balances rigorous testing with expanding access to diverse educational opportunities.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education system evolved from Islamic pesantren and Dutch colonial models with stratified schooling based on ethnicity and social status. The current system mandates 12 years of education, divided into primary, junior, and senior secondary levels with academic and vocational tracks. Religious schools coexist with secular public schools, reflecting the country’s diversity.

Early education includes largely private pre-schools. Secondary education offers general and vocational programs, with Islamic madrasas integrating religious and secular content. Higher education includes universities, institutes, and academies across academic, vocational, and professional education aligned with national qualification frameworks. Challenges include teacher shortages, funding disparities, and regional inequality. Pedagogy often emphasizes rote learning, though reforms seek to broaden curricula.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system follows the 9-3-4 model: 6 years primary, 3 years junior secondary, 3 years senior secondary, and 4 years tertiary education. Primary education began universalizing in 1976; secondary education offers academic, technical, and vocational tracks. Federal, state, and private schools coexist with varying quality and resources. Exams like SSCE and NECO govern progression.

Tertiary education is expanding, with federal, state, and private universities serving over a million students. Vocational education remains vital but faces challenges in funding and quality. Informal education and adult literacy programs help address educational gaps, though funding and political issues hinder progress. Female education has improved but faces cultural and security challenges. Illiteracy and uneven access remain critical concerns despite government and NGO interventions.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education traces to ancient institutions like the “House of Life,” evolving through Islamic madrasas to modern French-influenced reforms under Muhammad Ali. The system includes compulsory basic education (ages 4–14), secondary tracks (general and technical), and extensive higher education with public and private universities.

Education is centralized but faces quality challenges, including underqualified teachers, reliance on rote learning, and inequities linked to wealth and geography. Private tutoring is widespread due to public system limitations. Al-Azhar provides a parallel religious education system. Recent reforms aim to improve access and technical education, but issues such as high youth unemployment persist. International schools and diverse curricula coexist with the national system.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland offers compulsory education from ages 6 to 16 or after three years of secondary education. The system includes preschool, primary (8 years), junior cycle (3 years), optional Transition Year, and senior cycle (2 years) culminating in the Leaving Certificate. Most schools are state-funded but predominantly under Catholic patronage, with a growing number of multidenominational and Gaelscoileanna (Irish-language schools).

The curriculum emphasizes holistic child development and includes various examinations. Secondary education offers a broad subject range with options for academic and vocational pathways. Special needs education and programs addressing disadvantaged areas exist. Ireland’s education ranks well internationally, supported by policies promoting equity and continuous investment. International students are attracted to Irish institutions.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education system, shaped by ancient Buddhist monastic traditions and colonial influences, comprises primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate, and tertiary levels. Education is free and compulsory up to grade 9, with literacy high and a network of government, private, religious, and international schools.

Tertiary education is competitive, with admissions based on advanced-level exams and limited university places; private universities remain limited. Vocational education training and non-formal adult education programs augment formal education. Tuition classes are widespread. The system faces criticism for rigidity and competitiveness. Administrative divisions across several ministries have caused inefficiencies. Recent policies addressed student leadership training and bolstered specialized education.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system consists of primary, middle, and high school with four main tracks: state secular, state religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab schools. Education is compulsory from kindergarten to 12th grade, but disparities exist, especially in the Arab and Haredi sectors, with lower matriculation rates and resource allocation. Efforts are ongoing to enhance access and inclusivity.

Higher education is extensive, with public universities requiring matriculation certificates and psychometric tests for admission. Israel ranks highly in tertiary education attainment globally and has notable universities in STEM fields. The Arab sector receives less funding and faces infrastructure shortages. Gender disparities exist in fields of study and faculty representation. Teacher quality and pay remain concerns, and textbooks occasionally face criticism for bias. Frequent strikes occur in the education sector. Education is key to Israel’s economic development and societal mobility.
