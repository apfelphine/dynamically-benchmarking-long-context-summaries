# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system spans from primary to tertiary levels, overseen by various governmental bodies like the Universal Basic Education Commission. Primary education lasts six years, focusing on core subjects and indigenous languages, with compulsory Common Entrance Exams for secondary admission. Secondary education follows the 6-3-3-4 system (six years primary, three years junior secondary, three years senior secondary, four years tertiary), with federal, state, and private schools differing in funding and quality. Federal “unity schools” admit via merit exams and are highly regarded, while state schools often face budget and staffing challenges. Private schools tend to offer better resources and instruction but at high costs.

Examinations include the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) and the General Certificate of Education (GCE), conducted by WAEC and NECO. Advanced-level programs like IJMB and JUPEB provide university entry alternatives. Tertiary education comprises federal, state, and private universities with diverse courses. Vocational education is significant but hindered by outdated curriculum and resource constraints. Female education has improved but faces challenges from socio-cultural barriers and security issues; UNICEF initiatives aim to enhance girls’ schooling. Literacy rates remain low, especially in northern regions, with government programs targeting improvement, including a push for digital literacy by 2030.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland mandates schooling ages 5 to 16, with primary education typically starting at age 4-5 and lasting eight years, including junior and senior infants through sixth class. Primary education emphasizes holistic child development. Secondary education lasts up to six years, divided into Junior Cycle (three years), optional Transition Year, and Senior Cycle (two years), culminating in state examinations: the Junior Certificate and Leaving Certificate. Schools include voluntary (mostly religious), community colleges, comprehensive schools, and fee-paying “grind” schools; the Irish language features prominently in Gaelscoileanna.

Higher education is well-developed, with universities ranked globally and various pathways catering to special needs and disadvantaged students. The state provides supports like DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) to underprivileged areas. International students are drawn by quality education and historic prominence. Irish education has evolved towards inclusivity and expanding secular options amid ongoing debates about religious influence, especially in admissions at primary level.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan’s education system offers 12 years of compulsory education: six years elementary, three years junior high, and three years senior high or vocational high school. Early childhood and kindergarten are widespread but not compulsory. The secondary and vocational sectors prepare students for national entrance exams, which dictate university admission.

Higher education includes numerous public and private universities, many internationally ranked, with popular fields including engineering and sciences. Taiwan also hosts junior colleges and institutes of technology with associate to doctoral degrees. Private education and cram schools supplement formal schooling, emphasizing English and Chinese language proficiency. Teacher training is well-structured with regional centers supporting in-service education. Special education, adult education, and international programs complement the mainstream system.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education spans primary (grades 1-6), middle (7-9), and high school (10-12) with compulsory education through grade 12. Schools are divided into state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors, reflecting societal diversity. The Haredi sector has distinct curricula emphasizing Torah study with low secular subject engagement, causing social challenges.

Education is highly valued culturally, linked to socioeconomic mobility, with high tertiary attainment (46% adults). The matriculation (“Bagrut”) exams determine university eligibility; approximately 46% of students earn certificates. Higher education requires Bagrut and Psychometric Entrance Test scores, with significant government subsidies, especially for military veterans. Arab education, while legally supported, faces resource disparities, lower matriculation rates, and recent affirmative action initiatives. Gender disparities exist with females generally outperforming males academically but underrepresented in STEM and senior academic roles. The system faces issues such as teacher strikes, budget constraints, and ideological controversies around textbooks and curricular content.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education system has deep historical roots from the Sasanian era and saw modernization waves during the Safavid and Qajar periods. The Pahlavi era and post-1979 Islamic Revolution introduced centralized, sex-segregated, and Islamized education. Primary and secondary education is compulsory and free with multiple branches: theoretical, technical-vocational, and manual skills. Higher education expanded dramatically post-revolution, regulated mainly by a national entrance exam (“Konkur”), and includes many prestigious universities and specialized institutions. Women’s participation in higher education now exceeds men’s, despite ongoing socio-cultural challenges.

Vocational and informal education are important amid recent reforms promoting research, entrepreneurship, and hybrid learning models. Problems include teacher shortages, inequalities between public and private schools, politicization of curricula, and increased privatization with rising education costs. Foreign languages, especially English and recently French, are taught in schools, but quality varies.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education extends from primary through tertiary levels and is overseen by multiple ministries. The system evolved from ancient Buddhist and colonial missionary schools, becoming free and universal post-independence. Primary education lasts five years; junior and senior secondary stages prepare students for national exams (G.C.E. O/L and A/L), which serve as university entrance qualifiers. Most schools are government-run, with national, provincial, and semi-government types; private and international schools also exist.

Tertiary education is competitive, largely state-funded, with limited private university options. Vocational education and training are supported through national frameworks targeting both youth and adults, including community-based programs. Criticisms include an overly competitive and rigid system, resistance to private universities, and issues around compulsory undergraduate leadership training (later abolished). Private tuition is widespread and expanding, particularly in urban areas.

# Education in Turkey
Turkey reformed its education system extensively after the Republic’s founding, emphasizing secularism, national unity, and centralized oversight by the Ministry of National Education. Education is compulsory through primary and middle school, recently extended to 12 years (the “4+4+4” system: four years each of primary, middle, and high school). Pre-primary education is available but not compulsory. The curriculum includes core subjects with multiple language options.

Secondary education divides into general, vocational, and technical tracks. Higher education comprises public and private universities, evaluated by national and international standards (e.g., ABET for engineering). Private schools include Turkish, minority, foreign, and international types. Religious education was removed post-Republic, then reintroduced in schools since 1949.

Issues include frequent curriculum changes, political influence, controversial textbook content (e.g., Armenian genocide denial), and debates about education’s role in nationalism and secularism. Turkey is a growing hub for international students supported by government scholarships.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education spans ancient origins through Islamic, Ottoman, and modern reforms. The system is highly centralized and divided into basic education (kindergarten, six years primary, three years preparatory), secondary education (three years general or technical), and diverse tertiary institutions. Public education is largely free up to preparatory, with entry to higher education competitive via national exams. The Al-Azhar system operates a parallel religious schooling branch.

Despite broad access improvements, quality remains uneven, hindered by infrastructure, teacher training, and traditional rote learning. Private tutoring is widespread but costly, increasing inequities. Higher education has expanded rapidly but faces funding and governance challenges. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated distance education use. Egypt also operates large agricultural education programs and has multiple scientific and cultural institutions.

# Education in France
France has a centralized education system regulated by the Ministry of National Education, offering free, secular, and compulsory schooling from ages 6 to 16. The structure includes preschool, primary, collège (middle school, ages 11-15), and lycée (high school, ages 15-18), culminating in national exams like the baccalauréat. Private schools exist under contract or independently, with religious instruction allowed in some.

Higher education divides into public universities, grandes écoles (elite institutions), and private colleges. The Bologna Process governs degrees (Licence, Master, Doctorat), with competitive preparatory classes for grandes écoles. Tuition fees are low for domestic/EU students but higher for internationals. France hosts many international schools and maintains a strong research infrastructure separate from universities.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education is shaped by Islamic boarding schools (pesantren), Dutch colonial systems, Japanese occupation influences, and post-independence national reforms. Education is compulsory for 12 years, divided into six years primary, three junior high, and three senior high or vocational school. Early childhood education includes playgroups and kindergartens, mostly private.

Public schools and religious schools (under the Ministry of Religious Affairs) coexist, with secular national curriculum emphasizing Pancasila (national ideology). Vocational and specialization tracks exist, including diverse Islamic schools: pesantren, madrasah, and sekolah islam. The higher education system includes universities, institutes, academies, and polytechnics offering academic, vocational, and professional education according to a national qualification framework.

Challenges include teacher shortages in remote areas, uneven access, and quality disparities. Cram schools (buxiban) supplement formal education heavily.

# Education in England
England mandates education or training from ages 5 to 18, with a structured system including foundation stages, primary (Key Stages 1-2), secondary (Key Stages 3-4), and post-16 education (Key Stage 5). Types of schools include comprehensive, grammar, academies, free schools, and private independent schools. The National Curriculum prescribes balanced subjects with national examinations like GCSEs and A-levels.

Further education colleges, apprenticeships, and T Levels provide diverse pathways post-16. Higher education includes universities offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, with tuition fees payable by many students. The Office for Students regulates access; quality is monitored by Ofsted and the QAA.

Adult education and lifelong learning are robust, with provisions like the Open University. England ranks highly in international assessments, with ongoing investment in school funding and educational equality.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia boasts near-universal literacy with a 12-year free compulsory education system divided into primary (grades 1-4), middle/basic (grades 5-9), and high school (grades 10-12) which splits into general and specialized tracks. Public schools dominate; private schools complement the system with varying resources.

Higher education includes 26 state universities and numerous private institutions, aligned with European frameworks like Bologna and engaged in Erasmus programmes. Entrance to higher education is standardized via exams, but rising absenteeism and dropout rates linked to child labor remain concerns.

Recent expansions in technology-based education centers aim to equip youth with modern skills. Education policy dialogues with the EU support ongoing reforms and quality assurance efforts.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education system has evolved from informal pre-colonial learning through colonial missionary and British systems to a nationally centralized framework comprising basic (kindergarten to junior high), secondary, and tertiary education. Education is primarily English-medium with free, compulsory basic education.

Basic education (12 years) includes core subjects and no certification at primary end; junior high ends with BECE exams. Secondary education offers academic senior high school (SHS) with elective streams and vocational training, culminating in WASSCE exams. Tertiary education growth includes universities, polytechnics, and colleges; admission is competitive and influenced by socioeconomic disparities.

Challenges include teacher shortages, quality gaps, underdeveloped vocational training, and equitable access. Government-private partnerships and ICT integration efforts are underway to address deficits. Adult literacy programs and non-formal education target gender and regional inequities.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education system includes compulsory elementary (grades 1-7, ages 6-12) and lower secondary school (grades 8-10, ages 13-16), followed by upper secondary education (videregående skole, ages 16-19). Education is primarily public and free, with private schools limited and regulated. Reform efforts emphasize equal access and the integration of vocational and general tracks. Upper secondary school offers pathways for university entrance or vocational apprenticeship.

Higher education includes universities and university colleges accredited by NOKUT. Grades use a letter system aligned with the Bologna Process at tertiary level. Special education integrates students in mainstream schools wherever possible.

The academic year spans August to June with scheduled breaks, and Norwegian education emphasizes social inclusion and adaptation to individual student needs.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education system combines a history of Chinese vernacular schools and British colonial influence. It features kindergarten, six-year primary, and six-year secondary education, with the latter divided into junior and senior stages culminating in the HKDSE examination. English and Cantonese (with increased use of Mandarin) are mediums of instruction, with some prestigious English-medium schools. Higher education is competitive and includes eight publicly funded universities and various private institutions.

Recent reforms emphasize active learning, integration of AI, and enhanced cooperation with mainland China. Challenges include political influence on curricula, emigration of students and teachers, and debates over national security education. International and private schools offering foreign curricula are prominent, catering mostly to expatriates and affluent locals.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden offers compulsory education from ages 6 to 16 through a comprehensive school system (grundskola), followed by voluntary upper secondary school (gymnasieskola). The curriculum mandates core subjects and language learning, with national testing at grades 3, 6, and 9.

Upper secondary education provides preparatory and vocational programs, with eligibility based on prior passing grades. Higher education conforms to Bologna Process cycles with bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees offered by universities and colleges. Independently run “free schools” (friskolor) coexist with public schools, funded per pupil but attracting debate over for-profit motives.

Grading uses A–F scales in compulsory levels and various criteria-referenced systems in tertiary. Sweden invests in inclusive education and student aid. Recent concerns include test integrity and educational inequality related to immigration. Sweden maintains a high global early childhood education ranking.
