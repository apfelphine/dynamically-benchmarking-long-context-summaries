# Global Education Systems: A Comparative Overview

## Introduction
The provided text outlines the educational landscapes of eight distinct nations: Nigeria, Ireland, Taiwan, Israel, Iran, Sri Lanka, Ghana, Norway, and Sweden. While each system reflects unique historical, cultural, and political contexts, they share common goals of fostering national development, ensuring literacy, and preparing students for the workforce. However, significant disparities exist regarding access, quality, funding, and equity.

## Nigeria
Nigeria's education system is characterized by a mix of federal, state, and private institutions, with the Universal Basic Education (UBE) policy making primary and junior secondary schooling free and compulsory. The curriculum follows a 6-3-3-4 structure (Primary, Junior Secondary, Senior Secondary, Tertiary). Challenges include severe teacher shortages, particularly in rural areas, corruption, and a lack of infrastructure. Secondary education is stratified into elite federal schools, underfunded state schools, and expensive private institutions. Vocational education exists but suffers from outdated curricula and low prestige. Literacy rates remain low, especially in the North, driven by poverty, insecurity, and cultural factors.

## Ireland
Ireland operates a highly decentralized system where parents have the constitutional right to choose between state, private, and home schooling. The system is dominated by religious institutions, historically Catholic, though reforms have increased diversity. Compulsory education lasts until age 16. The curriculum is standardized nationally but allows local flexibility. Ireland consistently ranks highly in international assessments like PISA, particularly in reading literacy. Special needs education is well-regulated, and there are specific initiatives to support disadvantaged students through the Deis scheme.

## Taiwan
Taiwan's system features a strict, centralized approach with a focus on discipline and high academic achievement. Compulsory education spans 12 years. Public schools emphasize rote learning and preparation for national entrance exams, which are highly competitive. The system has evolved to include vocational tracks and skill-based high schools alongside traditional academic paths. Universities are state-controlled, though private institutions have gained prominence. Engineering and technology are heavily supported by government policy.

## Israel
Israel's education system is unique due to its diverse religious and ethnic population, creating four distinct tracks: secular, religious, Haredi (ultra-Orthodox), and Arab. There is a strong societal emphasis on higher education as a primary route to economic success, resulting in some of the world's highest university enrollment rates. The Haredi sector faces significant challenges regarding workforce participation and academic integration. The Arab sector struggles with resource disparities and lower pass rates compared to Jewish sectors.

## Iran
Iran's education system has undergone major transformations, particularly following the 1979 Islamic Revolution, which Islamized the curriculum and segregated schooling by gender. While primary education is free and compulsory, secondary and tertiary education face challenges including teacher shortages, a rigid centralized bureaucracy, and political interference. Women's enrollment in higher education is exceptionally high, often exceeding male enrollment. The system produces a large number of engineers and scientists, though vocational training remains underdeveloped compared to formal higher education.

## Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's education system is deeply influenced by its history and religion, with Buddhist *pirivenas* historically serving as centers of learning. The current system is centralized, with state universities offering free but highly competitive education. A significant gap exists between the elite state system and private/international schools. Vocational education is managed separately but faces challenges in quality and recognition. The system is marked by intense competition for university places, leading to high dropout rates and a reliance on private tutoring.

## Ghana
Ghana's system is undergoing rapid expansion following independence, aiming for free and compulsory universal basic education. It is divided into basic, secondary, and tertiary levels. While enrollment has increased significantly, challenges persist regarding teacher quality, funding, and the disparity between public and private sectors. Vocational education is informal and often lacks accreditation. The government relies heavily on private institutions and international donors to fill the gap in public service delivery.

## Norway
Norway's system is known for its egalitarian principles, free education, and strong emphasis on environmental and social studies. Compulsory education covers grades 1-10. The upper secondary system (*videregående skole*) offers a choice between general and vocational tracks. Education is highly centralized with strong municipal support. Recent reforms have focused on increasing student choice and integrating IT, though debates continue regarding performance metrics and the role of independent (private) schools.

## Sweden
Sweden features a comprehensive nine-year compulsory school system followed by an upper secondary level (*gymnasieskola*) with a high enrollment rate. The system emphasizes equality, student autonomy, and a broad curriculum including extensive language learning. Grading is based on a letter system (A-F) with a focus on individual development plans rather than competitive ranking. Higher education is free for EU citizens and incorporates the Bologna Process. Independent schools (*friskolor*) are a growing sector funded by municipalities, sparking debates over standards and profit motives.
