# Comparative Summary of Education Systems around the World

---

## Africa

### Nigeria
Education begins with compulsory primary school at age five, followed by six years of primary and three years of junior secondary, forming the Universal Basic Education (UBE) system. Secondary education consists of three years in senior secondary school or technical colleges. Federal, state, and private secondary schools differ in funding, staff, and reputation, with private schools often considered elite but costly. Vocational and informal education are widespread, though challenged by underfunding, poor policies, and staff shortages. Literacy rates remain low, but efforts by the government and NGOs target improvements, especially in digital literacy and access for vulnerable groups. Female education is progressing but faces barriers like early marriage and insecurity, with programs such as UNICEF's Girls' Education Project supporting enrollment and retention. Tertiary education is extensive, with over 150 universities and multiple specialized institutions; admissions are competitive and tiered. Advanced-level entry such as IJMB and JUPEB provide alternatives to university. International and literacy education face funding gaps and disparities, particularly in rural Northern regions.

### Ghana
Historically, education evolved from informal, missionary-led efforts to formal systems under colonial rule. Compulsory free basic education lasts 12 years, with high enrollment but disparities in quality, access, and gender equity. Secondary and tertiary education have expanded, but vocational training faces challenges in infrastructure and perception. Teacher training is standardized through dedicated colleges. Financing is shared by government, private institutions, and international donors. Despite increased infrastructure, literacy and adult education programs must address socio-economic and geographic inequalities.

## Europe

### Republic of Ireland
Education is compulsory from ages 6 to 16, structured into primary, secondary (junior/senior cycles), and tertiary levels. The vast majority of schools have a religious patronage, but reforms are increasing non-denominational options. Secondary education culminates in state exams—the Junior Certificate and Leaving Certificate—with high rates of completion and performance in EU rankings. Special needs and disadvantaged areas are supported by targeted programs and funding. Higher education is competitive and globally respected, attracting international students; private and fee-paying schools exist beside the state sector.

### England
Education is compulsory from ages 5–18 and divided into primary, secondary (with Key Stages), and post-sixteen education through sixth forms, colleges, or apprenticeships. State schools are mostly comprehensive, though selective grammar and faith schools persist. The national curriculum mandates core subjects with standardized exams (GCSEs, A-levels), while higher education quality is high and university admissions are competitive, affected by funding and fee structures. Private schools serve about 7% of pupils and homeschooling is allowed. Emphasis is on inclusion, academic standards, and vocational pathways.

### France
The centralized, secular French education system is regulated by the Ministry of National Education. Primary schooling begins at age 3–6 (preschool) and is followed by elementary, middle, and high school. Secondary education offers academic or vocational routes, with the baccalauréat as a key qualification for higher education. Higher education includes universities and selective grandes écoles, with tuition largely state-funded. Private schools exist but must adhere to national standards. Reforms promote equity and teacher qualifications are highly regulated; religious instruction is generally excluded from state schools.

### Sweden & Norway
Both countries have comprehensive publicly funded systems. Sweden mandates nine years of schooling, with a course- and grade-based selection for upper secondary and university entry; independent schools are permitted under strict regulations. Norway provides universal and compulsory grundskole before upper secondary, with teacher training and higher education regulated and free. Both countries have experienced reforms aimed at inclusivity, IT integration, and equity; grading systems focus on achievement rather than ranking. Special education is integrated into public schools, and higher education institutions are largely state-run.

## Middle East & Asia

### Israel
The system is tiered and divided into state-secular, state-religious, Arab, and Haredi tracks, with compulsory education through high school and a strong societal emphasis on academics. High school students take bagrut matriculation exams required for university entry; access and quality in the Arab sector remain uneven. Higher education is well-respected globally, with government-subsidized universities and high attainment rates among the Jewish population. Persistent challenges include funding disparities, gender gaps, and large class sizes.

### Iran
Education is highly centralized and free, with primary and lower secondary compulsory. Reforms have increased emphasis on research, vocational pathways, and gender equity, and enrollment at the tertiary level is high, especially for women. The government funds education generously, though regional and rural disparities, teacher shortages, and privatization issues persist. Higher education is competitive and public universities dominate, with admissions based on national entrance exams.

## Asia-Pacific

### Indonesia
Education is compulsory for 12 years, with a history rooted in both Islamic and Western traditions. The system includes foundational primary and secondary schooling, vocational streams, and extensive religious Islamic schooling (pesantren, madrasah) complementing secular options. Higher education includes universities, polytechnics, and academies, with admissions based on performance and national tests. Challenges include staff shortages, infrastructure gaps, and urban-rural inequalities, though ongoing reforms address quality, equity, and teacher training.

### Hong Kong
The system blends Chinese and British influences, with compulsory education from six to fifteen, followed by competitive secondary and university admissions. The medium of instruction includes both English and Cantonese, with international schools catering primarily to expatriates. Political developments have led to curriculum changes and an emphasis on patriotism. Higher education is competitive and highly ranked; technological adoption (AI, e-learning) is advancing. Adult education and vocational training receive government support, and special/needy students are integrated.

### Taiwan
Education is compulsory for 12 years from elementary through high school; schools are academically rigorous, and entrance exams dictate access to further education. Multiple tracks exist in secondary and vocational education, with a strong emphasis on STEM and engineering reflecting government priorities. Higher education is highly accessible and modeled after Western systems, with a proliferation of universities and technical colleges, and a robust vocational sector. Private and supplementary cram schools are common due to exam pressures.

## Middle East & North Africa

### Egypt
The system is centralized, comprising compulsory basic education (kindergarten to preparatory), three years of secondary school, and extensive higher education. Historical progressions include religious, colonial, and socialist reforms leading to wide access but persistent gaps in quality, infrastructure, teacher training, and equity. Technical and vocational education is expanding, and private and international schools augment public provision. Challenges include high youth unemployment among graduates, disparities in educational outcomes based on wealth and region, and widespread reliance on private tutoring.

---

## Eastern Europe

### Armenia
Boasts near universal literacy and a robust public education system with compulsory secondary education. Education reforms post-independence have focused on national identity, language, and quality assurance aligned with European standards. Private schools exist but are limited in access. Higher education includes universities and specialized institutions, with international cooperation and modernization. Disparities in rural and marginalized populations persist, and student absenteeism is a concern.

---

## Turkey
Compulsory education lasts 12 years in a 4+4+4 format. The Ministry of National Education controls curriculum and school openings, with both public and private schools present. Secondary education includes academic and vocational streams, with standardized, competitive entrance exams for both high school and university admission. Higher education is provided by numerous state and private universities, with some internationally accredited programs. Challenges include frequent reforms, political influence, quality disparities, and linguistic and religious issues in curriculum and instruction.

---

## Summary of Trends and Challenges

Across countries, education systems face persistent issues regarding equitable access, funding, teacher qualifications, regional disparities, and balancing academic rigor with vocational and practical skills. Reforms commonly target inclusion, digital literacy, decentralization, and responses to societal, economic, and political changes. Internationalization, technological innovation, and alternative pathways (vocational, adult, informal education) are increasingly prominent, as systems aim to prepare youth for modern economies and diverse societies.
