**Updated Integrated Summary (Incorporating All Chunks, including Chunk 45):**

Education systems across countries vary significantly in structure, access, quality, and equity. In **Nigeria**, a 9-3-4 system provides free, compulsory primary and junior secondary education under the Universal Basic Education (UBE), with students required to pass the SSCE (WAEC/NECO) for university entry. Secondary education diverges: federal schools are well-resourced and merit-based, while state schools face budget and staffing challenges; private schools offer better facilities at high costs. Tertiary education includes 153 institutions (40 federal, 45 state, 68 private), with growing private approvals since 2021. Entry requires SSCE credits, JAMB scores, or NCE/ND qualifications; undergraduate programs last 4–6 years. Vocational education (NABTEB) is underfunded and outdated. Literacy stands at 69% (2022), with rural and female education facing barriers. The government targets 95% digital literacy by 2030.

**Ireland** offers a structured, high-performing system from early childhood to tertiary education. Primary education is compulsory from age 6 with a national curriculum; secondary includes Junior and Leaving Certificates. Most university courses are in English, with strong performance in reading and math. Special needs education is well-supported, and disadvantaged schools receive targeted aid.

**Taiwan** features a centralized, disciplined 12-year compulsory system, with academic progression from kindergarten to senior high school. A 3+3 high school model enables direct university entry. Private education is prominent, with large cram schools. Special education services are available, and the system emphasizes discipline and national identity.

**Israel** operates a three-tier system with state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab tracks. Haredi schools offer limited secular education. Education is highly valued, with 46% of 25–64-year-olds holding degrees. STEM excellence drives economic growth and global university rankings. Disparities exist in the Arab sector, marked by lower funding and higher dropout rates. The Bagrut exam is critical for university and military entry.

**Iran** has a centralized, Islamic-reform-oriented system with free, compulsory education up to Grade 9. Students must pass the *Konkur* exam for university entry. Gender parity is strong, with women outnumbering men in universities. STEM graduates are among the world’s top (5th highest), 70% of engineering graduates are women. The system faces political control, including mandatory prayer sessions and protest restrictions. Hybrid learning and private education are growing.

**Sri Lanka** follows a five-tier system ending at Grade 9. G.C.E. O/Ls and A/Ls are key university entrance exams. The system includes 10,155 government schools (373 national, 9,782 provincial, 561 Pirivenas) and 104 private schools. State universities (15) offer free undergraduate education but are highly competitive (~16% admission rate). Limited opportunities drive many students abroad. Vocational education is managed by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, with a non-formal program for dropouts. Opposition to private universities persists due to quality concerns.

**Turkey** features a "4+4+4" primary-to-middle school model, with secondary education lasting 3 years (Grades 9–12). Students take a national exam for university entry. A standard diploma eliminates track-based advantages. Vocational education includes technical, trade, and religious schools. Higher education includes 167 universities (public and private), with growing industry partnerships and recognition of engineering programs comparable to U.S. standards. The curriculum includes state-sanctioned narratives on the 1915 Armenian deportations, portraying them as Ottoman actions. Minority language education (e.g., Hebrew) is inconsistently protected; French instruction declined, and religious minority education was restricted post-1923.

**Egypt** has a highly centralized system with free public education expanded since 1952, achieving high access (96.9% primary enrollment), though quality remains poor. It ranks poorly in international assessments (49th in reading, 34th in math) due to high student-to-teacher ratios, rote learning, and inadequate infrastructure. Basic education (ages 4–14) is compulsory and free, with disparities between wealthy and poor students. Secondary education offers general and technical tracks; technical training is enhanced by Applied Technology Schools. Higher education is extensive but faces governance, funding, and quality issues—only ~50% of entrants graduate. Reforms (e.g., Higher Education Enhancement Project, 2025 Japan-inspired engineering diploma) aim to improve autonomy and outcomes. Egyptian universities show gradual improvement in global rankings (e.g., Cairo University at 350th, American University in Cairo at 410th in QS 2025). The system includes international schools (offering British, American, IB, or French/German curricula), the Al-Azhar system (religious, Muslim-only), and agricultural education programs. Despite reforms, public education faces low quality, teacher shortages, low salaries, widespread private tutoring (58% of families), examination leakage, and inequities—leading to high youth unemployment (36.1%).

**France** established a secular, mandatory, free public education system under Jules Ferry, emphasizing national identity. Mandatory schooling runs from age 6 to 16, with standardized curricula and oversight by regional académies. Primary education begins at age 3 with preschool (maternelle), progressing to elementary school (CP–CM2). Middle and high school includes *collège* (ages 11–15) and *lycée* (ages 15–18), culminating in the *baccalauréat* (general, technological, or professional) or *CAP* (vocational). Most private schools follow the national curriculum ("under contract"); others use private curricula with religious instruction. International schools (105 as of 2015) offer alternative curricula. Higher education includes public universities, IUTs (technical institutes), and prestigious *grandes écoles*—highly selective and focused on specific fields. Public universities follow the Bologna Process with structured degree programs (Licence, Master, Doctorat). Admission to elite *CPGE* preparatory classes (e.g., sciences or humanities) is highly competitive (only 5% accepted), with intense oral exams (*colles*). Teacher recruitment follows distinct pathways (INSPE, CAPES, Agrégation, *enseignants-chercheurs*). Public schools are secular (*laïcité*); religious instruction is banned except in Alsace and Moselle. The population is aging (youth under 25 at 32%), though birth rates are rising. Approximately 2.9 million students attend higher education.

**Indonesia** has a 12-year compulsory system with public and private (including Islamic) schools. The curriculum emphasizes Pancasila, though its influence has diminished since 1998. Public schools use rote learning and teacher-centered instruction, with instruction in Indonesian from Grade 3. Students progress through primary (6–12), middle (12–15), and high school (15–18), with academic and vocational tracks. Challenges persist in teacher shortages, equity, quality, and access—especially in remote areas. Islamic schools include *pesantren* (deep Islamic studies, varying ideologies), *madrassa* (blended Islamic and secular), and *sekolah islam*. Higher education includes academic, vocational, and professional institutions (*Universitas*, *Institut*, *Politeknik*, *Akademi*), with a national qualification framework (KKNI) defining degrees.

**England** has a state-funded system with 93% of children aged 3–18 attending free schools (academies, community, free, foundation, voluntary aided/controlled, UTCs). Schools follow the national curriculum, inspected by Ofsted. Most secondary schools are comprehensive, though selective grammar schools exist (via eleven plus). Faith-based schools are common, and students receive mandatory Relationship and Sex Education and Personal, Social, Health and Economic (PSHE) education. Compulsory education ends at age 16; students remain in education until 18 via A-levels, BTECs, T Levels, apprenticeships, or traineeships. Higher education includes universities, colleges, and private institutions. Bachelor’s degrees (honours) typically last three years; integrated master’s degrees are available in STEM. Tuition fees (capped at £9,535 annually from 2024–25) are repayable after graduation. Postgraduate programs include master’s and doctorates. Adult education includes access courses, distance learning, and vocational training. A national qualifications framework (RQF/FHEQ) enables clear progression. British 15-year-olds rank 13th globally in literacy, math, and science (average 503.7), with primary students ranked 4th in reading. The UK ranks 7th in the UN Education Index. City academies expanded in disadvantaged areas; 88% of schools were rated good or outstanding by 2015. Schools are now funded via a national formula, with increased budgets and £780 million allocated for Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND).

**Armenia** offers free, compulsory 12-year secondary education with 100% literacy. Preschool (ages 2–6/7) is widely available; public schools (97%) follow a national curriculum. Middle and high school offer broad or specialized education. Entrance exams determine university or vocational school admission. Private schools provide specialized programs and better facilities but are accessible only to wealthier families. Key institutions include Yerevan State University (founded 1920), Yerevan State Medical University, and private universities like the American University of Armenia. Armenia participates in European initiatives (Bologna Process, Erasmus+) and has a quality assurance system aligned with European standards. However, student absenteeism and dropout rates have risen sharply—rising from 1,531 (2002–2003) to 7,630 (2004–2005), due to child labor and poor education quality.

**Ghana** evolved from informal oral transmission to formal systems influenced by colonialism and independence-driven reforms. Key milestones include the Philip Quaque Boys School (1765) and the 1957 push for free, universal basic education. Major reforms in 1987 and 1992 introduced compulsory education (ages 6–14), vocational training, and decentralization. The system includes basic (12 years), secondary (academic or vocational), and tertiary education. Basic education ends with the BECE; secondary offers academic (SHS, 3 years, culminating in WASSCE) or vocational (TVET) tracks. Tertiary education has expanded, driven by private institutions, offering degrees from bachelor to PhD. Admission to colleges of education requires online application and payment. Grading uses numeric points (A1 to F9) with a 24-point cap for bachelor’s admissions. Vocational education faces low prestige and industry demand. Private education is prominent—74.7% of schools in Ga district are private—driven by public-private partnerships. The Ministry of Education oversees policy through GES, National Schools Inspectorate, and NCCA. Teacher training occurs at 46 Colleges of Education (Diploma in Basic Education), with supplementary options at universities and via distance learning. Adult literacy improved from 54% (1997) to 71.5% (2010) via the National Functional Literacy Programme (FNLP). TVET is prioritized to address unemployment but suffers from low completion rates, poor quality, inadequate infrastructure, and unqualified certifications. Access to tertiary education is increasingly selective, with socioeconomic status and gender significantly influencing enrollment. Women remain underrepresented in higher education due to cultural norms, early marriages, and gender-based barriers.

**Norway** has a comprehensive, equitable system with compulsory primary and lower secondary education (ages 6–16). It includes elementary (barneskole), lower secondary (ungdomsskole), and upper secondary (videregående skole, ages 16–19). Primary education emphasizes play-based learning; lower secondary introduces grading and electives. Students are graded based on performance and location, affecting upper secondary admissions. The 1994 "Reform 94" unified upper secondary into general and vocational tracks, facilitating smoother transitions. Students graduate as "russ" and celebrate before final exams. Educators include preschool teachers (bachelor’s), adjuncts (bachelor’s + pedagogy), and lecturers (master’s + pedagogy). Higher education (3+ years or more) requires a general university admissions certificate (generell studiekompetanse), including universities (theoretical), university colleges (applied/professional), and private institutions. There is no formal distinction between vocational and non-vocational higher education. Special education is integrated into local schools. The grading system uses a Bologna-hybrid scale (1–6), with final diplomas showing only whole numbers. Grades may include + or – for mid-term assessments. The academic year has two semesters (August–December, January–June), with summer, Christmas, fall, and winter breaks. Students are exempt from school on 13 national holidays. Higher education is free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens; student aid is provided by CSN. Anti-discrimination policies are enforced. Higher vocational education is distinct, offering practical training without Bologna compatibility. Independent schools (*friskolor*), publicly funded and open to all, have grown significantly and sparked debate over equity, grading integrity, and profit motives. Some research suggests improved performance, but critics argue standards have declined. Norway ranks highly in early childhood education and shows recovery in PISA and PIRLS scores, though concerns remain about testing validity and immigrant impact.

**Hong Kong** features compulsory primary and secondary education with public schooling free. Pre-school is not free, but government vouchers are available. The system includes a structured school year (Kindergarten to Secondary 6), with the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) as the main exam. Some schools offer the International Baccalaureate (IB) program. Vocational education is available through the Vocational Training Council (VTC), with plans to reduce secondary school duration to two years. International schools (175 listed by ISC, including 24 with UK curricula and 33 offering IB) are largely unregulated, with reputation driven by parent communities. Tuition fees range from HKD 216,500 (CIS) to HKD 360,000 (Li Po Chun), with private international schools increasingly enrolling Chinese students. The system transitioned from a selective, two-tiered structure (pre-2012) to a unified, three-year senior secondary system (post-2012), leading to the HKDSE. The English Schools Foundation offers subsidized English-medium education as an alternative to high-pressure local schooling. The system emphasizes discipline, with strict rules and reporting of misbehavior. Education is often described as "spoon-fed," prioritizing exam scores and rote learning over critical thinking. Class sizes have decreased due to low birth rates, sparking debate over small class teaching. Reforms aim to increase group work and open-ended tasks, though excessive homework and memorization remain concerns. Most university programs are taught in English, and admission is primarily through JUPAS. Key institutions include HKU, CUHK, HKUST, PolyU, CityU, HKBU, Lingnan, and EdUHK—many consistently ranked globally. Self-financing institutions and post-secondary colleges offer additional options. Adult and special education are growing, with efforts to integrate immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children into mainstream schools. A mix of Cantonese and Putonghua is used in Chinese language instruction, with growing use of Standard Mandarin.

**Sweden** has a comprehensive, equitable system with free, accessible preschool for children from age 1. Primary education (folkskola) is standardized, with a national curriculum introduced in 1919. The 9-year comprehensive school (grundskola) was introduced in 1962 and expanded with standardized grading from year 6. Students study 16–17 compulsory subjects, with grades determining eligibility for upper secondary school. National tests are administered in grades 3, 6, and 9, though leaks have led to policy changes. Upper secondary education (gymnasieskola) offers 18 specialized programs: two-thirds are preparatory (providing university eligibility), one-third vocational (job-specific training). Students take core courses (English, Swedish, math) and complete a diploma project (graded E or F, not counted in merit). Eligibility requires passing 12 subjects (for preparatory) or 8 (for vocational), with math, Swedish, and English required. Non-qualifiers may enter introductory programs. Higher education includes academic degrees (bachelor’s, master’s, PhD) aligned with the Bologna Process, as well as professional degrees and vocational training. International students must demonstrate proficiency in Swedish and English, often via the SweSAT test. Higher education is free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens; student aid is provided by CSN. Anti-discrimination policies are enforced. Higher vocational education is distinct and not Bologna-compatible. Independent schools (*friskolor*), publicly funded and open to all, have grown significantly and have sparked debate over equity, grading integrity, and profit motives. While some studies suggest improved performance, critics argue standards have declined. Sweden ranks highly in early childhood education and shows recovery in PISA and PIRLS scores, though concerns remain about testing validity and immigrant impact.
