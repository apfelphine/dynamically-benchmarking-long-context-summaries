# Summary of Education in Nigeria, the Republic of Ireland, Taiwan, Israel, Iran, Sri Lanka, Turkey, Egypt, France, Indonesia, England, Armenia, Ghana, Norway, Hong Kong, and Sweden

## Education in Nigeria  
Nigeria’s "6-3-3-4" system mandates nine years of free Universal Basic Education delivered through federal, state, private, religious, and international institutions. Vocational training struggles with relevance, and female education faces socio-economic barriers despite initiatives like UNICEF cash transfers. Literacy stands at 69%, with goals to raise digital literacy to 95% by 2030.

## Education in the Republic of Ireland  
Compulsory education spans ages 6 to 16 or three years of secondary education, transitioning from Catholic dominance to multi-denominational and Irish-language schools. Secondary education includes Junior and Senior Cycles with state exams, special needs support, and strong OECD literacy rankings, hosting prestigious universities.

## Education in Taiwan  
Taiwan’s 12-year system offers academic and vocational tracks emphasizing Mandarin, native languages, early English, and arts. Over 95% of high school graduates attend university. Teacher training integrates digital tools; special education employs combined models; Taiwan attracts many international students, especially from mainland China.

## Education in Israel  
Compulsory schooling until grade 12 serves diverse ethnic and religious groups via state-secular, religious, Haredi, Arab, and private schools. The Pupils’ Rights Law prohibits sectarian discrimination. Focus areas include social mobility and STEM amidst funding disparities, gender gaps, and teacher morale challenges.

## Education in Iran  
A centralized system combines Islamic and Western curricula with gender segregation since 1979. Women outnumber men in higher education. The system includes 6 years elementary, 3 years compulsory lower secondary, and 3 years optional upper secondary. University entry relies on competitive exams, with expanded hybrid and digital learning post-pandemic. Private Islamic Azad University offers alternatives amid political complexities.

## Education in Sri Lanka  
Free universal education emphasizes national languages and religion, governed by several ministries. Schooling is compulsory until 14; university entry depends on GCE A-Levels with district quotas. Vocational education is nationally regulated but constrained by rigidity, unregulated foreign degrees, and widespread private tuition, increasingly online.

## Education in Turkey  
Turkey’s secular "4+4+4" system mandates 12 years, with academic, vocational, and Imam Hatip religious tracks. University entry requires secondary graduation exams. The system includes 167 universities and European programs alongside private and minority schools. Religious education largely teaches Islam; curricula advance nationalist narratives denying the Armenian genocide. Minority-language education declined amid restrictions. Frequent reforms and textbook controversies encourage private schooling.

## Education in Egypt  
Free, compulsory education from ages 4–14 offers general and technical secondary tracks alongside private international and Al-Azhar religious schools. Agricultural education links theory and practice. Higher education serves 30% of youth via public and private institutions facing accreditation and research challenges. COVID-19 accelerated distance learning. Issues include uneven quality, costly private tutoring (61% of education spending), low teacher pay and qualifications, corporal punishment, enrollment disparities, and youth unemployment linked to inadequate vocational training.

## Education in France  
Compulsory, free, secular education from age 3 spans primary to lycée, ending with the baccalauréat or vocational CAP. Private schools often religious and state-contracted serve 18 million students, including 2.9 million in higher education. Aligned with the Bologna Process, the system includes universities, vocational institutes, Catholic private universities, foreign branches, and elite Grandes écoles. Teachers have high qualifications; university staff engage in research. Laïcité enforces strict secularism, banning conspicuous religious symbols since 2004 (except in Alsace-Moselle).

## Education in Indonesia  
Twelve years of compulsory schooling cover academic, vocational, and domestic streams. Public and private religious schools—including pesantren and madrassas—integrate secular and religious curricula but generally lag in quality. Despite high primary enrollment, secondary participation falls, with only 36.3% in tertiary education. Challenges include funding, equity, teacher shortages, and ongoing reforms. Higher education comprises universities, institutes, polytechnics, and academies aligned with national qualification frameworks.

## Education in England  
Compulsory education spans ages 5 to 18, with diverse state-funded schools (academies, faith schools, selective schools) and a 7% private sector. The national curriculum includes Relationship and Sex Education and broad subjects at Key Stage 4. Post-16 education is compulsory, featuring A-levels, BTECs, NVQs, and T Levels. Higher education hosts prestigious universities with capped tuition fees and student loans. Lifelong adult education is robust, aligned with European frameworks. England performs well internationally, with improved outcomes in disadvantaged areas and increased funding for special educational needs.

## Education in Armenia  
Armenia achieved 100% literacy by 1960. Post-independence reforms emphasize Armenian language and culture, with Russian and English widely taught. Preschool starts at age 2; compulsory free secondary education spans grades 1–12 in general and specialized tracks. Public schools follow national curricula. Higher education includes state and private institutions aligned with European standards and quality assurance. Challenges include absenteeism and dropouts partly linked to child labor. Non-formal education centers and technology hubs enhance youth digital and civic skills.

## Education in Ghana  
Ghana’s education evolved from colonial informal and missionary systems to free, compulsory education emphasizing practical skills and equity reforms. The 20-year system spans kindergarten to undergraduate studies, with 79% literacy and near gender parity. Basic education is compulsory and free for 12 years, culminating in exams leading to Senior High academic or vocational tracks. TVET faces low prestige, poor infrastructure, and dominance of informal apprenticeships despite initiatives like STEP (2005) and COTVET (2006). Secondary enrollment growth strains selective tertiary admissions, with rural, female, and low-income students underrepresented despite scholarships. ICT policies mandate digital integration focusing on basic skills. Governance involves Ministry of Education and decentralized agencies, funded by government, private sector, and declining donor aid. Teacher training includes colleges and universities with distance options, while adult literacy targets rural and female populations. A national service year and international schools offering IB and IGCSE curricula supplement the system.

## Education in Norway  
Norway’s system, compulsory until 16, covers elementary (ages 6–12), lower secondary (13–16), and upper secondary (16–19) levels with broad curricula, graded assessments from lower secondary onward, and two semesters annually. Upper secondary combines general and vocational tracks with apprenticeships; the 2017 graduation rate was 73%. Grading blends traditional scales and Bologna frameworks, with teacher-assigned final grades and random exams. Vacations include multiple terms and 13 national holidays. Educators hold bachelor’s to master’s degrees. Higher education includes universities, colleges, and private specialized schools quality-assured by NOKUT. Special education shifted to inclusive models since the 1970s, mandated also at university level.

## Education in Hong Kong  
Hong Kong’s bilingual, bicultural system evolved from British missionary and village schools, with key institutions like Queen’s College (1862). Post-1997 reforms promote patriotism, replacing Liberal Studies with Citizenship and Social Development, and require mainland China field trips for subsidized students. Education stages include voluntary preschool (ages 3–6), compulsory primary (6 years), and secondary divided into junior (Forms 1–3) and senior (Forms 4–6), mostly culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education; some schools offer the International Baccalaureate. Vocational education combines skills training and work experience via the Vocational Training Council and Hong Kong Institute of Vocational Education. The tertiary sector expanded to eight UGC-funded globally ranked universities plus public and self-financing institutions, with four-year undergraduate programs and centralized admissions (JUPAS).

Language instruction has shifted predominantly from English to Cantonese, with 20–30% of secondary schools remaining English-medium, associated with higher prestige and university access; Putonghua is increasingly used though Cantonese dominates. Special education is moving toward integration, supported by government programs for immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children.

School types include government-run, government-funded aided (often charitable/religious), Direct Subsidy Scheme schools with flexible curricula, caput schools funded per pupil, private schools teaching in English and Cantonese, privately run higher-fee international schools, and the English Schools Foundation (ESF) as a partially subsidized alternative with lower fees. Historically, half-day schooling with split sessions prevailed due to high demand but has largely shifted to full-day schools; declining birth rates have led to smaller classes, prompting discussions on the benefits of reduced class sizes to ease teacher workload. Discipline is emphasized per Education Bureau guidelines. Criticisms include an exam-focused culture reliant on rote memorization and textbooks despite reforms toward group work; concerns remain over workload and history teaching quality.

Internationalization includes 175 international schools (as of 2015) mainly using English-medium UK-based or IB curricula with growing enrollment from Hong Kong and mainland China, spanning a wide fee range; Li Po Chun UWC is among the costliest, and the French International School is uniquely accredited by France’s education ministry. MIT’s innovation node in Hong Kong fosters tertiary collaboration. In 2023, Hong Kong and Guangdong signed a cooperation framework enhancing education exchange in the Greater Bay Area, encompassing higher education, vocational training, and talent development.

## Education in Sweden  
Sweden’s education system includes free preschool from age three focusing on play and early learning, progressing to a 9-year compulsory comprehensive school covering 16 subjects with national tests; continued education requires passing key subjects. Upper secondary education (Gymnasieskola), compulsory to age 18, offers preparatory (academic) and vocational national programmes with competitive admission and diploma projects, enabling university entry. Higher education follows the Bologna Process with Bachelor’s, Master’s, Licentiate, Doctorate degrees, and degree options covering general academic and professional fields. Language proficiency in Swedish and English is required, with admissions aided by the SweSAT aptitude test. Student aid includes loans and grants; higher education is free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens. Anti-discrimination policies ensure fairness in admissions and exams. Higher vocational education, non-Bologna aligned, offers shorter programs with workplace experience.

Since 1992, independent schools (friskolor) publicly funded serve 10–30% of students, offering alternative pedagogies, international, religious, or special needs profiles without charging fees or discriminating. However, concerns include profit motives driving inequality and grade inflation, fueling political debates over regulation.

Sweden ranks highly for early childhood education per UNICEF (2008). PISA scores hover near OECD averages, experiencing declines (2006–2012) partly attributed to immigration effects, followed by modest recovery. Reading assessments (PIRLS) show similar trends. Controversies over flawed national testing led to investigations and political fallout, highlighting challenges in assessment and equity within a broadly inclusive and well-resourced system.
