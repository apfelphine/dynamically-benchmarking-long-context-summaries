# Education Systems Overview

## Nigeria, Ireland, Taiwan, Israel, Iran, and Sri Lanka  
Nigeria’s education system is transitioning from a 6-3-3-4 to a 9-3-4 structure, starting with six years of primary education under Universal Basic Education. Despite funding shortages, outdated curricula, and security challenges, adult literacy rates have improved markedly through digital literacy initiatives and efforts to reduce out-of-school rates. Ireland mandates education from ages 5 or 6 to 16 or beyond, with a strong focus on equity through programs like DEIS and broad access to higher education, primarily in English. Taiwan requires 12 years of compulsory education, featuring academic and vocational tracks, strong teacher training, and extensive private cram schools, though corporal punishment reportedly persists.

Israel’s diverse system accommodates secular, religious, Haredi, and Arab schools, aiming for equality despite funding and morale challenges; it boasts high tertiary attainment and above-average investment. Iran’s centralized system emphasizes religious curricula and gender segregation post-revolution, with over 70% of university students being women; challenges include dropout rates, teacher shortages, and inequalities linked to private education and political factors. Sri Lanka merges ancient Buddhist traditions with colonial legacies, providing multilingual compulsory education through Grade 9, with competitive university entry and competency-based vocational training; widespread tertiary access contrasts with graduate unemployment and reliance on private (now increasingly online) tutoring.

## Turkey, Egypt, France, Indonesia, England, and Armenia  
Turkey employs a centralized 4+4+4 system blending secular and religious education, recently reinstating mandatory Islamic instruction amid criticism over political bias and scientific curriculum reduction. Vocational and academic tracks prepare students for universities accessed via national exams. Egypt’s historically rich system mandates basic education from ages 4–14, with expanding tertiary education hindered by overcrowding and rote learning; private tutoring is common and digital learning expanded during the COVID-19 pandemic.

France operates a secular, centrally managed system from preschool to lycée, culminating in the baccalauréat or vocational CAP, with highly qualified teachers and strict secularism in schools. Ongoing debates surround privatization and religious freedom. Indonesia requires 12 years of education across secular and private Islamic schools governed by different ministries, facing challenges including rote learning, funding gaps, and declining enrollment. England mandates education from 5 to 18, featuring diverse school types, national curricula, and prestigious universities such as Oxford and Cambridge, with ongoing reforms targeting funding and equity.

Armenia provides 12 years of free education with a focus on national language and history through public schools, while private schools offer curricular diversity but raise inequality. Higher education aligns with European frameworks offering multilingual instruction. Challenges include absenteeism and dropouts related to child labor, addressed partly via rural skills training. Innovative technology-driven centers like the Tumo Center for Creative Technologies enhance digital literacy and creativity.

## Ghana, Norway, Hong Kong, and Sweden  
Ghana’s system blends public and a large private sector, with up to 75% private enrollment in some areas due to funding shortfalls. It offers 12 years of free basic education and technical training amid infrastructure and relevance challenges, with gender parity mostly achieved at basic and secondary levels. ICT integration progresses despite resource limits, and universities adapted to virtual learning during COVID-19.

Norway structures education into compulsory elementary and lower secondary (ages 6–16), followed by upper secondary (16–19) offering unified general and vocational tracks, featuring a "2+2" apprenticeship model. Emphasizing inclusion, balanced development, and digital integration, Norway maintains strong international assessment outcomes and supports diverse learners through quality assurance mechanisms.

Hong Kong’s system spans preschool to tertiary levels, with compulsory education from ages 6 to 18. Multiple school types operate with instruction in Cantonese, English, or other languages. The Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) guides university admissions. Vocational training and higher education are well developed, with recent reforms promoting active learning, citizenship aligned with mainland China, and responsible AI use. Inclusion for immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking students is expanding, alongside a regional vocational cooperation framework within the Greater Bay Area. Over 175 costly international schools attract diverse student populations.

Sweden offers subsidized preschool, compulsory primary ("Grundskola," ages 7–16), and elective upper secondary ("Gymnasium," ages 16–19) with academic and vocational tracks. Publicly funded but privately operated independent schools ("friskolor") serve over 10% of pupils. National assessments lead to diplomas and higher education follows the Bologna Process. Recent declines in PISA/PIRLS scores, linked partly to immigrant populations and testing issues, have prompted investigations and reforms. Education is free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens, with financial aid and strict anti-discrimination policies in place.
