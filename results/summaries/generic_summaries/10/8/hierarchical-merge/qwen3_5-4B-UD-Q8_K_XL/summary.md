# Comparative Analysis of Global Education Systems

The educational frameworks analyzed reveal a complex interplay between state mandates, historical legacies, and systemic deficits. Systems in Turkey, Sri Lanka, Egypt, Iran, Nigeria, and Israel are deeply influenced by political ideology, religious laws, or regional inequalities, often struggling with centralized deficits, staffing shortages, or funding inequities. In contrast, nations like Taiwan and Sweden utilize rigorous, highly structured compulsory systems—ranging from 12-year tracks to nine years of core schooling—to achieve high enrollment rates and academic equity. Taiwan's emphasis on academic preparation drives over 95% university participation, while Sweden's structured framework ensures equity through standardized national testing and a clear distinction between preparatory and vocational upper secondary tracks.

Recent structural shifts and technological integration define emerging trends across diverse regions. Armenia is pioneering technology-based centers for digital skills, while Ghana transitions to a free universal basic education system despite facing infrastructure deficits and teacher shortages. Norway is reforming its inclusive model to enhance flexibility through a "2+2" vocational structure. Meanwhile, Hong Kong has undergone significant transformation following the 1997 handover and 2020 national security law, shifting toward Chinese-medium instruction, introducing AI tools, and restructuring the curriculum to emphasize patriotism amidst political developments and demographic changes.

### Institutional Capacity and Higher Education

Institutional capacity varies widely, with public universities in Nigeria, Iran, and Egypt struggling with outdated curricula and budget constraints. Conversely, systems in Taiwan and Israel leverage diverse sectors, from private cram schools to specialized religious institutions, to maintain high academic standards. Higher education in Sweden aligns with the Bologna Process, offering flexible pathways from Bachelor's to Doctoral degrees with free funding for citizens, while Hong Kong's universities operate under varying funding models including UGC grants, public funding, and self-financing mechanisms.

Despite these differences, all systems navigate unique cultural, economic, and political landscapes to aim for compulsory attendance and higher education access. Challenges such as mismatches between graduate skills and market demand, seen in Sri Lanka, persist alongside bottlenecks related to national exam pressures in Taiwan. Ultimately, the tension between national mandates and internal disparities remains a recurring theme, dictating specific policy responses ranging from centralized reforms to sectoral diversification.
