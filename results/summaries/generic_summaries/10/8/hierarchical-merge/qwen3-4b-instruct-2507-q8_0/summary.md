# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in Nigeria, Ireland, Taiwan, Israel, Iran, Sri Lanka, France, Turkey, Egypt, Indonesia, England, Armenia, Ghana, Norway, Hong Kong, and Sweden**

## **Structural Features and Comparative Highlights**

The education systems across these diverse nations reflect a mix of centralized governance, cultural values, and economic priorities. Most countries provide compulsory basic education, with pathways diverging into academic, vocational, or religious tracks. **Nigeria** follows a 9-3-4 structure under the Universal Basic Education (UBE) policy, requiring JAMB scores and SSCE/GCE credits for tertiary entry. Private and religious schools offer better facilities but at high cost. **Ireland** delivers free primary and secondary education with strong equity measures (e.g., DEIS), and its higher education is globally recognized. **Taiwan** offers 12 years of compulsory basic education, with national exams determining academic or vocational routes; its high-tech alignment and strong private institutions support engineering and international programs.

**Israel** features four distinct tracks—state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab—emphasizing STEM excellence to support its tech economy. However, Arab students face underfunding, low teacher ratios, and curriculum bias. **Iran** operates a centralized, Islamic system with compulsory education through grade 9, emphasizing religious values and national leadership; English was banned in primary schools in 2018, and teacher shortages persist. **Sri Lanka** provides free primary and secondary education but has a rigid, competitive structure where only 16% of A/L qualifiers gain university admission; vocational training is underregulated and politically influenced.

In contrast, **France** maintains a secular, competitive system with mandatory primary education and strict *laïcité*, supported by elite *grandes écoles* and competitive *CPGEs*. Teacher recruitment is tiered and highly selective. **Turkey** has a centralized, secular 4+4+4 structure, but curriculum controversies—such as the inclusion of jihad and downplaying of the Armenian genocide—highlight political interference. **Egypt** sees high enrollment but poor quality, with rote learning, teacher shortages, and widespread private tutoring; historical bias and youth unemployment remain persistent. **Indonesia** transitioned from colonial segregation to a nationally inclusive model, with strong primary completion but persistent teacher shortages and low tertiary participation. **England** offers a diversified, state-funded system with accessible pathways (A-levels, BTECs, T Levels), improved school quality via academies, and robust post-16 options. **Armenia** provides free 12-year education and 100% literacy, yet faces rising dropout rates and declining retention due to child labor and quality issues.

In **Ghana**, a 20-year system includes basic (12 years), secondary (academic or vocational), and tertiary education. The BECE and WASSCE govern entry, while TVET remains underdeveloped due to low prestige, poor infrastructure, and weak industry links. Teacher training is primarily through public Colleges of Education, and private education is widespread, especially in urban areas. **Norway** operates a cohesive, high-performing public system with 93% upper secondary enrollment; the 1994 and 2006 reforms unified academic and vocational tracks, enabling smoother transitions. The vocational path follows a "2+2" apprenticeship model. Students earn diplomas based on grades, and higher education includes universities, university colleges, and private institutions. **Hong Kong** offers free compulsory primary and secondary education with a selective curriculum leading to the HKDSE or IB. English-medium instruction correlates with higher university acceptance, but political reforms post-2020—such as removing liberal studies and emphasizing Chinese nationalism—have led to declining enrolment and rising tensions. **Sweden** emphasizes equity and student well-being, with a nine-year mandatory comprehensive school and inclusive pathways. Upper secondary offers both preparatory and vocational tracks, and higher education follows the Bologna Process with strong financial aid and ECTS alignment.

## **Persistent Challenges and Common Themes**

Across all systems, **curriculum rigidity**, **political influence**, and **teacher shortages** are widespread. In Egypt, Turkey, and Sri Lanka, state curricula contain historical bias—particularly regarding the Armenian genocide—while in Israel and Iran, religious content is deeply embedded and often controversial. **Privatization** is growing, driven by demand for quality and access, but often exacerbates inequality. Teacher quality and training remain low, especially in rural and marginalized areas, with low salaries and limited autonomy. **Vocational education** is increasingly promoted—particularly in Taiwan, Indonesia, and Iran—but lacks consistent quality assurance or labor market alignment.

Equity and access remain uneven. In Nigeria, rural insecurity and gender disparities limit female enrollment; in Israel, Arab students face structural barriers; in Sri Lanka, intense competition and low university admission rates create social stratification. In Ghana, rising costs and competition have widened access gaps, especially for women and rural students. In Hong Kong, political shifts and rising costs have led to declining enrolment in prestigious schools. Digital transformation is emerging—Nigeria aims for 95% digital literacy by 2030, while Taiwan and Iran use digital platforms to expand learning. However, investment in teacher development, curriculum innovation, and inclusive policies remains inconsistent.

Despite strong foundational structures and global recognition in specific fields (e.g., Norway’s equity, Taiwan’s tech alignment, Ireland’s inclusivity), systemic weaknesses in equity, quality, political neutrality, and teacher capacity continue to hinder sustainable educational advancement.
