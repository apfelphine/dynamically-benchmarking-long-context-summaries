# **Merged Summary: Education Systems in Taiwan, Nigeria, Iran, Ireland, Turkey, Israel, France, Sri Lanka, England, Egypt, Ghana, Indonesia, Hong Kong, Armenia, Sweden, and Norway**

---

## **Regional Highlights and Comparative Analysis**

### **Taiwan**  
Taiwan operates a highly equitable, standardized, and high-performing education system grounded in national unity and academic excellence. Since 1983, it has implemented a 12-year national basic education framework, with compulsory public schooling from primary to junior high (since 1968), and plans to extend to include preschool. The curriculum emphasizes Mandarin, mathematics, science, English (introduced in Grade 3), social studies, arts, and physical education. Students take national entrance exams to enter one of three academic tracks—science, liberal arts, or vocational—each with tailored curricula. Vocational training spans six areas (agriculture, industry, business, marine products, daily life, art), preparing students for workforce entry, entrepreneurship, or further study. Over 95% of high school graduates enter university since 2008, with many pursuing graduate studies abroad—especially in the U.S. Prestigious public universities like National Taiwan University and National Tsing Hua lead in engineering and research, supported by strong ties to high-tech industries. The system includes robust pathways for students with disabilities or giftedness, five-year junior colleges, and two-year vocational programs. Nursing education offers multiple entry routes to licensure, and private cram schools support exam preparation. The 3+3 high school model (skipping the CAP test and entering university via entrance exams) is widely adopted for its efficiency.

### **Nigeria**  
Nigeria follows a 6-3-3-4 model with compulsory, free universal basic education (6 years primary + 3 years junior secondary), administered by the Universal Basic Education Commission (UBEC). Key gateways to tertiary education include the Common Entrance Examination (CEE) and the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE), administered by WAEC or NECO. The system includes federal, state, and private schools—each with distinct access, quality, and cost structures. Federal schools are government-funded and merit-based, while state schools face staffing shortages and underfunding. Private and religious schools offer better facilities and qualified teachers but are costly. With 153 universities (40 federal, 45 state, 68 private), entry requires SSCE/GCE credits, a JAMB score of at least 180, or a diploma. Vocational training through the National Board for Technical Education (NBTE) addresses youth unemployment but suffers from outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and misalignment with labor needs. Rural education remains weak due to insecurity, poor infrastructure, and non-payment of salaries. Literacy has improved from 62.02% (2018) to 69% (2022), with a target of 95% digital literacy by 2030. Gender disparities persist, particularly in the North, due to cultural norms and teen pregnancy, though initiatives like UNICEF’s Girls’ Education Project have improved enrollment. Informal education has expanded in areas like HIV/AIDS awareness and psychological development.

### **Iran**  
Iran’s education system is deeply rooted in Islamic values and centrally managed by the Ministry of Education, receiving 20% of government spending and 5% of GDP—mainly directed to secondary education. Public schools and universities are well-funded, with teacher training managed through Farhangian University’s 98 centers. The curriculum includes Persian (national), Arabic (grades 7–12), English (taught only in secondary, banned in primary since 2018), and French (added in 2022). Over 5,000 foreign language schools exist, though public English remains limited. The system mandates daily Islamic prayers (30 minutes) and requires university students to study General Soleimani’s will, reflecting its ideological character. Women constitute over 70% of university students and lead in STEM enrollment, with 70% of engineering graduates being female and around 335,000 STEM graduates annually. The National Organization for Development of Exceptional Talents (NODET) supports gifted students through selective schools, while the Office of Educational Research and Planning (OERP) aligns curricula with national and religious values. Notable institutions include the University of Tehran (largest and oldest), Tarbiat Modares (only postgraduate university), and top engineering schools like Sharif and Amirkabir. Despite strong performance in medicine, engineering, and technical training, the system faces teacher shortages (60,000) and rural access gaps. A controversial “starred students” list—allegedly used to punish politically or religiously dissenting students—is denied by the Ministry of Science and Research.

### **Ireland**  
Ireland’s education system is conducted primarily in English, with Irish as a core subject in public schools and the medium of instruction in Gaelscoileanna. Compulsory education spans ages 6 to 16, including eight years of primary and a structured secondary phase: the Junior Cycle (ages 12–14) and Senior Cycle (ages 16–19), culminating in the **Leaving Certificate**. The curriculum follows the European Higher Education Area (EHEA) and the National Framework of Qualifications (NFQ), with schools offering at least 28 hours of instruction per week. Ireland ranks **second globally in reading** and **11th in mathematics** in PISA assessments, with strong performance in science and critical thinking. Third-level education ranks among the **top 20 globally** (US News 2022), and the country is a major destination for international students. Special needs education is supported by the *Education for Persons with Special Educational Needs Act 2004* and the National Council for Special Education (NCSE), while the DEIS program targets inequality through reduced class sizes, financial aid, and infrastructure investment. The academic year includes 182 days (primary) and 167 days (post-primary), with breaks during Christmas, Easter, and summer. The system emphasizes equity, cultural preservation, and high academic standards.

### **Turkey**  
Turkey emphasizes national identity, state-approved historical narratives, and employment access. Since 2012, a 12-year compulsory cycle has been implemented under the "4+4+4" reform. Primary education is free and compulsory, with core subjects including Turkish, math, science, and English. Secondary education includes academic tracks (science, social sciences, Turkish-math, foreign languages) and vocational paths, with university entry via the national **YKS exam**. Religious instruction began in 1956 and is taught from 4th grade onward, though curriculum reforms since 2017 have introduced content on jihad and omitted or distorted Armenian history—contradicting international consensus. Turkey has 167 universities with over 1 million students; public institutions offer low-cost education, while private ones are more expensive. Turkish universities are internationally recognized—especially in engineering—and have seen rising international enrollment. However, the system faces criticism for **curriculum bias**, poor textbook quality, limited instruction in minority languages or religious diversity, and widespread reliance on private exam preparation due to public education quality gaps.

### **Israel**  
Israel’s education system prioritizes Jewish cultural values, socioeconomic mobility, and technological advancement. It boasts **46% of the population with post-secondary degrees** and an average of **11.6 years of schooling**. The **Bagrut exam** serves as a key gateway to university, military service, and employment. Israeli universities are globally recognized in **STEM fields**, and Arab student enrollment has risen sharply—by **133% from 2012 to 2022**—supported by integrated schools that promote Jewish-Arab coexistence. The system is characterized by strong public investment (7.3% of GDP), equitable access, and a clear focus on economic development through education. Unlike Turkey, Israel integrates diversity into its curriculum and institutional design, demonstrating how cultural pluralism can coexist with national cohesion. While Turkey’s curriculum reflects state narratives and biases, Israel’s model exemplifies how education can drive innovation, inclusion, and economic growth. However, deep structural inequities persist between Jewish and Arab communities: Arab students receive only $192 in funding compared to $1,100 for Jewish students, face higher dropout rates (12% vs. 6%), and suffer from shortages in teachers, resources, and curriculum content. Textbook content has been criticized for promoting negative Arab stereotypes, though advocacy groups push for more balanced, coexistence-focused materials. Recurring strikes—such as student protests in 2007 over tuition hikes and teacher strikes in 2008 demanding better pay, smaller class sizes, and extended school years—highlight ongoing tensions.

### **France**  
France maintains a centralized, secular, and free public education system established under Napoleon and Jules Ferry, serving 18 million students, including 2.9 million in higher education. Mandatory schooling runs from age 6 to 16, with a standardized curriculum guided by the national reference guide (B.O.). Students progress through preschool, elementary (CP to CM2), middle school (collège), and high school (lycée), earning either the *baccalauréat* (for university entry) or vocational certifications (CAP). Private schools exist in two forms: those following the national curriculum under contract, and independent institutions offering religious instruction. International schools—105 as of 2015—provide English-medium education.

Higher education includes three academic levels—Licence, Master, and Doctorat—governed by the ECTS system. Public universities offer broad programs, while elite *grandes écoles*—highly selective and prestigious in engineering, business, and humanities—serve as gateways to top careers. Technical training is provided by IUTs. Tuition is free for EU/EEA/Swiss/Quebec students (€170–380/year), though international and private institutions may charge up to €40,000 annually. Research is led by independent bodies such as CNRS and INSERM. A critical preparatory stage, the *CPGEs*, prepares students for *grandes écoles*: scientific CPGEs (e.g., MPSI, PC, PT) emphasize STEM with intense academic loads and weekly oral exams (*colles*); business CPGEs (ECS/ECE) target business schools; humanities CPGEs (Hypokhâgne/Khâgne) focus on literature and philosophy. Teachers include *enseignants-chercheurs*, *PRAG/PRCE*, and *CPGE* instructors, with salaries ranging from €1,400 to €8,800 per month. Public schools enforce *laïcité*, banning conspicuous religious symbols (except in Alsace-Moselle), and religious instruction is prohibited nationwide to prevent proselytism. Youth under 25 now constitute 32% of the population, and only 39 out of 75,000 schools were deemed "seriously violent" in 2000.

### **Sri Lanka**  
Sri Lanka’s education system spans five levels: primary (Grades 1–5), junior secondary (6–9), senior secondary (10–11), collegiate (12–13), and tertiary. Students must pass the G.C.E. Ordinary Level (O/L) to advance to senior secondary and the G.C.E. Advanced Level (A/L), which serves as the primary university entrance exam. The system is overseen by multiple ministries—Ministry of Education (primary and teacher training), Ministry of Higher Education (universities), Ministry of Education Services (infrastructure), and Ministry of Skills Development (vocational training)—with the National Institute of Education (NIE) promoting quality, equity, and relevance.

The system includes three types of government schools: **National Schools** (elite, centrally funded), **Provincial Schools** (vast majority, locally funded and often under-resourced), and **Pirivenas** (monastic institutions for Buddhist priest training that also offer GCE exams). Private schools—such as **assisted** (semi-government) and **autonomous** (fee-charging)—offer better facilities and follow the national curriculum. **International schools** serve expatriate communities and offer curricula like Edexcel IGCSE and A-levels or Cambridge exams. Madrasas (205 registered) provide Islamic education through 749 Muslim schools. Semi-government schools such as Zahira, Wesley, and St. Joseph’s receive government support for textbooks, uniforms, and national exams.

Tuition is in high demand due to intense competition in key exams, especially in urban centers like Colombo, Kandy, and Galle. Online platforms like *tuteclass.com* have expanded access to private tutoring. At the tertiary level, state universities are free but highly competitive—less than 16% of GCE A/L qualifiers gain admission, based on Z-score rankings and district-based quotas. Graduates often face unemployment, leading many to pursue further studies abroad or at the Open University. Only 15 state universities exist, and private universities are limited due to quality concerns. Three types of private institutes offer degrees: Sri Lankan, foreign, or professional qualifications. Vocational education is managed by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, including NVQSL (Levels 1–7), apprenticeships, and programs at technical colleges. Non-formal vocational training is also available for dropouts in fields like hairdressing, carpentry, and beauty culture. The system is criticized for being overly competitive and rigid, with a controversial military-style leadership training program (2011–2015) being discontinued due to conscription allegations.

### **England**  
England’s education system is compulsory from age 5 to 18, with pre-school education available for children aged 3–5 (up to 600 hours annually). The system is structured into a Foundation Stage and four Key Stages (KS1–KS4), with post-16 education (KS5) offering qualifications such as A-levels, BTECs, and vocational pathways. It includes a wide variety of school types—academies, community schools, free schools, foundation schools, voluntary aided and controlled schools, UTCs (University Technical Colleges), and maths schools—with nearly 90% of secondary schools being comprehensive or specialist. All state schools are inspected by Ofsted and must follow the National Curriculum. Private schools, which account for about 7% of enrollment, are fee-charging and not bound by the National Curriculum. Home education is legally permitted but not financially supported.

Post-16 pathways include A-levels, BTECs, T Levels, and vocational qualifications. Higher education is accessible via universities, colleges, and private institutions, with undergraduate degrees typically lasting three years (four for integrated master’s in STEM). Tuition fees are capped at £9,535 annually (2024–25) and are repayable after graduation, with targeted support for disadvantaged students. Postgraduate courses range from certificates to PhDs. Adult education includes access courses, distance learning, and vocational training. A unified qualifications framework—comprising the RQF (Regulated Qualifications Framework) and FHEQ (Framework for Higher Education Qualifications)—aligns education levels with European standards, promoting lifelong learning and equitable access. England ranks 13th globally in literacy, mathematics, and science (average score: 503.7), with primary students performing 4th in reading. The UK is 7th in the UN Education Index. Since 1997, the expansion of the academy system has improved school quality, with 88% of schools rated as good or outstanding by 2015. Since 2018, schools are funded through a national formula, with budgets rising to £7.1 billion by 2023, including dedicated funding for Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND).

### **Egypt**  
Egypt has a long educational history, rooted in ancient institutions like "Bar Ankh" and the Alexandrian University, as well as medieval centers such as al-Azhar and Dar al-Ilm. Modernization began under Muhammad Ali with a dual religious-secular system, expanded by Khedive Ismail, though British occupation (1882) led to neglect and elitist curricula. Since the 1952 revolution, free and compulsory public education has been established, with high access rates—96.9% primary net enrollment and low dropout rates—but poor performance in international assessments (49th in reading, 34th in math). Public education faces significant challenges, including low quality, underpaid and poorly qualified teachers, widespread private tutoring (used by 58% of families, with high spending among wealthier households), examination leakage, corporal punishment, regional disparities, and high youth unemployment (36.1% among graduates). Egypt ranks last in the Middle East for primary education quality and is 118th globally.

The system includes international schools offering British, American, IB, or European curricula, the religious Al-Azhar system (Muslim-only, covering primary, preparatory, and secondary stages under the Supreme Council of Al-Azhar and linked to Al-Azhar University), and specialized agricultural schools. Despite reforms in pedagogy, decentralization, and technology, progress has been limited. Higher education is extensive but faces centralization, low per-student spending, and only about 50% of university entrants graduating. Recent reforms, including a Japan-inspired engineering diploma, aim to improve governance and program relevance.

### **Ghana**  
Ghana’s education system spans from kindergarten to undergraduate level, with a 20-year structure and instruction primarily in English. It includes primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Secondary education culminates in the West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE), following the Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE), with students choosing between academic Senior High School (SHS)—three years, reduced from four in 2009—or vocational/technical education (TVET) through SHS electives or technical institutes (TVIs). TVET remains largely informal, with limited formal recognition and low completion rates. The system is decentralized, governed by 16 regions and 230 district offices, with key institutions including the Ministry of Education, Ghana Education Service (GES), National Council for Curriculum and Assessment (NCCA), and West African Examination Council (WAEC).

Tertiary education includes 6 public and 49 private universities, 10 polytechnics offering Higher National Diplomas (HNDs) and Bachelor of Technology degrees, and colleges of education. Teacher training is primarily delivered through 46 Colleges of Education offering a three-year Diploma in Basic Education (DBE), with graduates required to serve one year in national service. Despite government reforms and donor support (e.g., World Bank, USAID, EU), access remains selective—acceptance rates dropped from 96% in 2001 to 52% in 2011—with persistent gender and socioeconomic disparities. Women and rural students are underrepresented, and male students from the top income quintile are over seven times more likely to attend than those from the poorest. Adult literacy has improved significantly, rising from 54% in 1997 to 79.04% in 2018, particularly among women and rural populations, due to programs like the National Functional Literacy Programme (FNLP). However, challenges in teacher training, infrastructure, quality, and equity continue to hinder equitable development.

### **Indonesia**  
Indonesia operates the fourth-largest education system globally, serving over 50 million students. Historically shaped by Islamic *Pesantren* institutions and colonial-era segregation, the system expanded through *Sekolah Rakjat* and village schools, improving rural literacy despite early gender disparities. Japanese occupation led to ideological control and declining enrollment, while post-independence development faced challenges such as teacher shortages and weak institutional structures. Public primary and secondary education (ages 6–15) is compulsory and free, with high school (ages 15–18) being optional and often fee-based, offering academic or vocational tracks. The 2013 curriculum introduces specialization in natural sciences, social sciences, language and literature, and religion, aligned with religious traditions in Islamic, Christian, and Catholic schools. Higher education is structured under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI), offering academic, vocational, and professional programs from diplomas to doctorates. Despite high enrollment and survival rates, challenges remain in equity, quality, teacher shortages, and access—particularly in remote areas. Tertiary participation is low at 36.31%, and teacher training now requires higher education qualifications.

### **Hong Kong**  
Hong Kong’s education system evolved from a British-influenced, bilingual (Chinese and English) model established in the 19th century, with institutions like Belilios Public School (1890) and Queen’s College (1862) shaping early education. The 1919 May Fourth and 1934 New Life Movements reinforced Chinese-language instruction. Since 1997, the system has increasingly emphasized national security and patriotism, including the Target Oriented Curriculum, the replacement of Liberal Studies with Citizenship and Social Development (focusing on national unity and visits to mainland China), and textbook revisions to remove sensitive content. The National Security Law has contributed to declining enrollment and staff retention. In response to technological change, institutions like HKUST and EdUHK have permitted AI tool use in coursework. Educational collaboration with Guangdong has been strengthened through a framework agreement.

The system is structured into three main stages: Kindergarten (3 years), Primary (6 years, compulsory), Junior Secondary (3 years, compulsory), and Senior Secondary (3 years, selective). Students can choose between the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) or the International Baccalaureate Diploma Programme (IBDP). The commerce stream is vocational, preparing students for work or further vocational education. English-medium instruction (EMI) schools are prestigious and offer higher university acceptance rates, while Chinese language instruction has increasingly shifted to Putonghua. Government policy reforms have reduced secondary school years to two, impacting education duration. The system traditionally emphasizes discipline, rote memorization, and exam performance, with widespread use of cram schools and heavy homework—often criticized as "spoon-fed" learning. However, there are growing efforts to integrate group projects and critical thinking. To address declining birth rates and class size, Hong Kong is transitioning from half-day to full-day schooling. Higher education has expanded significantly since the 1970s, with eight universities funded by the University Grants Commission (UGC)—including HKU, CUHK, HKUST, PolyU, CityU, HKBU, Lingnan University, and EdUHK—offering four-year bachelor’s degrees since 2012. Admission is primarily through JUPAS, with English as the dominant medium. International students often follow the IBDP and apply directly to universities. Publicly-funded institutions like HKAPA and self-financing statutory universities such as HKMU provide diverse pathways. International schools (UK or IB curricula) are widespread, with high fees and growing enrollment, contributing to a more diverse system.

Special education is moving toward integration, with targeted support for severely disabled students. Immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children receive language induction and support. Adult education offers fee remission and vocational pathways. Overall, the system reflects a shift from a rigid, exam-focused model to a more inclusive, diverse, and accessible structure, with ongoing reforms to support non-local and international participation.

### **Armenia**  
Armenia’s education system features a 100% literacy rate since 1960 and has undergone significant reform post-Soviet era to emphasize Armenian history, culture, and language. The K–12 structure includes 4 years of primary (Grades 1–4), 5 years of middle school (Grades 5–9), and 3 years of high school (Grades 10–12), with secondary education being compulsory and free. Students take entrance exams to determine admission to universities or vocational schools. Public schools (97% of institutions) are state-funded and follow a national curriculum, while private schools operate under Ministry regulations and offer specialized programs, though access remains unequal due to tuition costs.

The system performs strongly in sciences, medicine, and engineering, with key institutions including Yerevan State University (founded 1920), Yerevan State Medical University (founded 1930), and private universities such as the American University of Armenia and Eurasia International University. Armenia has 26 state and 33 private higher education institutions and is actively aligning with international standards through the Bologna Process and global accreditation. Rural education is supported by non-formal centers like COAF SMART.  

Armenia has also strengthened its alignment with European standards through participation in the Bologna Process, Lisbon Convention, and EU initiatives such as Erasmus+ and TEMPUS, enhancing quality assurance and policy dialogue with the EU. However, challenges such as rising student absenteeism and dropout rates persist, often linked to child labor and declining educational quality. Post-school initiatives like the Tumo Center promote 21st-century skills, innovation, and youth engagement, fostering innovation and civic participation among young people.

### **Sweden**  
Sweden operates a highly structured, equitable, and inclusive education system. Compulsory education spans nine years (grundskola) from ages 6 to 16, supported by free preschool from age 3. Rooted in historical reforms—including the 1842 national school act and the 1919 standardized curriculum—the system expanded in the 1950s and 1960s to include gymnasieskolan and realskolan, broadening access to secondary education. The curriculum includes 16 core subjects, with over 85% of grade 9 students studying a third language, and students select a subject of personal interest. Grading uses an A–E scale, with final scores determining upper secondary placement. National assessments at grades 3, 6, and 9 evaluate core subjects, though test leak controversies have prompted policy changes.

Upper secondary education includes two-thirds of students on preparatory tracks (e.g., Social Science, Natural Science, Humanities) and one-third in vocational programmes (e.g., Health, Construction, Tourism). All students take core subjects in English, Swedish, and mathematics, plus a mandatory diploma project. Eligibility requires passing 12 subjects (preparatory) or 8 (vocational), including math, Swedish, and English, with additional subject-specific requirements. Students who fail may enter introductory programmes. Higher education offers academic degrees (Bachelor’s, Master’s, PhD) through universities and university colleges, as well as professional degrees aligned with the Bologna Process. International students must demonstrate proficiency in Swedish and English via tests like SweSAT. Higher education is free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens, with financial support available through CSN. Universities are required to ensure fair admissions and prevent discrimination. Vocational education remains distinct and non-Bologna-aligned, focusing on practical, job-oriented training. Independent schools (friskolor), publicly funded and open to all, have grown in number and raise debates over equity, grading integrity, and potential profit motives, despite some research indicating improved student performance. Sweden ranks highly in early childhood education and saw a recovery in PISA scores in 2015, though earlier declines and concerns about testing integrity persist.

### **Norway**  
Norway’s education system evolved from early cathedral schools to a modern, inclusive, and comprehensive structure. Compulsory education began in the 19th century with the establishment of *folkeskole*, later replaced by *grunnskole* covering ages 6–16. The system is divided into three levels: elementary (*barneskole*), lower secondary (*ungdomsskole*), and upper secondary (*videregående skole*), which offers either general or vocational tracks. A key reform in 1994 unified education pathways, promoting smoother transitions and emphasizing general knowledge to support access to higher education. Most students attend public schools, with private institutions being limited and requiring government approval.

Higher education includes universities (offering bachelor’s, master’s, and PhD degrees in theoretical and research-based fields), university colleges (providing diverse programs including professional and vocational degrees, requiring NOKUT accreditation), and private institutions (specializing in areas such as business and arts). There is no formal distinction between vocational and non-vocational education, reflecting a holistic approach to learning. Special education is fully integrated into local schools, supported by educational psychologists and social workers, with a strong focus on inclusive and flexible learning environments. Grading uses a 1–6 scale (6 being the highest, 2 the lowest passing), with a dual system (numbers and letters) introduced through the Bologna process. Upper secondary students earn diplomas with teacher-assigned grades and may be selected for "trekkfag" (exam subjects). The academic year follows a two-semester structure (August–December and January–June), with extended breaks including summer (8 weeks), Christmas, and winter holidays. Students are exempt from school during 13 national holidays, many of which align with major breaks. The system is overseen by NOKUT for quality assurance in higher education, and teachers are highly qualified, with strong emphasis on pedagogy and inclusive curricula. While students perform well internationally, some concerns remain regarding grading practices and parental responsibilities.

## **Comparative Overview**

- **Taiwan** stands out for its **standardization, equity, and academic performance**, with a balanced curriculum, high university enrollment, and strong vocational and special education pathways.  
- **Nigeria** emphasizes **universal access and inclusivity**, but struggles with infrastructure, teacher quality, rural access, and gender equity—particularly in the North.  
- **Iran** maintains a **centralized, ideologically grounded system** with strong performance in technical and medical education and high female participation in STEM, though it faces curricular rigidity, teacher shortages, and religious constraints.  
- **Ireland** excels in **academic outcomes**, particularly in reading and mathematics, with strong cultural preservation and equity mechanisms.  
- **Turkey** promotes **national identity** and employment access but suffers from **curriculum bias, textbook issues, and limited diversity**, especially in religious and historical content.  
- **Israel** demonstrates how education can serve as a driver of **economic development, innovation, and social cohesion**, particularly through integration of Arab and Jewish students and a strong focus on STEM—despite persistent structural inequities between communities.  
- **France** offers a **centralized, secular, and high-quality system** with strong research output and elite institutions, though it enforces *laïcité* and faces criticism over religious exclusion.  
- **Sri Lanka** is characterized by **intense competition and rigid structure**, with unequal access and underperformance in tertiary admissions and employment outcomes.  
- **England** shows **improving quality and equity** through the academy system and strong post-16 pathways, though it faces challenges in teacher quality and regional disparities.  
- **Egypt** has **high access but poor quality and performance**, with systemic issues in teacher training, private tutoring, and youth unemployment.  
- **Ghana** has made progress in **adult literacy and access**, but continues to face **deep inequities in access, quality, and outcomes**, especially for women and rural students.  
- **Indonesia** has a large, diverse system with strong rural literacy foundations, but faces persistent challenges in teacher quality, access, and tertiary participation.  
- **Hong Kong** reflects a **rigid, exam-focused model** with growing emphasis on national identity and technology integration, transitioning toward inclusivity and diversity.  
- **Armenia** emphasizes **national identity and cultural heritage**, with strong performance in STEM and active EU integration, though challenges remain in rural access and student retention.  
- **Sweden and Norway** lead in **equity, inclusion, and holistic development**, with strong public investment, inclusive policies, and high-quality vocational and academic pathways. They exemplify how inclusive, student-centered systems can support both individual growth and societal well-being.

Together, these systems reflect diverse models of education shaped by history, ideology, economic priorities, and cultural values—offering valuable lessons in equity, access, performance, and reform. Notably, **Sweden and Norway** set benchmarks in equity and inclusion, while **Taiwan, Ireland, and Israel** demonstrate excellence in academic outcomes and innovation. In contrast, systems like **Nigeria, Egypt, and Ghana** highlight the persistent challenges of access, quality, and equity, particularly in rural and marginalized communities. The integration of technology (e.g., Ghana, Hong Kong) and alignment with international frameworks (e.g., Armenia, Sweden, Norway) represent emerging trends toward modernization and global competitiveness.
