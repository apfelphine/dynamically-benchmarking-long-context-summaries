# Education Systems Overview

## Taiwan, Nigeria, Iran, Turkey, Israel, and the Republic of Ireland  
Taiwan offers 12 years of compulsory schooling focusing on Mandarin, academics, and vocational training, bolstered by private cram schools and government initiatives in early childhood and international education. Nigeria’s system includes primary, Universal Basic Education, and divided secondary cycles ending with national exams for university admission, but faces challenges like teacher shortages, rural access, and gender gaps while promoting literacy and digital learning. Iran’s centralized, Islamized, and sex-segregated education features compulsory elementary education and optional academic or vocational upper secondary tracks, with women well represented in STEM and ongoing reforms supporting digital skills, despite political constraints.

Turkey imposes 12 years of compulsory education through primary, middle, and secondary stages that offer general, vocational, and technical paths; religious education has been politically reintroduced, but minority education rights remain limited amid frequent reforms. Israel mandates kindergarten to 12th grade within multiple sectors—state-secular, religious, Haredi, and Arab—excelling in STEM education and tertiary enrollment, though disparities in Arab education persist. The Republic of Ireland requires education from ages 6 to 16 or three secondary years, dominated by Catholic schools but increasingly including multidenominational and Irish-language institutions, with strong international student performance and inclusive tertiary programs.

## France, Sri Lanka, England, Egypt, and Ghana  
France’s centralized, secular system offers free education from age 3 to high school, culminating in the baccalauréat, with higher education aligned to the Bologna process through Grandes Écoles and public universities supported by rigorous teacher training and research institutions. Sri Lanka serves over 4 million students through multiple ministries and school types, offering free compulsory education until age 14 and competitive, free tertiary education, while vocational education seeks to enhance employment despite systemic rigidity.

England mandates education from ages 5 to 18 under the National Curriculum, with state-funded and private schools regulated by Ofsted; post-16 education offers diverse academic and vocational qualifications and ongoing funding reforms. Egypt combines secular and religious education, compulsory until age 14, but struggles with quality, overcrowding, and inequality; reforms focus on teacher training, governance, vocational expansion, and distance learning amid youth unemployment. Ghana provides about 20 years of formal education primarily in English, with free universal basic education and compulsory exams; despite gender and urban-rural disparities and low vocational participation due to stigma, Ghana advances ICT integration and scholarship initiatives to improve access and digital competencies.

## Armenia, Sweden, Norway, Indonesia, and Hong Kong  
Armenia’s 12-year compulsory education includes primary, middle, and high school levels, culminating in centralized exams for higher education or vocational choices; public schools follow strict standards while private schools increase disparities. The country participates actively in European harmonization efforts (Bologna Process, Erasmus+) and nurtures digital skills via innovative centers, though absenteeism linked to child labor remains a challenge. Sweden offers comprehensive preschool to higher education aligned with the Bologna Process, tuition-free for EU/EEA citizens, with broad curricula, inclusive special education, and publicly funded independent schools, though concerns over grading and testing irregularities persist.

Norway features three compulsory levels with general and vocational upper secondary tracks, including apprenticeships and inclusive special education, and a diverse higher education sector requiring competitive grades for admission; assessments combine teacher grading with random exams. Indonesia, with the world’s fourth-largest system, mandates 12 years spanning primary to senior high, integrating vocational and religious education across diverse school types, facing funding, equity, and teacher shortages especially in remote areas, while advancing teacher professionalization and following national qualification frameworks in higher education.

Hong Kong’s education system, shaped by its British colonial past and post-1997 reforms, mandates education from ages 6 to 18 through kindergarten to senior secondary stages culminating in the HKDSE. Instruction employs Cantonese and English, with increasing use of Putonghua, alongside numerous international curricula. Universities offer four-year honors degrees with English-language instruction and centralized admissions, promoting active learning despite criticism about exam pressure. The government supports subsidized preschool, adult education, inclusive special needs programs, and fosters cross-border cooperation within the Greater Bay Area, while universities explore AI tools like ChatGPT to enhance learning.
