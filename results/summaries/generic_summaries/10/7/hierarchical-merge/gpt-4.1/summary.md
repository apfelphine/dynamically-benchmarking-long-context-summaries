# Overview of Education Systems Worldwide

This synthesis explores the structures, reforms, and challenges of various national education systems. Taiwan and Turkey mandate 12 years of education, emphasizing vocational training, digital literacy, and diverse curricula, while Taiwan’s system strongly supports adult and special needs education. Nigeria (9-3-4) and Ghana provide free, compulsory basic education—both countries face resource constraints, teacher shortages, and regional disparities but are expanding vocational and digital initiatives with international support. Ghana further emphasizes ICT integration and digital scholarship access, though implementation gaps remain.

Iran’s centralized system offers free public and private education, with high female university enrollment and persistent competitiveness (Konkur exam), yet faces inequalities and political constraints. Ireland delivers free, compulsory education with diverse, high-achieving institutions and growing support for special needs; Sri Lanka blends colonial, religious, and linguistic influences, with competitive university entry and complex administration. England’s state-supported system features academic/vocational routes, rising tuition, lifelong learning, and Ofsted oversight prioritizing equity. Egypt’s reforms focus on access and technology but struggle with low teacher pay and persistent inequalities.

Israel’s complex structure includes state-secular, religious, Haredi, Arab, and integrated schools, marked by high tertiary enrollment, top universities, and ongoing efforts to address underfunding, inclusion, and social disparities. France’s secular, centralized system centers on the baccalauréat and selective higher education, with moderate teacher salaries but strong research foundations.

# Additional Systems and Reform Efforts

Indonesia’s constitution-based framework stresses nationalism, inclusivity, and religious respect, with compulsory schooling and blended vocational/secular tracks, but struggles with teacher shortages and inequality. Hong Kong’s three-tiered system is adapting language and curriculum to social reforms and patriotic aims, with English-medium universities partnering technologically with mainland institutions. Armenia has shifted from Soviet models to near-universal literacy and European-compatible curricula via the Bologna Process; notable initiatives like Tumo foster tech-driven learning, but the system faces infrastructure shortages, dropouts, and equity issues.

Sweden provides compulsory schooling and vocational/academic secondary education, prioritizing anti-discrimination, minority inclusion, and financial aid. Debate continues around private friskolor and grade inflation, alongside challenges in maintaining standards and equity. Norway’s stringent, inclusive system features regulated private education, mainstreamed special-needs support, state-run universities, and reforms focused on creating supportive, equitable environments aligned with European grading practices.
