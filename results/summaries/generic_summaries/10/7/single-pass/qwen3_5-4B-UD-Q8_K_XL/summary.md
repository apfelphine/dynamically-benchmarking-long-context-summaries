# Global Education Systems: A Comparative Overview

## Asia and the Pacific

**Taiwan** operates a highly centralized public education system transitioning from a nine-year to a twelve-year compulsory model. The curriculum emphasizes Mandarin, rigorous academic preparation for national entrance exams, and strict discipline, often enforced by military officers in high schools. While public education is dominant, private institutions and supplementary cram schools (*buxiban*) are pervasive due to intense academic pressure. Higher education is competitive, with a high university entrance rate driven by reforms that elevated junior colleges and institutes of technology.

**Nigeria** utilizes a "6-3-3-4" structure (six years primary, three junior secondary, three senior secondary, four tertiary), though reforms are shifting toward a 9-3-4 model. The system is stratified by funding: elite Federal Government schools offer free education based on merit, while State schools often suffer from budget deficits and unqualified teachers. Private institutions are expensive but offer better facilities. Promotional examinations like the SSCE (WAEC) and GCE determine university entry, and the sector faces significant challenges regarding teacher shortages, regional disparities, and the low status of vocational education.

**Iran** features a highly centralized system overseen by the Ministry of Education, where public universities are tuition-free and students must serve the state for a period after graduation. Education is heavily influenced by Islamic values, with mandatory prayer times and segregated schooling. Despite high literacy rates and significant government spending, the system faces criticism for rote learning, a shortage of teachers, and the disparity between the quality of public and private institutions.

**The Republic of Ireland** guarantees free state-funded education from age six to 16. The system is characterized by a strong role for the Catholic Church, with 88% of primary and 47% of secondary schools under its patronage. Secondary education culminates in the Leaving Certificate, and the country ranks highly in international assessments. Special needs education is supported by specific legislation and assistants, though access to higher education remains competitive.

**Sri Lanka** has a history of education rooted in Buddhism and colonial influence, resulting in a diverse system of state, private, and religious schools. The curriculum is divided into primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, and collegiate levels, with university entry determined by the highly competitive G.C.E. Advanced Level exams. While state universities are free, they are extremely selective, leading many students to seek education abroad or in private institutes.

**Indonesia** provides free, compulsory 12-year education. The system is diverse, blending public secular schools with a vast network of private religious institutions (*pesantren* and *madrasah*). Pedagogy in public schools traditionally emphasizes rote learning and teacher authority. Challenges include funding gaps in remote areas, teacher shortages, and a low tertiary participation rate despite high primary enrollment.

**Hong Kong** transitioned from a British colonial system to one under Chinese sovereignty, maintaining English as the primary medium of instruction for elite schools. Education is intensely competitive, with a heavy reliance on cram schools and a focus on exam scores (HKDSE). The system is divided between government-funded schools and private institutions, with significant disparities in resources and outcomes between local and international curricula.

**Armenia** follows a centralized Soviet model that shifted toward emphasizing Armenian history and culture post-independence. Education is free and compulsory through secondary school. The system is dominated by public institutions but faces issues with high student absenteeism, dropout rates linked to child labor, and a need to modernize curriculum standards to meet international benchmarks.

**Sweden** operates a comprehensive 9-year compulsory school (*grundskola*) with a strong focus on equity, inclusion, and high-quality preschool education. The grading system is non-punitive until year 6, and national tests are used for quality assurance rather than student ranking. Upper secondary education (*gymnasium*) is elective and highly successful, with a clear pathway to university. The system is facing challenges related to immigrant integration and recent declines in PISA scores.

**Norway** has a unified *grunnskole* (elementary and lower secondary) system with no official grades until age 13. Upper secondary school (*videregående skole*) offers a choice between general studies and vocational training, with a strong emphasis on student autonomy and outdoor education. The system is publicly funded, though independent schools (*friskoler*) are permitted and receive state funding, sparking debates over academic standards and profit models.

## Europe and the Americas

**Turkey** implemented the "4+4+4" system in 2012, mandating 12 years of compulsory education. The curriculum is heavily influenced by Turkish nationalism and secularism, though recent political shifts have reintroduced religious education and contested historical narratives regarding the Armenian genocide. Higher education is selective and competitive, with a growing number of foreign students attracted by affordable tuition.

**Israel** values education as a primary driver of economic development, with high rates of tertiary attainment among Jewish citizens. The system is segmented into state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab sectors, leading to significant disparities in resources and outcomes. The *Bagrut* exam is crucial for university and military service, and the system is often criticized for its selectivity and the marginalization of certain religious and ethnic groups.

**France** maintains a highly centralized, secular (*laïque*) education system where the state controls the curriculum and employs teachers as civil servants. Higher education is divided between public universities and elite *grandes écoles*, with the latter requiring preparatory classes (*prépas*). The system emphasizes "common culture" and national identity, though it faces challenges regarding diversity and regional inequality.

**England** operates a comprehensive state system where education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18. The curriculum is standardized (National Curriculum), but schools are inspected by Ofsted, which significantly influences school performance and reputation. Private ("independent") schools charge high fees but offer greater autonomy. Post-16 education includes a mix of academic (A-levels), vocational (BTECs), and apprenticeships.

**Egypt** has a highly centralized system where basic education is free and compulsory, but quality and teacher qualifications remain issues. A parallel market for private tutoring has become ubiquitous to bridge the gap in public education quality. The Al-Azhar system operates alongside the state system, providing religious education to Muslims.

**Ghana** offers free, compulsory basic education from ages 4 to 14. The system is heavily reliant on public-private partnerships due to government budget constraints, with a significant portion of schools being privately operated or managed. Tertiary education enrollment has grown rapidly, driven by private institutions, though equity remains a challenge.
