# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan's education system has evolved under Dutch, Qing, Japanese, and Republic of China rule, with private schools initially suppressed then revitalized post-1954 under state influence. Public education spans from nursery to university, with compulsory education extended to 12 years by 2014. School discipline is strict, including uniforms and cleaning duties, though corporal punishment persists culturally. Instruction is primarily in Mandarin, with supplemental classes common.

The 12-year basic education includes six years of elementary (Grades 1–6) and three years each of junior and senior high school, with national exams controlling progression to higher levels. Senior high offers academic tracks (liberal arts, science) and vocational schools (skill-based). Higher education includes universities and junior colleges offering bachelor’s to doctoral degrees. Private education is widespread, particularly cram schools supplementing academics amid cultural pressure. Preschool options include growing private English immersion programs.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system follows a 9-3-4 structure: six years primary, three years junior secondary, three years senior secondary, and tertiary education. Universal Basic Education (UBE) is free and compulsory for nine years. Federal and state secondary schools differ in funding and quality, with federal schools generally better staffed and regarded. Private and religious schools possess smaller classes and better resources.

Secondary education culminates in the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE). International schools offer English-medium curricula like British and American systems. Advanced level programs (IJMB, JUPEB) provide university direct entry routes. Tertiary education comprises universities, polytechnics, and colleges, with over 150 universities nationwide. Vocational training is emphasized but faces challenges like underfunding and poor infrastructure. Literacy rates remain low, especially in northern regions, with government-led initiatives focused on improving female education and reducing disparities.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education traces back to ancient times with the Academy of Gondishapur. Modern schooling began under Safavid and Qajar eras, evolving significantly post-1979 Revolution with Islamization of curricula and gender segregation. Compulsory elementary and junior high education is free; upper secondary is optional and divided into theoretical, vocational, and manual skill paths.

Higher education is centralized under the Ministry of Education and includes universities offering bachelor’s to doctoral degrees, with admission based on the national Konkour exam. Women comprise over 70% of university students, despite some societal and legal challenges. Languages taught include Persian (official), Arabic, and increasingly French alongside English. Iranian universities are internationally ranked in STEM and other fields. Reforms focus on curriculum development, teacher training at Farhangian University, and integration of hybrid learning post-pandemic.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Education is compulsory from ages 6 to 16 (or until three years of second-level education). The system features both English and Irish language instruction, with Gaelscoileanna offering Irish-medium education. Primary education covers Junior Infants to Sixth Class (ages 4–12). Secondary education includes Junior Cycle (3 years), optional Transition Year, and Senior Cycle (2 years), culminating in the Leaving Certificate exams.

Schools are mainly state-funded but under varied religious patronage (mostly Catholic). There's a growing presence of multidenominational and secular schools. Third-level education is highly regarded internationally with universities attracting many global students. Special needs education is supported by the National Council for Special Education. Disadvantage schemes assist low-income families, and the DEIS program targets educational inequality.

# Education in Turkey
The Turkish education system underwent major reforms after 1924, uniting previously separate school types under Ministry of National Education. Compulsory education extends to 12 years under the current "4+4+4" system (elementary, middle, and secondary school each lasting four years). Secondary education divides into general and vocational tracks, with universities offering undergraduate and postgraduate education.

Religious education was abolished but reintroduced as a mandatory course from primary to high school. Foreign languages include English (from 2nd grade), Kurdish, Arabic, and others. Private and minority schools exist alongside public institutions. Turkey hosts numerous universities with growing international student populations, and its technical universities gain international accreditation. Criticisms include politicized curricula, textbook quality, and religious-nationalist indoctrination.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education is divided into primary, middle, and high school with compulsory education through 12th grade. Schools are categorized into state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors, with increasing Arab and Haredi populations. Education emphasizes Jewish cultural values, but disparities exist, especially in the Arab sector where funding and resources lag. Matriculation exams (bagrut) and Psychometric Entrance Test govern university admission.

Higher education is well-developed, with government-subsidized universities and high tertiary attainment rates. Despite high levels of female participation, STEM fields remain male-dominated. Challenges include teacher strikes, educational inequality, and criticism over textbook portrayals of Arabs. International rankings commend Israeli universities particularly in STEM disciplines.

# Education in France
Modern French education began in the late 19th century with Jules Ferry laws establishing mandatory, free, and secular schooling for ages 6–12. The Ministry of National Education centrally regulates curriculum and examinations. The system includes preschool (maternelle), elementary, collège (middle school), and lycée (high school), culminating in the baccalauréat exams with general, technological, and professional streams.

Higher education consists of public universities and prestigious grandes écoles, with selective preparatory classes (CPGEs) feeding into grandes écoles. Tuition in public universities is low due to state funding, while private and elite schools charge higher fees. Religious instruction is excluded from public schools. France hosts many international schools and has incorporated the Bologna process into its tertiary education system.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Administered mainly by multiple ministries, Sri Lankan education dates back over 2,300 years, rooted in Buddhist monastic institutions and expanded during colonial rule with Christian mission schools. Free education was introduced formally in 1938. The structure comprises five years primary, four years junior secondary, two years senior secondary, culminating in the GCE O-Level and A-Level examinations that govern university entrance.

The country has a network of national, provincial, and semi-government schools alongside private and international schools, with 205 madrasas providing Islamic education. Tuition is free, but private tutoring is widespread due to competitive exams. Higher education is highly selective and free at public universities, with demand exceeding capacity, leading many to study abroad or in private institutes. Vocational education and leadership training programs also exist.

# Education in England
Education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18, covering primary and secondary stages with standardized national curriculum and public examinations (GCSEs, A-levels). Various state-funded school types exist including academies, free schools, grammar schools, and faith schools. Private schools educate about 7% of pupils and are inspected separately.

Post-16 education includes sixth form and further education colleges offering academic and technical qualifications such as A-levels, BTECs, and apprenticeships. Higher education comprises universities and colleges offering undergraduate to postgraduate degrees, with tuition fees capped and partially repayable. Adult education and national qualification frameworks support lifelong learning. England scores above OECD averages in literacy and mathematics.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt's education, one of the oldest in history, evolved from ancient institutions like al-Azhar to modern reforms instigated by Muhammad Ali and influenced by French and British occupation. The system consists of basic education (kindergarten to preparatory), secondary education (general and technical tracks), and higher education with public and private universities.

Compulsory basic education covers nine years, free in public schools, with disparities due to wealth and regional factors. Secondary education leads to final exams, while tertiary education faces challenges of quality, centralization, and high unemployment among graduates. The Al-Azhar system operates parallelly with religious emphasis. Private tutoring is pervasive due to public school quality. Reforms focus on pedagogy, curriculum, and expanding access, with international schools offering diverse foreign curricula.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education evolved from informal, oral traditions and mission schools to a modern, government-led system emphasizing free, compulsory basic education from ages 4 to 15. The 12-year basic education includes kindergarten, primary school, and junior high. Senior high school offers academic and vocational tracks, culminating in the West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE).

Tertiary education has expanded with public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges. The government collaborates with private and international bodies for funding, infrastructure, and literacy programs. Challenges include low vocational education uptake, infrastructural deficits, and educational inequality based on gender and socioeconomic status. ICT integration and scholarship programs are priorities for improving access and quality.

# Education in Indonesia
Education in Indonesia stems from Islamic pesantren traditions and Dutch colonial structures with stratified schooling based on social status. The modern system mandates 12 years of schooling (6 primary, 3 junior high, 3 senior high), with public secular schools overseen by the Ministry of Education and religious schools by the Ministry of Religious Affairs.

Curriculum emphasizes Pancasila ideology and rote learning, with early childhood education mostly private and optional. Islamic education includes pesantren (boarding schools), madrasah, and other Islamic schools with varying religious and secular content. Higher education institutions offer academic, vocational, and professional degrees under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework. Challenges include teacher shortages, equity, and quality issues across the archipelago.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong’s education evolved from early Chinese village schools and missionary-influenced western models under British rule. The system comprises 3 years kindergarten, 6 years primary, and 6 years secondary education culminating in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE). English and Cantonese are main media of instruction, though Putonghua (Mandarin) is increasingly introduced.

The city has eight publicly funded universities highly ranked internationally, plus numerous private and self-financing institutions. International schools offer diverse foreign curricula, serving expatriates and locals. Recent political changes have affected school enrollment patterns and curricula content, with increased focus on national security and patriotism. Adult education and special education programs exist, along with initiatives for immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia boasts near-universal literacy with a publicly funded, compulsory 12-year education system split into primary (grades 1–4), middle (5–9), and high school (10–12) with general and specialized tracks. National exams govern university entrance. Public schools dominate, following a national curriculum, while private schools exist with state oversight.

Higher education includes numerous state and private universities offering bachelor's to doctoral studies, implementing Bologna Process standards and participating in Erasmus programs. Challenges include increasing absenteeism linked to child labor. Technology-based centers like the Tumo Center support skill development. International partnerships aid quality assurance and reforms.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden’s education system is comprehensive and free, featuring 9 years of compulsory school (grades 1–9) followed by optional 3 years upper secondary school (gymnasium) with diverse national programmes preparing for university or vocational paths. Preschool is widely accessible and subsidized.

Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Grading is standardized, and standardized national tests occur in grades 3, 6, and 9. Independent (free) schools receive public funding and have diverse pedagogical profiles.

Sweden faces debates over private schooling and educational quality as revealed by international assessments (PISA, PIRLS), with immigrant enrollment influencing outcomes. The country supports learner aid and anti-discrimination policies. Vocational education is separate from university system and emphasizes workplace experience.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education dates to medieval cathedral schools with compulsory education established in primary (grades 1–7) and lower secondary (8–10) schools. Upper secondary (videregående skole) education is elective and follows reform merging vocational and general tracks.

Teachers vary by qualifications from preschool to university levels. Higher education includes universities and university colleges, with no strict vocational division at tertiary level. Grading systems vary, and admission uses grades and aptitude tests.

The academic year runs August to June with multiple breaks. Special education emphasizes inclusion. The system features public dominance with limited private schools. Education reforms focus on accessibility and quality, with cultural emphasis on foundational social and moral education.
