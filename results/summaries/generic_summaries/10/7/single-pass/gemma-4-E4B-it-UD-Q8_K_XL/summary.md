# Educational Systems Overview

## Taiwan Education
Taiwan's education system evolved through various historical influences (Dutch, Qing China, Japanese). Following the ROC's retreat in 1949, there was suppression of private schools, later followed by revitalization under state influence. Public education spans nursery to university, with compulsory education mandated from primary through junior high school (expanded to 12 years by 2014). Daily discipline in public schools is generally strict. The school year has two semesters, with instruction primarily in Mandarin.

The system is highly structured, with elementary school covering grades 1-6 and junior high covering 7-9. Senior high school (grades 10-12) prepares students for national university entrance exams, administered via recommendation or direct exam. Higher education includes junior colleges, undergraduate, and postgraduate tracks. Private education is extensive, featuring cram schools, and kindergartens, with the 3+3 High School system allowing some students to bypass the CAP test.

## Nigeria Education
Nigeria's education structure is divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary school lasts six years, followed by nine years of Universal Basic Education (UBE) encompassing primary and junior secondary school. Secondary school is divided into Junior Secondary School (JSS, 3 years) and Senior Secondary School (SSS, 3 years) under the 6-3-3-4 system.

Federal-sponsored secondary schools are elite, merit-based institutions. Private schools are significantly more expensive but offer smaller classes. Vocational education is managed by the National Board for Technical Education, and informal education, including apprenticeships, remains a significant component. Tertiary education is overseen by the National Universities Commission (NUC), with public and private options available.

## Iran Education
Iranian education has roots in ancient Persia and underwent modern reforms across various eras. The system is highly centralized, overseen by the Ministry of Education. Pre-primary education is optional, followed by 6 years of compulsory elementary education. Lower-secondary education is mandatory (3 years), while upper-secondary education (3 years) is not compulsory.

The system incorporates technical/vocational branches and is heavily influenced by Islamic values, which were emphasized post-1979 revolution. Higher education is state-funded, with admission based on the national Konkur exam. Women show high participation rates in higher education, and the country boasts strong output in engineering and STEM fields.

## Republic of Ireland Education
Irish education is compulsory from age six to sixteen (or completion of three years of second-level education). The primary curriculum aims to nurture the child in all dimensions of life. The secondary system involves a three-year Junior Cycle culminating in the Junior Certificate, followed by a two-year Senior Cycle leading to the Leaving Certificate.

Education is delivered through various types of schools, including National, Gaelscoileanna (Irish-language), and Multidenominational schools. Tertiary education is structured using the EHEA cycle, with most courses taught in English, though Irish is a core subject in all public schools. The system emphasizes breadth alongside depth of learning.

## Turkey Education
Turkish education is highly centralized, managed by the Ministry of National Education. The system recently adopted the "4+4+4" structure (4 years primary, 4 years middle school, 4 years secondary). The curriculum historically focused on Turkish nationalism in Atatürk's thought.

Secondary education includes general, vocational/technical, and Anatolian high schools. Students are guided by national curriculum and take a high school finishing examination before university entry. Religious education is mandatory in schools, covering various world religions.

## Israel Education
Israel's education system is tiered (Primary 1-6, Middle 7-9, High 10-12) and compulsory from kindergarten through 12th grade. Schools are divided into four tracks: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab.

Societally, higher education is valued as a route to socioeconomic mobility, heavily driven by the high-tech economy. The Matriculation (Bagrut) exams are crucial for university admission. Despite high attainment, there are noted disparities, particularly concerning the under-education of Haredi students and resource allocation gaps in the Arab sector.

## France Education
France's modern education began with Jules Ferry's laws in 1881, establishing free, mandatory, and secular public schooling. The curriculum is standardized nationally, though specialized sections exist. The secondary stage is divided into *collège* (middle school, 4 years) and *lycée* (high school, 3 years), leading to the *baccalauréat*.

Higher education is divided between public universities and elite *grandes écoles*. Students gain credit using the ECTS system. While public higher education is mostly state-funded and low-cost, elite private institutions are highly selective and prestigious.

## Sri Lanka Education
Sri Lanka's system, rooted in ancient Buddhist traditions, is administered by several ministries. Primary school is five years, followed by junior secondary (4 years) and senior secondary (2 years) leading to the G.C.E. Ordinary Level (O/Ls). University entrance requires the G.C.E. Advanced Level (A/Ls).

The majority of schools are government-maintained, though private and international schools are increasing. Tertiary education in state universities is free but extremely competitive, with admission based on GCE A/L ranks. Vocational training is managed by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission.

## England Education
Education in England is compulsory from age 5 to 18, with state-provided schooling being mostly free. The structure includes Early Years, four Key Stages (KS1-KS4), and post-16 education (sixth form). State-funded schools range from comprehensive to selective grammar schools.

The National Curriculum dictates core subjects across all stages. Post-16 students can pursue vocational routes (like T Levels) or higher education, with qualifications aligning with the RQF/FHEQ. While 93% of children are in state-funded schools, there is a growing private sector and an "Education Otherwise" option.

## Egypt Education
Egypt's system is highly centralized, with basic education (ages 4-14) being compulsory and free in public institutions. Secondary education has general and technical tracks. Higher education is supervised by the Ministry of Higher Education, featuring public and private institutions.

Progress in enrollment is high, yet standardized tests reveal substantial quality issues. A major challenge is the parallel market of private tutoring, which has become nearly compulsory due to perceived inadequacies in public schooling. The Al-Azhar system runs concurrently, focusing heavily on religious studies.

## Ghana Education
Ghana's education structure is divided into basic (12 years, ages 4-15), secondary, and tertiary levels, with lessons primarily in English. Basic education is free and compulsory. Basic education ends with the BECE, allowing students to proceed to academic or vocational Senior High School (SHS).

While enrollment has increased rapidly, the quality of TVET is often informal (apprenticeships). Tertiary education enrollment is increasing, though it is heavily skewed towards wealthier males. The government relies on public-private partnerships to address financing gaps.

## Indonesia Education
Indonesia requires 12 years of schooling, which is compulsory. Pre-school is under PAUD, and primary/secondary levels are free in public schools. The national curriculum emphasizes secular wisdom and Pancasila.

The system accommodates diverse learning styles, including *Pesantren* (Islamic boarding schools) and *Madrasah* (integrated religious/secular schools). Tertiary education offers various qualifications (Bachelor, Master, Doctorate) through different institution types. Challenges persist in funding, management, and equity across the archipelago.

## Hong Kong Education
Hong Kong's education is compulsory through secondary school, which is free in the public sector. The system has transitioned from traditional methods to active/experiential pedagogy. The curriculum is divided into junior and senior years, culminating in the HKDSE.

The medium of instruction has shifted toward Chinese (Cantonese/Mandarin), though English Medium Instruction (EMI) schools remain prestigious. Tertiary education is led by eight UGC-funded universities. International schools offer non-local curricula and cater to diverse populations, while government schools adapt to address immigrant children.

## Armenia Education
Armenian education is free and compulsory through secondary school (12 years). Public schools are dominant (97%), following a national curriculum that balances global trends with local culture. Secondary education is split into general and specialized tracks.

Higher education is strong in STEM fields and includes numerous state and private institutions, with international integration via the Bologna Process. Despite high enrollment, student absenteeism and dropout rates are linked to child labor.

## Sweden Education
Sweden operates a nine-year comprehensive compulsory school system (*grundskola*), covering 16 mandatory subjects. Preschool is heavily subsidized, and the system is known for its integrated approach.

Upper secondary education (*gymnasieskola*) offers both preparatory and vocational paths. Tertiary education aligns with the Bologna Process (Bachelor, Master, PhD). While state-funded, independent schools (*friskolor*) are allowed, raising concerns over potential declining standards and inequality.

## Norway Education
Norway's system is compulsory from age six to sixteen, divided into elementary, lower secondary, and upper secondary school. The system emphasizes play in early years and gradual introduction of grading starting in lower secondary. The 1994 reform merged older branches into a single system, offering both general and vocational tracks.

Higher education is largely state-run, with the Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (NOKUT) ensuring quality. The system supports inclusive education, with special education integrated into ordinary schools.

## Comparative Summaries
Across these diverse systems, key trends emerge: high societal value placed on education (Taiwan, Israel, Sweden, Norway), significant government centralization versus decentralization (Taiwan, Egypt vs. Ireland, Sweden), and the persistent tension between high academic standards and socio-economic equity (Ghana, Sri Lanka, Egypt). While many countries embrace modernization and international integration (Iran, Armenia, Sweden), challenges regarding curriculum bias, teacher quality, and the impact of private/informal sectors remain widespread.
