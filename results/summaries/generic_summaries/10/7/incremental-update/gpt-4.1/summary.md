## Summary

### Taiwan
Taiwan's twelve-year compulsory education, governed by the Ministry of Education, emphasizes Mandarin instruction, provides both academic and vocational tracks, features competitive university entry, and reflects strong U.S. influences in higher education.

### Nigeria
Nigeria’s "9-3-4" structure centers on primary and junior secondary schooling, with students sorted by national exams into academic or vocational routes. Private schools tend to outperform public ones. Reforms target literacy, female access, vocational education barriers, and digital learning, supported by government and NGOs.

### Iran
Iran’s centralized system yields high literacy and robust STEM education for gifted students, while facing teacher shortages and high dropout rates. Ongoing reforms promote research, digital advancement, and global competitiveness.

### Republic of Ireland
Ireland’s inclusive system blends Irish language and diverse school ownership types, prioritizes special needs and disadvantaged students, and operates distinct Junior and Senior Cycles, amid ongoing debates over homeschooling and parental rights.

### Turkey
Turkey’s "4+4+4" compulsory program offers general, vocational, and apprentice tracks, marked by centralized, nationalist governance and limited minority representation, with ongoing reforms.

### Israel
Israel's heterogeneous sectors—secular, religious, Haredi, Arab—operate under inclusion laws and with key exams shaping progression. Access for Arabs in higher education is improving, and reforms address teacher pay and inclusion, though STEM gender gaps persist.

### France
France provides free, secular schooling from preschool through lycée, split into academic and vocational baccalaureate paths under strong centralized control. Higher education remains low-cost, with elite Grandes Écoles; secularism and balanced teaching/research are priorities.

### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka mandates education through age 14; competitive exams dictate selective university entry. Vocational and overseas study options exist, but systemic rigidity and intense competition are challenges.

### England
England’s system educates students ages 5 to 18, regulated by Ofsted. Advancement progresses through GCSEs and A-levels, with academic and vocational pathways. Higher education emphasizes inclusion and support for disadvantaged groups, with high standards and accountability.

### Egypt
Egypt’s centralized system covers basic through higher education, offering academic, vocational, international, and religious schooling. Major challenges include inequity, teacher pay, youth unemployment, and governance reforms.

### Ghana
Ghana’s decentralized approach focuses on basic education, gender equity, infrastructure, and enrollment, with policy backing for ICT. Vocational pathways and employment outcomes need strengthening; private schools mainly affect early education.

### Indonesia
Indonesia’s decentralized, twelve-year compulsory system features nationalist and rote-learning curricula, with many Islamic schools. Higher education meets national standards, but faces teacher shortages and rural funding gaps.

### Hong Kong
Hong Kong blends British and Chinese educational traditions, provides free education through junior secondary, selective senior schooling, HKDSE/IB exams, and both Cantonese/English-medium schools. Inclusion for disabled and immigrant students, and vocational partnership initiatives are priorities.

### Armenia
Armenia achieves near-universal literacy with twelve-year compulsory schooling predominantly in Armenian, expanded foreign language use, rigorous standards, and international accreditation. Innovative rural programs and reforms support European integration.

### Sweden
Sweden’s education includes preschool, nine-year compulsory grundskola, and upper secondary (gymnasieskola) with academic/vocational divisions. Tertiary education aligns with Bologna standards, is free for nationals and most Europeans, and admission is based on prior education and language proficiency. The rise of independent (friskolor) schools sparks inequality and grade inflation debates, prompting reviews and reform efforts.

### Norway
Norway's education traces its roots to cathedral schools, evolving to modern compulsory primary and lower secondary levels, and diversified upper secondary academic/vocational tracks. Private schools are limited and strictly regulated. Reforms have merged academic/vocational pathways, promoted IT integration, and improved flexibility; teacher qualifications vary but generally require specialized degrees and pedagogy courses. State-run higher education is overseen by NOKUT for quality assurance.

Norwegian higher education includes universities (offering degrees from bachelor's to PhD including professional fields), accredited university colleges, and a few private institutions. Admission depends on GPA, with students sometimes retaking exams for improvement; vocational and academic distinctions are not formalized at the tertiary level. The sector has evolved from the founding of the University of Oslo in 1811 to recent university mergers and upgrades. Special education is integrated and inclusive, tailored to local schools. The grading system has evolved to letter grades, with teacher-assigned marks and randomized exam subject inclusion on diplomas. The academic year runs August to June, split into semesters, with long summer breaks, holidays, and fewer breaks for university students. Socioeconomic disparities persist in completion rates.
