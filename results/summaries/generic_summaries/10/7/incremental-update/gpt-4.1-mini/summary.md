# Updated Summary of Education in Taiwan, Nigeria, Iran, Ireland, Turkey, Israel, France, Sri Lanka, England, Egypt, Ghana, Indonesia, Hong Kong, Armenia, Sweden, and Norway

This comprehensive summary outlines historical backgrounds, structural organization, curricula, governance, equity challenges, reforms, and innovations in education systems across diverse countries, reflecting global diversity and ongoing developments in education.

## Taiwan  
Taiwan’s education system integrates diverse historical influences, emphasizing publicly funded schooling with state oversight, covering nursery to university, and enforcing 12 years of compulsory education since 2014. Mandarin is primary language. The system provides academic and vocational secondary tracks; its U.S.-inspired higher education admits over 95% of senior high graduates. Teacher training is structured, and special education serves ages 2–22. Challenges include corporal punishment bans and household registration affecting school placements.

## Nigeria  
Education starts with six years of primary and nine years of Universal Basic Education (primary and junior secondary). Secondary education offers academic and vocational paths; federal schools are better funded and merit-based. The Senior Secondary Certificate Examination governs university entry. Postsecondary education includes over 150 universities and polytechnics. Vocational education faces staffing and curriculum shortages. Initiatives tackle adult literacy (69%), digital skills, gender equity, and insecurity limiting rural girls’ schooling.

## Iran  
Iran’s system blends Islamic and scientific traditions with sex-segregated, Islamic curricula post-1979. Education spans optional pre-primary, compulsory elementary and lower secondary, and optional upper secondary tracks. University admission depends on the Konkur exam. Reforms promote equity, critical thinking, and research. Persian is official; Arabic is compulsory; English is prominent at higher education but banned in primary since 2018, replaced by French. Women are over 70% of university students. Gifted education and entrepreneurship grow amid shortages and private school expansion.

## Republic of Ireland  
Compulsory education spans ages 5–16 (or three years post-primary), with English dominant and Irish a core curriculum language. Primary education lasts eight years, mostly Catholic and holistic; multidenominational and fee-paying schools grow. Secondary education includes Junior Cycle, optional Transition Year, and Senior Cycle ending in the Leaving Certificate. Ireland ranks highly in literacy and science, attracts international students, and supports special needs and disadvantaged schools. Early childhood is mainly private, with rising Irish-language preschools. Catholic influence remains amid secularization.

## Turkey  
Republic-era secular reforms centralized education and introduced the Latin alphabet; compulsory education covers 12 years with optional pre-primary. Free primary (ages 6–14) and secondary academic or vocational tracks emphasize apprenticeships and entrepreneurship. National exams select students for 167 universities; Erasmus participation is robust. Religious education, banned 1927–1949, was reintroduced as compulsory ethics/religion courses covering Islam, secularism, and other faiths. English is the main foreign language; minority languages are optional. Curriculum promotes Turkish nationalism and is frequently revised; minority schools face language and religious restrictions.

## Israel  
Compulsory education extends through grade 12 via four streams: state-secular, state-religious, independent Haredi, and Arab schools. Haredi schools focus heavily on religion with limited secular subjects, leading to low matriculation and employment; government efforts aim to improve outcomes. Integrated Jewish-Arab schools promote coexistence. Nearly half of Israeli Jews hold post-secondary degrees; Arab university enrollment rises. Admission requires Bagrut matriculation and Psychometric Test. Universities are subsidized; some tuition benefits link to military service. Inequalities persist in underfunded Arab schools, infrastructure, and dropout rates. Females dominate university enrolment but remain underrepresented in some STEM fields. Teacher strikes occur over low salaries. Curriculum debates involve ethnic stereotyping and coexistence efforts. Municipalities foster innovation and immigrant integration.

## France  
France mandates free, secular, compulsory schooling ages 3–16 through preschool, elementary, middle, and high school, culminating in the baccalauréat or vocational certificates. Public schools are secular; national laws ban religious instruction except in Alsace-Moselle and prohibit religious symbols since 2004. About 18 million students attend public, regulated private religious, or international schools. Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, combining selective Grandes Écoles and open universities. Teachers undergo competitive exams and INSPE training; salaries vary by rank. Tuition is low and state-subsidized with scholarships. CNRS leads research, and since 2018 universities may partner with private colleges offering joint diplomas without degree-awarding powers.

## Sri Lanka  
Sri Lanka’s fragmented education system reflects Buddhist temple and colonial Christian traditions. Free education commenced in 1938. Schools include Buddhist, Hindu, Catholic, Muslim, madrasas, and an Islamic university. Education stages—Primary, Junior Secondary, Senior Secondary, Collegiate—follow national exams and compulsory schooling until age 14. Schools vary from centrally funded National to provincial, private, international, and Buddhist Pirivenas. Private tuition is widespread due to exam competition. State universities admit about 16% of applicants. Vocational training, overseen by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission, faces critiques of rigidity.

## England  
England mandates schooling ages 5–18 (plus early years from 3), with the Early Years Foundation Stage and four National Curriculum Key Stages. Schools include academies, free schools, faith schools, University Technical Colleges, and private schools educating 93% of children. Home education is legal but unfunded. Secondary is mainly comprehensive, with selective grammar and specialist schools. Ofsted inspects schools; uniforms and free meals promote equity. Compulsory education includes Relationship and Sex Education plus Personal, Social, Health, and Economic education. Post-16 education includes sixth forms, further education colleges, apprenticeships, and traineeships. Universities award degrees up to doctorates; tuition fees are capped (~£9,535 in 2024–25) with loans. Prestigious Russell Group universities maintain quality through the Office for Students and QAA. Adult education supports lifelong learning via access courses, distance learning, and community programs aligned with national and European standards. Targeted initiatives aim to improve disadvantaged schools.

## Egypt  
Egypt’s education combines ancient centralized public schooling with basic (ages 4–14), secondary (general and technical/vocational), and higher sectors. Post-1952 reforms introduced free education; current efforts enhance access, curriculum, teaching, and quality assurance amid challenges including low teacher qualifications, corporal punishment, infrastructure deficits, and regional inequities favoring affluent areas. Public education quality is low, prompting widespread tutoring. Vocational training is viewed as insufficient despite new Applied Technology Schools. Higher education enrolls ~30% of eligible students with a 50% graduation rate; governance and quality issues remain. The Al-Azhar system combines religious and general education with gender-segregated schools. Agricultural education integrates 133 secondary schools blending theory and practice. Youth unemployment remains high due to skill mismatches.

## Ghana  
Ghana’s education evolved from oral traditions and colonial schools to 1961 reforms establishing free, compulsory primary education, expanded by 2007–08 to include kindergarten. Education accounts for ~25% of the budget; English is instruction language. Literacy reached 79% by 2018 with gender parity in primary and secondary enrollment. The system serves 7.5 million pupils, 269,000 teachers, and 57,000 schools, with tertiary enrollment around 262,000. Structure includes:  
- Basic Education (ages 4–15; 12 years), free and compulsory, ending with the Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE);  
- Secondary Education with three years of Senior High School offering academic tracks, ending with the WASSCE; vocational training is mostly informal;  
- Tertiary Education expanding with private and public institutions; admission uses WASSCE; mandatory National Service follows graduation.  

Assessment employs diverse grading systems. Governance is decentralized; agencies oversee standards and vocational training. Teacher training happens in public Colleges of Education and universities. Adult education targeted literacy and life skills, raising literacy from 54% (1997) to 71.5% (2010). Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) faces issues in completion, instructor quality, infrastructure, and employment; reforms are led by COTVET. Barriers persist for women, rural and low-income groups despite scholarships and a 2020 online application system. ICT use is mandated despite device access challenges.

## Indonesia  
Indonesia’s education reflects pesantren Islamic boarding schools, colonial stratified schooling, and post-independence secular-nationalist reforms emphasizing Pancasila. Literacy has improved substantially since mid-20th century. Pre-school (PAUD) is mostly private for ages 2–4. Compulsory education covers 12 years: six primary, three junior high, and three senior high with 5–6 school days weekly. Public schools are secular; private/semi-private often have religious affiliations. Curricula stress nationalism and religious values and were decentralized from 1998. Academic and vocational tracks start in junior high with specializations. Special needs students attend separate schools. Over 50 million students are enrolled; primary and middle school are free; senior high charges minimal fees. Tertiary enrollment is low (~36%). The 2013 Curriculum organizes tracks into natural sciences, social sciences, language/literature, and religious studies. Islamic education integrates pesantren, madrasah (religious and secular), and secular Sekolah Islam. Higher education includes diverse institutions under the National Qualification Framework awarding diplomas to doctorates.

## Hong Kong  
Hong Kong’s education evolved into a bilingual, bicultural model under British colonial rule, with ongoing reform stressing experiential learning. Stages include voluntary 3-year Kindergarten, compulsory 6-year Primary, compulsory 3-year Junior Secondary, selective 3-year Senior Secondary, and various tertiary options. Half-day schooling with two sessions is transitioning to full day. Discipline is emphasized with Education Bureau guidelines. Criticisms include rote learning and exam focus despite reforms promoting group work and open-ended tasks; high workloads and ineffective history education are noted. Instruction mediums shifted mostly from English to Cantonese, with some EMI and Putonghua schools. Graduates receive the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) or International Baccalaureate. Vocational education offers specializations. About 175 international schools offer UK GCSE/A-levels and IB curricula. Higher education expanded post-1975; undergraduate degrees extended to four years since 2012. Eight UGC-funded universities rank highly (HKU, CUHK, HKUST), alongside other approved and self-financing institutions. Adult education is popular. Special education emphasizes integration except for severe disabilities. Immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children receive free induction programs. School sectors include Government, Aided, DSS, Private, Private International, and English Schools Foundation. Reforms plan to shorten secondary education and integrate AI tools. Cooperation with Guangdong enhances vocational training and Greater Bay Area exchanges.

## Armenia  
Armenia, achieving 100% literacy by 1960 under Soviet-style education, reformed to emphasize Armenian language and culture. Preschool covers ages 2–6/7; compulsory secondary lasts 12 years (primary, middle, high school) with general and specialized tracks. Enrollment is high in primary and secondary but lower at high school versus EU/OECD averages. State exams determine university/vocational access. Public schools (97%) are state-funded with regulated curricula and high teacher certification, though funding challenges persist. English is increasingly emphasized; Chinese language schools exist. Private schools comply with national standards but have more autonomy and rely on tuition/donations, possibly deepening disparities.  
Higher education includes ~26 state and 33 private universities aligned with international standards and the Bologna Process. Notable institutions include Yerevan State University (1920) and others specializing in medicine, engineering, and linguistics. COAF SMART center promotes non-formal and rural skills education. Armenia participates in EU mobility programs (Erasmus Mundus, Jean Monnet, TEMPUS, Erasmus+). A national quality assurance agency meets European standards. The EU-Armenia Education Policy Dialogue supports ongoing reforms. However, student absenteeism and dropout rates surged (2002–2005), linked to child labor and education quality. New centers like Tumo offer youth modern technology-based skills training.

## Sweden  
Sweden evolved from a 19th-century primary school to a comprehensive model with compulsory schooling ages 6–16, national tests, and 16 subjects. Upper secondary offers 22 vocational/preparatory programs qualifying for university. Admission is grade-based with some exams/auditions. Higher education follows Bologna standards with bachelor’s, master’s, licentiates, and doctorates; grading varies, convertible to ECTS. Swedish and English proficiency are required; the SweSAT exam is used. Student aid is available; education is free to Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens. Anti-discrimination policies are legally mandated.  
Independent schools (friskolor), publicly funded since 1992, cannot charge fees/discriminate but accept donations. Types vary (Montessori, international, religious, special needs, elite sports), enrolling over 10% of pupils by 2008. Debates persist on profit motives and grade inflation; Sweden is noted for a mixed public-private model.  
Sweden ranks first in early childhood education per UNICEF (2008), though PISA scores dipped 2006–2012 with slight improvement by 2015; PIRLS showed similar trends. Immigration and testing irregularities influenced scores, prompting investigations. Concerns about school violence led to national guidelines. The system emphasizes social equality, indigenous (Sámi) education, special needs, and adult learning (komvux). Higher vocational education offers short, practical programs distinct from traditional university tracks.

## Norway  
Norway’s education traces back to cathedral schools and the Reformation, evolving into the mandatory folkeskole by 1889 and later the grunnskole in the 1970s–80s. Education comprises three tiers: elementary (barneskole, 6–12) focusing on basic skills and social learning without grades; lower secondary (ungdomsskole, 13–16) offering graded assessment and electives affecting upper secondary admission; and upper secondary (videregående, 16–19) providing general and vocational tracks with about 73% graduation after reforms since 1994. Preschool teachers require bachelor’s degrees; adjunct and master’s level lecturers teach higher grades.  
Higher education requires university admission certification, is mostly public and self-governing with quality overseen by the Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education. Admission is based on upper secondary grades, with some retaking exams to improve GPA. Higher education divides into:  
- Universities: theorized-focused, autonomous curricula, offering bachelor’s to PhD and professional studies;  
- University colleges: bachelor to PhD programs, requiring accreditation for advanced degrees;  
- Private schools: niche specializations, attended by about 10% of students.  

Norway formally distinguishes little between vocational and academic higher education. Historical milestones include the 1750 Military Academy and the 2018 University College of South-Eastern Norway attaining university status.  
Special education policy since the 1970s integrates support mainly in local schools, with assistance from Educational/Psychological Services and special educators. Universities legally ensure inclusive, flexible learning environments.  
Grading transitioned from a 1.0–6.0 scale to the Bologna letter system; lower education uses a 1–6 scale with 2 as passing. Upper secondary diplomas rely on teacher-assigned grades, with some students randomly selected for formal exams ("trekkfag").  
The academic year features two semesters (August–December and January–June) with breaks including eight weeks’ summer vacation, and holidays such as Easter, May 1, Constitution Day (May 17), Ascension, Pentecost, and Whit Monday. Universities generally do not have fall or winter breaks.
