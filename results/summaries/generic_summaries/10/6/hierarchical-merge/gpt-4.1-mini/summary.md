# Master Summary: Comparative Education Systems Worldwide

## Middle East and Asia

**Iran** operates a centrally governed education system combining Islamic and traditional influences, compulsory through elementary and lower secondary levels with academic and vocational tracks thereafter. University entry is competitive via the Konkur exam. Despite advancements in STEM fields and high female university enrollment, challenges include teacher shortages and inequities linked to costly private schooling.

**Taiwan** mandates 12 years of education focused on Mandarin proficiency and standardized assessments delineating academic and vocational pathways. Over 95% of high school graduates pursue higher education, supported by subsidized public universities. Teacher training is continuous and university-based. Widespread supplementary education, including cram schools and bilingual kindergartens, attracts international learners, alongside inclusive alternative and special education programs.

**Turkey** offers secular compulsory education structured as 4+4+4 years, with balanced academic and vocational secondary options. University admission relies on national exams, with over 167 universities, including technical institutions. The system includes religious studies alongside secular ethics and nationalism. Minority language education and early foreign language instruction are part of the curriculum, amid critique over frequent reforms and political influence.

**Israel** features a robust system aligned with its technological economy, culminating in Bagrut matriculation exams for higher education and elite military entry. STEM fields and female student representation are strong, though underfunding causes disparities within the Arab sector. University admission combines matriculation and psychometric testing; government-subsidized institutions hold strong global reputations.

**Hong Kong** provides compulsory education from ages 6 to 18, ending with the HKDSE or IB diploma. Instruction primarily occurs in Cantonese with English and Putonghua presence. The system transitioned from rote to open-ended learning and includes government, private, international, and English Schools Foundation schools. Recent curricular reforms emphasize patriotism and national security, impacted by political changes affecting university enrollment. Higher education has expanded since the 1970s, with developments in AI and regional collaborations. Vocational education is overseen by the Vocational Training Council.

**Sri Lanka** mandates education up to Grade 9, with national examinations guiding university admission. State universities offer tuition-free undergraduate education but capacity limitations push many towards overseas or vocational training. The education landscape includes government, private, international, Islamic schools, and a competitive private tuition market. Higher education is regulated by the University Grants Commission; vocational education falls under the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission.

**Egypt** features free, compulsory basic education (ages 4–14), then secondary education divided into general, technical, and vocational tracks, followed by higher education through public and private universities. Challenges include reliance on rote learning, underqualified teachers, resource limits, governance issues, and unequal access. Current reforms focus on improving quality, accreditation, research, labor market alignment, distance learning, and specialized diplomas.

**Indonesia** mandates 12 years of education (6 primary, 3 junior high free; 3 senior high/vocational possibly fee-based) across public secular, private, semi-private religious schools, and special education. Islamic education operates through pesantren, madrasah, and sekolah islam. Post-1998 decentralization emphasized subject specialization and Islamic content integration. Despite high primary enrollment and survival, tertiary participation remains low. Challenges include teacher shortages, low salaries, and regional disparities.

## Africa and Europe

**Nigeria** mandates primary and secondary education supplemented by vocational institutes. Educational quality varies, with federal schools outperforming many state-run ones, while private and religious schools offer superior, costly facilities. National exams govern progression, but vocational education is underfunded and fragmented. Female education faces socio-cultural and security challenges, particularly in rural areas. Adult literacy is about 69%, with international efforts aiming to enhance digital literacy by 2030.

**Ghana** provides approximately 20 years of formal English-medium education with key national exams. Despite increasing enrollment and significant budget allocation (~25%), the system struggles with teacher shortages, quality concerns, limited vocational infrastructure, and equity issues favoring wealthier males in tertiary education. Non-formal education supports a literacy rate near 79%.

**France** operates a centralized, secular, and compulsory system from preschool through lycée, culminating in the national baccalauréat. Public universities coexist with elite Grandes écoles. Teachers require master’s degrees and certifications. The curriculum promotes republican values and bans conspicuous religious symbols in public schools since 2004, though private schools may include religious instruction. France maintains extensive regional international school networks.

**Republic of Ireland** requires education from ages 6 to 16 or longer, offering free upper secondary education since 1967. Its system spans primary (ages 4–12) and secondary cycles ending with the Leaving Certificate necessary for university. Instruction is mainly English with core Irish language education and immersion options. Religious education is typically church-led. Special education is legislated and well-resourced. Ireland ranks among the world’s top 20 education systems and attracts many international students.

**England** mandates education from ages 5 to 16, extended to 18 via compulsory education or training. The National Curriculum divides schooling into key stages, culminating in GCSEs and offering post-16 options such as A-levels, BTECs, and T Levels. Approximately 93% attend state-funded schools under Ofsted oversight, with increasing emphasis on Special Educational Needs and Disabilities. England holds strong global literacy and numeracy rankings and boasts a competitive higher education sector appealing to international students.

**Sweden** provides free preschool from age three, followed by nine years of compulsory primary education and three years of upper secondary Gymnasium with academic and vocational tracks leading to higher education. Diverse learners, including Sámi, special needs, adults, and homeschoolers, are supported. Publicly funded independent schools coexist with scrutiny over quality and funding. Higher education aligns with the Bologna Process, offering bachelor’s, master’s (with thesis), doctoral, and vocational degrees. While early childhood education is highly regarded internationally, global assessment results are mixed, influenced by demographic factors.

**Norway** requires education from ages 6 to 16, encompassing primary (grades 1–7), lower secondary (8–10), and upper secondary general or vocational tracks with a 2+2 apprenticeship model (73% graduation). Recent reforms prioritize IT integration, pedagogical flexibility, and formal teacher qualification. Special education operates under universal design principles. Higher education includes universities, colleges, and private institutions with competitive GPA-based admissions. National exams balance teacher assessments and randomized testing to evaluate academic and practical skills.

**Armenia** offers free, compulsory K-12 education with increasing specialization at higher levels, beginning with preschool from age two. Nearly all schools are public but face resource challenges. Post-independence reforms emphasize Armenian language and culture and expand Russian, English, and Chinese studies. Its higher education sector includes 26 state and 33 private universities, with strong participation in European frameworks like the Bologna Process and Erasmus+. Technology centers support rural youth skill development. Challenges include absenteeism and dropout due to socio-economic issues like child labor. Ongoing EU-supported reforms aim to improve access and quality nationally.

---

This overview highlights the diversity, strengths, challenges, and ongoing reforms characterizing global education systems, reflecting regional cultural influences, governance structures, and socio-economic contexts.
