# Updated Summary of Education in Iran, Taiwan, Turkey, Nigeria, France, the Republic of Ireland, England, Israel, Ghana, Sri Lanka, Hong Kong, Egypt, Sweden, Indonesia, Norway, and Armenia

## Education in Iran  
Iran’s education integrates pre-Islamic and Islamic traditions, with post-1979 reforms emphasizing gender segregation, religious curricula, and mandatory Islamic prayer by 2023. Public education spans optional pre-primary through university, funded by about 5% of GDP. Persian is the national language; Arabic is compulsory in grades 7–12 alongside increasing English instruction. Hybrid and digital learning expanded post-pandemic. Strengths include engineering graduates and female STEM participation; challenges involve teacher shortages, rural disparities, ideological restrictions, and growing private schooling impacting equity.

## Education in Taiwan  
Since 2014, Taiwan mandates twelve years of compulsory education with a broad curriculum including Mandarin, math, science, English from grade 3, native languages, arts, and physical education. Corporal punishment is banned but incidents occur. High school graduates mostly pursue university, including doctoral studies and internships. Private preschools and cram schools complement formal education.

## Education in Turkey  
Turkey’s secular “4+4+4” system features optional pre-primary, eight years of primary, and various secondary tracks like vocational and Imam Hatip religious schools. The 2017 removal of evolution in favor of Islamic content highlights ideological shifts. Minority language education is limited and optional; governance involves national councils and presidential oversight. Internationalization efforts include entrance exams and Erasmus scholarships.

## Education in Nigeria  
Beginning at age five, Nigeria offers six years primary, nine years Universal Basic Education, and six years secondary schooling. Federal elite schools contrast with under-resourced states; private and religious schools are generally better equipped. National exams regulate access to over 150 universities/polytechnics. Vocational training grows amid shortages. Female enrollment rises despite social challenges; rural areas struggle with infrastructure and security. Adult literacy averages 69%, lower in northern regions.

## Education in France  
France provides secular, free education compulsory from ages 6–12, encompassing preschool, elementary, collège, and lycée culminating in the baccalauréat. Grandes Écoles offer selective higher education after preparatory classes. Teachers hold advanced certifications; strict laïcité prohibits conspicuous religious symbols.

## Education in the Republic of Ireland  
Upper secondary education is free and compulsory ages 6–16 or three years post-primary, with primary schools mainly church-controlled. Secondary options include voluntary, community colleges, and comprehensives; about 3% attend Irish-language Gaelcholáistí. Junior and Senior Cycles conclude with standardized exams. Ireland is noted for educational quality, international student attraction, and support for special needs and disadvantaged schools.

## Education in England  
Compulsory education spans ages 5–18, transitioning from religious to predominantly state-funded schools. The National Curriculum covers core subjects through Key Stages 1–4; post-16 includes sixth forms and vocational qualifications. Private and home schooling coexist. Universities offer doctoral degrees with capped fees and student support. Adult education includes access courses and Open University programs. UK students excel in literacy and STEM; funding for special educational needs has increased.

## Education in Israel  
Israel’s system includes state secular, state religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab streams, with Haredi and Arab populations projected at 60% by 2030. Haredi education restricts secular subjects, limiting labor integration; Arab education is underfunded and underperforming, with affirmative action addressing disparities. Nearly half of Israeli Jews hold post-secondary degrees. Matriculation exams guide military and career paths. Teacher strikes occur over wages and reforms; textbooks face criticism over Arab portrayals. Female students outperform males overall, dominating higher education enrollment, though males lead in STEM.

## Education in Ghana  
With colonial missionary roots, Ghana offers free compulsory primary education and expanded universities. The 1987 Education Act established 12-year pre-tertiary education emphasizing vocational skills and compulsory schooling ages 6–14. Basic education ends with BECE; secondary with WASSCE. Governance is decentralized with about 23% education expenditure; donor funding declined post-2005. Teacher training occurs at public colleges and via distance education. Adult literacy improved, focusing on women and rural areas; vocational training remains limited. COVID-19 accelerated digital learning; ICT is a development priority.

## Education in Sri Lanka  
Drawing on 2300 years of Buddhist educational history, Sri Lanka’s colonial legacy introduced denominational schools; free education since 1938 emphasizes national languages, equity, and religious freedom. The system includes primary; junior and senior secondary; collegiate; and tertiary education, compulsory until age 14. Key exams include the Grade 5 Scholarship and G.C.E. Levels. Elite National Schools coexist with resource-poor Provincial Schools; Madrasas and private international schools also operate. High exam competition drives private tuition demand. Universities admit about 16% of qualified applicants; others attend vocational colleges or study abroad. Vocational training offers certificates to degrees. Challenges include graduate unemployment and resistance to private universities; digital learning grows mainly in urban areas.

## Education in Hong Kong  
Hong Kong’s education evolved from Chinese and missionary bilingual schools to a system balancing Chinese traditions and Western education. Since the 1970s, funding expansion increased access; post-1997 curricula emphasize active learning and patriotism, especially after national security law suppressions. Cooperation with Guangdong since 2023 supports vocational and talent education. The system includes voluntary kindergarten (3 years), compulsory primary (6 years), junior secondary (3 years), selective senior secondary (3 years), and competitive tertiary education. Discipline is emphasized; history education remains limited. Language instruction shifted predominantly to Cantonese post-1997; 20–30% of schools retain English-medium status, while Putonghua use rises. Numerous international schools offer British, American, and IB curricula. Tertiary education includes eight highly ranked universities and self-financing institutions. Adult education features government-supported evening and Open University programs. Special education promotes inclusion and immigrant support. School types vary widely. Digital innovations and AI tools like ChatGPT have been integrated since 2023.

## Education in Egypt  
Building on ancient and medieval Islamic scholarly traditions, Egypt’s education developed dual traditional-religious and modern secular systems, with British colonial neglect limiting access and vocational focus. Post-1952 revolution introduced free public education. Despite reforms since the 1990s, challenges include low quality, rote learning, inadequate infrastructure, unequal access, reliance on private tutoring, disciplinary issues, low teacher salaries, and youth unemployment.

The centralized system comprises: Basic Education (ages 4–14), Secondary Education (ages 15–17) with general and technical tracks, and Higher Education with about 30% enrollment. Fifteen universities appear in QS rankings; a new engineering diploma begins 2025. Over 180 private international schools offer varied curricula. Separate Al-Azhar religious education operates alongside, with gender segregation. Agricultural education involves numerous secondary schools. COVID-19 accelerated distance education, while national plans focus on improving access, teacher development, curricula, and quality.

## Education in Sweden  
Sweden’s education, rooted in the 1842 national school act, emphasizes universal access, equality, and comprehensive schooling. Preschool serves children from age one, free for limited hours from age three, focusing on play and early learning. Compulsory Grundskola lasts nine years (ages 6–16), covering 16 core subjects with optional languages and grading supported by tests. Secondary education (Gymnasieskola) is elective and three years, offering academic and vocational tracks with near-universal enrollment. Special education includes Sámi schools and provisions for special needs. Adult education supports lifelong learning; homeschooling is rare and mainly health-related. School violence policies have been implemented since 2014.

Upper secondary divides into preparatory and vocational programs, both university-qualifying. About two-thirds pursue preparatory tracks focused on social sciences, natural sciences, technology, arts, and languages; one-third vocational fields like health and trades. Diploma projects and eligibility determine university admission; special programs assist others.

Higher education requires gymnasieskola equivalency and includes bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, with Swedish and English proficiency mandatory. Independent vocational education integrates work and theory. Since 1992, independent schools (friskolor) funded by municipalities serve 15–30% of students, offering alternative pedagogies, including religious and international profiles, though critiques arise about standards and profit motives.

Sweden ranks highly in early childhood education but saw PISA and PIRLS declines partly linked to immigration and testing issues, prompting scrutiny and political debate.

## Education in Indonesia  
Indonesia’s education reflects Islamic boarding schools (pesantren) focusing on Quran, Arabic, sharia, and traditions alongside modern subjects; these vary from progressive to traditionalist and remain mostly moderate. The madrassa integrates religious and secular education but typically ranks below pesantren. Islamic education aligns with national school levels (RA for pre-school to MA for senior secondary). Higher education includes universities, institutes, polytechnics, and academies offering academic, vocational, and professional programs.

Since 2012, Indonesia follows the National Qualification Framework (KKNI) covering diploma to doctorate and specialist levels. Pre-school (PAUD) mostly private, is non-compulsory. Compulsory 12 years encompass Sekolah Dasar (6 years), Junior High (3 years), and Senior High (general or vocational tracks). Religious schooling and curricula adapt to Islamic, Christian, and Catholic contexts. Enrollment is high in primary but declines at secondary and tertiary levels; teacher certification and student-teacher ratios have improved, though remote shortages persist. Challenges include funding, equity, teacher quality, and resource distribution, especially in remote regions.

## Education in Norway  
Norway’s education stems from ancient traditions, evolving into a three-tier system: barneskole (elementary, ages 6–12), ungdomsskole (lower secondary, 13–16), and videregående (upper secondary, 16–19). Education is compulsory from 6 to 16 years with rare private schools. Elementary focuses on foundational skills with teacher and parental assessments instead of formal grades; lower secondary introduces grading and electives influencing upper secondary admission.

Lower secondary students (13–16) may take Grade 10 exams early if exempt. Norwegian 15-year-olds perform well in OECD assessments but lag behind top countries. Support for slower learners is criticized.

Upper secondary schools, mostly public, had a 73% graduation rate in 2017. Since 1994, general, mercantile, and vocational paths merged with flexible credit transfers. Vocational training typically follows a "2+2" model: two years in school plus two years apprenticeship. IT integration is mandatory with laptops provided. Graduates celebrate as "russ" before final exams.

Preschool teachers require a bachelor’s degree; adjunct teachers (bachelor’s plus pedagogy) teach mainly grades 5–10; lecturers (master’s plus pedagogy) teach grades 8–13 with academic focus.

Higher education demands a university admissions certificate or equivalent, offering bachelor, master, and PhD degrees including professional studies. University colleges provide broad programs needing accreditation for advanced degrees. Private higher education exists but is less common (~10%). Admissions are competitive, and students may retake exams for better grades. Vocational and academic higher education distinctions are blurred.

Since the 1970s, special education policies emphasize integration in local schools with tailored support for students unable to benefit from regular teaching. Special education involves qualified specialists supporting students and families, emphasizing inclusive and flexible learning environments mandated by law.

Grading scales differ: universities used a 1.0–6.0 scale (4.0+ passing); lower education now uses 1–6, with 2 as lowest passing grade. Upper secondary diplomas combine teacher-assigned grades and random exam grades ("trekkfag"). The academic year has two semesters (Aug–Dec, Jan–Jun) with long breaks including 8-week summer vacation, plus Christmas, fall, and winter breaks. Norway observes 13 national holidays during which schools are closed.

## Education in Armenia  
Armenia achieved universal literacy by 1960 and maintains a Soviet-influenced education system adapted to Armenian language and culture, with widespread Russian and growing English instruction; Chinese language education began in 2018. Preschool (ages 2–6/7) emphasizes communication, languages, and basic math.

Compulsory and free secondary education spans 12 years: primary (grades 1–4), middle (5–9) with a basic education certificate, and high school (10–12) offering general and specialized tracks. State exams at secondary completion determine university or vocational school entrance. Public schools cover 97% of institutions and are government-funded and regulated, though funding challenges remain. Private schools operate under Ministry of Education licensing, tailoring teaching methods and staff hiring within national curriculum standards; quality varies.

Leading universities include Yerevan State University (1920), Yerevan State Medical University (1930), State Engineering University, Yerevan State Linguistic University, and the American University of Armenia (1991), offering instruction in Armenian, Russian, and English across sciences, engineering, medicine, humanities, and business. Armenia hosts 26 state and 33 private higher education institutions, strong in STEM and Armenian studies. The COAF SMART center provides free non-formal education for rural youth, alongside technology-based centers like Tumo.

Armenia joined key European higher education agreements—the Lisbon Recognition Convention (1997), Bologna Process (2005), and became a full member of the European Higher Education Area—facilitating alignment with European standards. It participates in EU programs such as Erasmus Mundus, Jean Monnet, Erasmus+, and TEMPUS to enhance cooperation and modernization. A quality assurance agency meeting European standards was established, joining ENQA and EQAR in 2017. The EU-Armenia Education Policy Dialogue began in 2019 to support reforms.

Despite progress, studies noted a sharp increase in student absenteeism and dropout rates (250% rise between 2002-2005), linked to child labor and education quality concerns.

## Overall  
This comprehensive summary illustrates diverse education systems from Iran to Armenia, detailing histories, structures, curricula, governance, challenges, reforms, and societal influences. Common themes include balancing tradition and modernization, expanding access, addressing equity gaps, integrating vocational training, promoting inclusivity and specialized support, and leveraging technology amid shifting political, economic, and cultural contexts. European integration efforts in Armenia exemplify regional collaboration for enhancing quality and standards, while countries worldwide face ongoing challenges with educational quality, equity, infrastructure, and adapting to modern demands.
