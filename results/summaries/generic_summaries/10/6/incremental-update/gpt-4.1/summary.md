## Summary

Education systems worldwide—spanning countries such as Iran, Taiwan, Turkey, Nigeria, France, Ireland, England, Israel, Ghana, Sri Lanka, Hong Kong, Egypt, Sweden, Norway, and Armenia—reflect diverse models influenced by unique historical, cultural, and social contexts. Common reform goals include inclusivity, access, quality, technology adoption, professional teacher development, and responsiveness to evolving societal needs and global standards.

### International Education Systems

- **Middle Eastern, Asian, and African Countries:**  
  Iran blends Islamic and Persian traditions in its curricula. Taiwan and Turkey have academic and vocational pathways, with Turkey balancing secularism and inclusion. Nigeria strives for increased access and literacy. France emphasizes secularism and varied learning tracks; Ireland fosters creativity, inclusiveness, and special needs support. Indonesia’s twelve-year system features predominant state schools, multiple educational tracks, and challenges in teacher quality and funding, managed by national ministries. England provides a mix of state and independent schools, health-aware curricula, competitive universities, and adult programs. Israel’s system covers secular, religious, and ethnic streams, with efforts toward integration, gender equity, and higher education technology. Ghana’s system combines English-based instruction, adult literacy, technical/vocational options, and ICT-based reforms. Sri Lanka ensures free public education, competitive universities, and alternatives to address systemic rigidity.

- **Hong Kong:**  
  Has government, aided, private, and international schools in an exam-driven environment, with reforms focusing on open-ended learning, technology integration, patriotic education, and vocational options.

- **Egypt:**  
  Maintains a tradition-based system with contemporary reforms, compulsory primary education, and various academic, vocational, and religious tracks, facing persistent challenges in quality, equity, and youth unemployment.

- **Sweden:**  
  Features broad participation since 19th-century reforms, a predominance of female teachers, strong early childhood education, tuition-free access for Swedish/EU/EEA students, academic/vocational tracks, and standardized qualifications. Independent schools are publicly funded with regulated admissions. Recent reforms address achievement gaps, exam integrity, school violence, and discrimination.

- **Norway:**  
  Rooted in historical and reformist traditions, the system offers compulsory elementary and secondary education, life-stage tailored upper secondary pathways, and integrated IT through Kunnskapsløftet reforms. Teacher qualifications are high, and graduation rates are strong. Special education is inclusively supported, universities ensure universal design, grading varies across education levels, and higher education is mostly state-run with competitive admissions.

- **Armenia:**  
  Armenia boasts near-universal literacy and has transitioned from Soviet-of-origin curricula to Armenian-centered models, with compulsory free secondary education and robust primary/middle school enrollment. Secondary schools offer general and specialized tracks with state exams for university entrance. Public schools strictly follow the national curriculum, while private ones provide innovative, flexible approaches but face equity and qualification issues. Higher education includes a strong network of state and private universities, excelling in science, medicine, and IT, often with multilingual courses. Armenia’s integration with European standards is advanced through participation in the Lisbon Recognition Convention and Bologna Process, making it a full member of the European Higher Education Area. Armenian institutions engage in EU programs (Erasmus Mundus, Jean Monnet, TEMPUS) and meet European quality assurance benchmarks. However, student absenteeism and dropouts linked to child labor and education quality remain significant challenges. Armenia has also developed prominent technology-based after-school centers (Tumo Center, COAF SMART Center) to foster 21st-century skills and rural youth development.

---

In summary, these countries are actively advancing educational equity, continuous reform, technology integration, and teacher professionalism, while broadening access and adapting to the demands of a rapidly changing global landscape. Each system balances tradition with innovation, working to overcome persistent challenges and promote lifelong learning.
