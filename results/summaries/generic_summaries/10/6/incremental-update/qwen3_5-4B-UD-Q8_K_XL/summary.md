### **Global Educational Systems: Egypt, Sweden, Indonesia, Norway, and Armenia**

**Egypt: Historical Legacy and Contemporary Struggles**
Egypt's system evolved from ancient institutions to a modern dual structure featuring free, compulsory basic education (ages 4–14) and mixed secondary tracks. Despite high primary enrollment (96.9%), the country faces significant challenges including low standardized test scores, high student-teacher ratios, and a heavy reliance on private tutoring (61–70%). Consequently, youth unemployment among graduates remains critically high at 36.1% (2019), prompting initiatives like Applied Technology Schools.

**Sweden: Comprehensive and Universal System**
Sweden expanded from limited 19th-century access to a comprehensive framework centered on a nine-year *grundskola* established in 1962. This includes a three-stage compulsory curriculum with national testing, followed by a nearly universal (99%) three-year upper secondary program (*gymnasieskola*) divided into preparatory and vocational tracks. Higher education is integrated into the Bologna Process, offering degrees largely free for citizens and EU/EEA residents. While preschool is free for ages 3+, the system faces scrutiny regarding private sector influence; independent schools (*friskolor*) receive public funding and account for over 10% of pupils, though critics argue they may distort outcomes or exacerbate inequality. International performance has been volatile, with PISA and PIRLS scores declining in the 2000s before recovering, a trend partly attributed to immigration and later found to be skewed by testing exemptions.

**Indonesia: Historical Evolution, Structure, and Challenges**
Indonesia's landscape shifted from segregated Dutch colonial tiers to a post-independence model prioritizing nationalism over elitism. This transition prioritized *Pondok Pesantren* and Islamic schools, raising literacy to 46.7% by 1961, though significant gender gaps persisted initially. Today, the system mandates 12 years of compulsory education across 300,000 schools serving over 50 million students, with only 36.31% participating in tertiary education. The curriculum historically emphasized *Pancasila* ideology and rote learning, shifting toward specialization groups after the 1998 decentralization reforms and the 2013 curriculum. The system faces critical challenges including teacher shortages in remote areas, certification issues, low salaries, and uneven distribution of resources. Islamic education is diverse, comprising religious *Pesantren*, ideologically varied *Madrasah*, and secular *Sekolah Islam*. Despite 99.35% of kindergartens being privately operated, the nation grapples with funding, equity, and quality disparities. Public schools face resistance from some Muslims, leading to a preference for *pesantren* or *madrassas*. The qualification framework was standardized in 2012 under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI).

**Norway: Historical Context, Structure, Reforms, and Specialized Features**
Norway's education dates back 2,000 years, with mandatory primary education established in 1827 and expanded to nine years by 1969 before reorganization into the *grunnskole* in the 1970s. The system is divided into elementary, lower secondary, and upper secondary stages, with compulsory education for grades 1–10. Primary education emphasizes play and social skills without grades, while grading begins in lower secondary, where electives determine university acceptance.
*   **Lower Secondary:** Students perform well internationally, though gaps remain in advanced mathematics compared to nations like China. Criticism exists regarding slow pacing.
*   **Upper Secondary (*Videregående skole*):** Reformed in 1994 to merge vocational and general tracks (allowing credit transfer) and refined in 2006 ("Kunnskapsløftet") to mandate IT integration.
*   **Private Sector:** Standard private schools were legalized in 2005. Public attendance remains dominant (93% in 2007), and institutions must follow international curricula while prohibiting discrimination.
*   **Higher Education:** Most institutions are state-run and accredited by NOKUT, divided into Universities (theoretical/professional), University Colleges (vocational/professional training), and Private Schools (business/arts, 10% of students). Admission generally requires a general certificate, though a specific law allows entry for those over 23 with significant work experience.
*   **Special Education & Grading:** Policy mandates inclusive education in local ordinary schools since the 1970s. Universities utilize a 1.0–6.0 scale (4.0+ passing), while lower education uses a 1–6 scale.
*   **Examinations & Schedule:** Upper secondary diplomas rely on teacher-assigned marks supplemented by random "trekkfag" exams. The academic year runs from August to June with two semesters and an 8-week summer vacation.

**Armenia: Transition from Soviet Model to Independent System**
Armenia has transitioned from a Soviet-controlled model to an independent system emphasizing Armenian culture and languages, though Russian remains a second language.
*   **K-12 Structure:** The system includes preschool (ages 2–7), primary (Grades 1–4), middle school (Grades 5–9), and high school (Grades 10–12) divided into general and specialized tracks. Public schools dominate (97%) and are state-funded, with compulsory secondary education being free. Entrance exams at the end of secondary school are critical for university or vocational placement.
*   **Higher Education:** Armenia has significantly integrated its higher education system into European frameworks by joining the Bologna Process, the European Higher Education Area, and various EU mobility and quality assurance programs. The sector is robust in Medicine, Engineering, IT, and Humanities, featuring institutions like Yerevan State University (founded 1920) and Yerevan State Medical University (founded 1930).
*   **Challenges & Initiatives:** Despite high enrollment rates, the country faces severe challenges regarding student absenteeism and dropout rates, which are strongly linked to child labor and educational quality issues. To address skill gaps, Armenia has established several new technology-based centers focused on providing youth with 21st-century competencies. Accessibility initiatives, such as the COAF SMART educational center, provide free non-formal training in rural communities.
