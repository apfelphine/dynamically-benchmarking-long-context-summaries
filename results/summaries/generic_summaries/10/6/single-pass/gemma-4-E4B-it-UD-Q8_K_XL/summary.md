## Comparative Overview of Global Education Systems

### Historical Development and Structure
Educational systems across various nations have evolved through distinct historical phases. In **Iran**, pre-Islamic Persia showed an early emphasis on knowledge, while the Safavid and Qajar eras introduced modern educational foundations. The **Qajar era** saw non-state initiatives promoting reform. In **Egypt**, education evolved from ancient learning centers like "Bar Ankh" to structured Islamic institutions (Al-Azhar) and later integrated modern, French-inspired secular systems under Muhammad Ali.

**Turkey** transitioned from religiously segregated channels to a unified, secular system under the Republic, while **Taiwan** adapted its education following the ROC retreat, balancing state control with revitalized private schooling. **Hong Kong**'s education was shaped by British missionary influence, shifting towards a bilingual, bicultural curriculum, and later adopting the Target Oriented Curriculum (TOC).

### Compulsory Education and Curriculum
**Sweden** operates a mandatory nine-year comprehensive school system, emphasizing a broad curriculum across compulsory subjects, with assessments beginning in Year 6. **Norway** mandates education from age 6 to 16 through the *grunnskole* (foundation school), which is split into compulsory elementary and lower secondary levels. In **Ireland**, compulsory education runs from age 6 to 16, featuring a Junior Cycle and Senior Cycle.

In **India**, the system is highly centralized, with the curriculum set by the Ministry of Education, promoting a "common culture" to aid immigrant integration. **Sri Lanka** mandates schooling until Grade 9, with the G.C.E. Ordinary Level serving as a key checkpoint before higher education. **Nigeria** transitioned from the Universal Primary Education program to the Universal Basic Education (UBE) system, integrating primary and junior secondary education.

### Higher Education and Vocational Training
**France** organizes higher education into public universities and elite *grandes écoles*, using the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS). **England** utilizes a diverse structure, ranging from state-funded academies to independent schools, with post-16 education including A-levels and vocational options like T Levels. **Israel** values higher education as a path to socioeconomic status, with state-subsidized universities and a mandatory *Bagrut* matriculation exam for entry.

**Ghana** sees rapid growth in tertiary education, heavily supported by the private sector, while **Indonesia** features a diverse structure, blending modern universities with traditional Islamic *Pesantren*. **Norway** differentiates between large state-run universities and specialized university colleges, while **Sweden** aligns its degrees with the Bologna Process.

### Access, Equity, and Challenges
Equity remains a global concern. In **Iran**, Islamization was a major post-revolutionary shift, although dropout rates remain high. **Nigeria** faces disparities between elite federal schools and under-resourced state schools, alongside challenges in vocational education quality. **Egypt** struggles with a "wealth gap," where private tutoring has become near-compulsory due to perceived public education quality issues.

**Kenya** highlights systemic issues, including a shortage of teachers and poor infrastructure, while **Armenia** grapples with high student absenteeism linked to child labor. **China** (Hong Kong context) faces pressures from national security policies and a shift towards experiential learning. **Mexico** (not provided, but comparative analysis is implied) is absent, but **Denmark** (implied by comparison to Sweden) is not detailed.

### Key Policy and Modern Trends
Several systems integrate modern pedagogical shifts. **Hong Kong** has moved towards active and experiential learning, while **Sweden** emphasizes a broad, balanced curriculum. **Ghana** has increased its focus on ICT in education, though implementation lags behind policy. **International programs** are significant in **Sri Lanka** and **Hong Kong**, while **Turkey** has recently introduced curriculum changes, including the incorporation of Islamic concepts.
