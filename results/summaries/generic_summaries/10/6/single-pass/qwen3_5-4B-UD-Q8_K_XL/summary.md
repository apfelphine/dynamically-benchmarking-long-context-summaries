# Global Education Systems Summary

This summary covers the structure, history, challenges, and key reforms of education systems in Iran, Taiwan, Turkey, Nigeria, France, Ireland, England, Israel, Ghana, Sri Lanka, Hong Kong, Egypt, Sweden, Indonesia, Norway, and Armenia.

## Education in Iran
Iran's education system has evolved from ancient religious schools to a modern, centralized state apparatus heavily influenced by Islamic values. The system is compulsory from ages 6 to 18, though enrollment in upper secondary education is not mandatory. Historically marked by the Safavid emphasis on science and the Qajar era's first organized reforms, the 1979 Revolution led to the Islamization of textbooks and the segregation of students by sex. Today, the Ministry of Education controls planning, financing, and curriculum, while universities operate under a centralized system where graduates often commit to government service. Despite high spending (20% of government budget), challenges include a teacher shortage, high dropout rates (around 4 million eligible children dropped out by 2014), and a widening gap between public and expensive private schools. Recent reforms aim to improve global standards and integrate technology, including a hybrid learning program introduced post-pandemic.

## Education in Taiwan
Taiwan's system, shaped by Dutch, Qing, Japanese, and ROC influences, features a 12-year compulsory education policy implemented by 2014. The structure spans kindergarten through high school, with strict discipline, mandatory uniforms, and rigorous national exams governing access to universities. The curriculum emphasizes Mandarin, science, and mathematics, with English becoming compulsory in elementary schools. Higher education is competitive but accessible, with a high university enrollment rate. The system is divided into public universities (funded by the state) and private institutions (often religious or corporate-backed). Vocational education is prominent through junior colleges and technical institutes. Criticism often focuses on the intense pressure on students and the resulting high unemployment among graduates, alongside debates over the role of cram schools.

## Education in Turkey
Turkey's education system was radically secularized in the 1920s following the establishment of the Republic, merging diverse religious and secular channels under the Ministry of National Education. Compulsory education was extended to 12 years in 2012. The system includes general secondary education (high school) and vocational tracks, with high schools often supervised by military officers. Recent political shifts have introduced elements of Islamic law, such as the concept of *jihad* in textbooks and the reopening of Imam Hatip schools, alongside controversies over the teaching of evolution. While technical universities are highly regarded and comparable to US standards, the system faces criticism regarding curriculum bias, particularly concerning the Armenian genocide, and the quality of state-approved textbooks.

## Education in Nigeria
Nigeria's education system is characterized by a 6-3-3-4 structure (primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, and tertiary), though a 9-3-4 system is under review. It is highly decentralized, with significant disparities between federal government schools (elite, merit-based), state-owned schools (often underfunded), and expensive private institutions. Access to higher education is controlled by the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB), with a heavy reliance on the West African Examination Council (WAEC). Challenges include a severe teacher shortage, inadequate infrastructure in rural areas, and high dropout rates. Vocational education is expanding but faces issues with low prestige and outdated curricula.

## Education in France
France operates a highly centralized, secular (*laïque*) system established by Jules Ferry in the 19th century, mandating free, compulsory, and secular education for all children. The curriculum is uniform, with a strong emphasis on French language and history to foster national identity. The school year runs from September to July, with standardized holidays. Higher education is divided between public universities (low tuition for EU citizens) and *grandes écoles* (elite, selective institutions). The system is known for its rigorous preparation classes (*prépas*) and unique grading and credit systems (ECTS). Recent debates focus on the balance between secularism and the rights of religious minorities, particularly regarding conspicuous religious symbols in schools.

## Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland's system features free upper secondary education since 1967 and a strong emphasis on the Leaving Certificate examination. Education is heavily influenced by religious patronage, with the majority of schools being Catholic-run, though reforms have increased multi-denominational options. The curriculum includes a unique Transition Year (TY) program designed to broaden students' horizons. Challenges include a historical preference for Catholic schools in admissions and disparities in access for non-Catholic families. Ireland consistently ranks highly in international assessments (PISA) for reading and mathematics.

## Education in England
England's system is legally compulsory for children aged 5 to 18, with a focus on the National Curriculum and standardized testing (GCSEs and A-levels). Schools are diverse, ranging from state-funded comprehensive schools to selective grammar schools and fee-charging private institutions. Recent reforms have expanded the role of Academies and Free Schools, which operate independently of local authorities. The system is characterized by high academic pressure, particularly in the "cram school" culture, and ongoing debates about the effectiveness of the curriculum in fostering critical thinking versus rote learning.

## Education in Israel
Israel's education system is highly valued and competitive, with a strong focus on science, technology, and higher education as economic drivers. The system is divided into four sectors: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab. While state universities are subsidized, admission is based on the highly selective Bagrut exam and the Psychometric Entrance Test. Significant challenges exist regarding equity, with the Arab sector facing resource shortages and lower enrollment rates. The Haredi sector, while religiously devoted, often lacks access to mainstream education, leading to low workforce participation.

## Education in Ghana
Ghana's system is guided by the Free, Compulsory and Universal Basic Education (FCUBE) policy, aiming for 12 years of basic education. The structure includes kindergarten, primary, junior high, and senior high schools. Challenges persist in teacher training, infrastructure, and the quality of vocational education. Private schools have grown significantly, often outpacing public schools in quality and resources. The government relies heavily on donor funding and is working to address regional disparities and gender gaps in enrollment.

## Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka's education system is divided into primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, and collegiate levels, leading to the GCE A/L exam, which is the gateway to universities. The system is dominated by public schools, though private and international schools offer alternatives. Historically influenced by colonial policies and Buddhist traditions, the system faces criticism for being too competitive and rigid. A major issue is the brain drain of graduates due to limited local job opportunities, prompting government initiatives like compulsory leadership training for undergraduates.

## Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong's system is characterized by high academic achievement and a "spoon-fed" culture, heavily reliant on exam performance. The curriculum is bilingual (Cantonese and English), with a significant portion of schools offering English-medium instruction (EMI). Recent political changes, including the National Security Law, have led to a shift in textbooks toward patriotism and a decline in enrollment at local schools as families opt for international education. The system is highly stratified, with elite private schools offering international curricula (IB, A-Levels) and a competitive public sector.

## Education in Egypt
Egypt's system is centralized and free for basic education, but quality is often low and unevenly distributed. Challenges include high student-to-teacher ratios, reliance on rote memorization, and the proliferation of private tutoring, which many families consider essential for success. Higher education is accessible but overcrowded, with a significant portion of graduates facing unemployment. Recent reforms aim to modernize pedagogy and integrate technology, though structural issues remain.

## Education in Sweden
Sweden operates a comprehensive, secular, and highly egalitarian system with free education from age 6. The curriculum emphasizes core subjects like Swedish, math, and English, with a focus on student choice and development plans rather than strict grading until age 6. Recent political debate centers on the impact of immigration and the introduction of independent (private) schools, which receive public funding but are criticized for potentially increasing inequality and lowering standards.

## Education in Indonesia
Indonesia's system is 12 years of compulsory education, heavily influenced by the state's push for secularism (*Pancasila*) and national unity. Public schools are dominant, but a significant minority of students attend Islamic *pesantren* or *madrasah*. The system faces challenges in funding, teacher distribution across the archipelago, and the quality of education in remote areas. Reforms have aimed to decentralize power and improve curriculum relevance, though rote learning remains common in public classrooms.

## Education in Norway
Norway's system is characterized by high equity, free education, and a strong focus on nature and democracy. It features a unified foundation school system with no official grades until age 16. Recent reforms have increased the role of IT and introduced "camp schools" (leirskole). The system is highly decentralized, with municipalities controlling funding and staffing. Independent schools exist but are rare and heavily grant-aided.

## Education in Armenia
Armenia's system transitioned from a Soviet model to a more diverse, market-oriented system in the 1990s, emphasizing the Armenian language and culture. Primary and secondary education are free and compulsory. Challenges include high dropout rates linked to child labor and absenteeism, as well as funding disparities between public and private schools. The country has seen a growth in foreign language schools, including a new Chinese language school, and is integrating into the Bologna Process for higher education.
