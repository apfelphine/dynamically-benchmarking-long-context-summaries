# Education Systems: Global Overview & Structure

---

## Armenia

Armenia has a literacy rate nearing 100%, with mandatory, free education for children aged 6–16. The system includes preschool, primary (grades 1–4), middle (grades 5–9), and high school (grades 10–12), culminating in standardized exams that determine access to higher education. The country has numerous public and private schools, with quality and access standards set by the government. Higher education is provided by many institutions, including Yerevan State University and international universities, conforming to European standards through initiatives like the Bologna Process and Erasmus+. Despite high enrollment, challenges remain in equity, funding, and growing student absenteeism.

## Egypt

Egypt’s education system is one of the world’s oldest and most extensive. It is highly centralized, with free, compulsory basic education (ages 4–14) encompassing kindergarten, primary, and preparatory stages. Students proceed to general or technical secondary schools and then to higher education, provided by public and private institutions. The system includes specialized tracks such as the Al-Azhar system and agricultural schools. Despite reforms, significant issues persist: quality remains low, with reliance on private tutoring, disparities in access, and high youth unemployment. International schools offer foreign curricula, and recent reforms focus on improving governance, access, and outcomes.

## Ghana

Education in Ghana is divided into basic (preschool to JHS, 12 years), secondary (SHS or TVET), and tertiary sectors. The system was historically shaped by colonial and missionary influences, expanding post-independence. Currently, there is a strong focus on literacy, equitable access, and vocational training, although vocational education faces reputational and structural challenges. Funding comes from both state and private sources. The country boasts high enrollment rates compared to its region, but equity issues persist in higher education participation.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong’s education is structured into three main levels: preschool (mostly private), primary, and secondary (junior and senior). The territory mandates education up to 15–16 years old. There are public, subsidized, private, and international schools, with a competitive, exam-focused culture. Medium of instruction varies: English, Cantonese, and increasing Putonghua. Higher education includes several top-ranked universities, with English as the main language of instruction. Recent years have seen reforms emphasizing national identity, new curricula, and integration with mainland China. The system is marked by significant academic pressure, large class sizes, and tuition costs in private schools.

## Indonesia

Indonesia mandates twelve years of education, divided into primary (SD), junior (SMP), and senior high school (SMA/SMK), with a significant proportion of students attending Islamic schools (pesantren, madrasah). The curriculum includes secular, vocational, and religious streams, with accelerated programs available. Higher education is diversified across universities, institutes, and vocational colleges. The system faces challenges in quality, teacher shortages, and unequal access, especially in remote areas. Education reform has focused on decentralization, diversity of curriculum, and efforts to improve teacher qualifications.

## Iran

Iran’s education spans pre-primary, primary, lower- and upper-secondary, technical/vocational, and post-secondary. The system is highly centralized, with heavy government involvement in curriculum, exams, and teacher training. Post-revolution reforms have emphasized Islamic values, sex segregation, and Islamization of textbooks. Efforts to improve quality have included credit-based courses and vocational tracks. Higher education involves a competitive national entrance exam (Konkur), with top universities in Tehran. Privatization has led to disparities; teacher shortages persist, and large numbers of students drop out before university. Women now outnumber men in higher education.

## Ireland

Education in Ireland is compulsory from ages 6–16, structured around primary, secondary (junior and senior cycle), and third-level education. Primary and secondary education is mainly state-funded, often under religious patronage, with recent reforms promoting multi-denominational schools. Junior cycle ends with the Junior Certificate; senior cycle ends with the Leaving Certificate, both key for university access. Special needs education and support for disadvantaged students are well-established. Irish universities rank among the global top, and the country is a popular destination for international students.

## Israel

Israel’s three-tier system consists of primary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), and high school (10–12), with education compulsory through graduation. The system includes state-secular, state-religious, Haredi, and Arab tracks. Emphasis is placed on academic achievement and mobility, with many students pursuing higher education, especially in science and technology. The matriculation exam (bagrut) is crucial for university admission. Disparities exist between Jewish and Arab sectors, and teacher quality remains a concern. Women perform strongly in education but are underrepresented in some STEM fields.

## Nigeria

Nigeria’s education consists of six years of primary, three years of junior secondary, three years of senior secondary, and various forms of tertiary education, including universities and vocational training. Universal Basic Education aims for compulsory, free education up to junior secondary. Private and international schools are increasingly prevalent. Challenges include low literacy rates (especially among females and in the north), teacher shortages, uneven access, and gender disparities. Vocational and informal education play key roles, and recent government initiatives focus on improving digital and functional literacy.

## Norway

Norway offers a three-part system: elementary (barneskole), lower secondary (ungdomsskole), and upper secondary (videregående), with elementary and lower secondary compulsory. Education prioritizes inclusivity and flexibility (e.g., special education, universal design). Upper secondary integrates general and vocational tracks, with reforms ensuring basic university eligibility for all. Higher education is primarily public and includes universities and colleges, with quality assurance overseen by NOKUT. Grading systems have shifted to align with European standards. Private schools are limited but exist for specific pedagogical or religious purposes.

## Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka’s education system comprises primary (grades 1–5), junior secondary (6–9), senior secondary (10–11), collegiate (12–13), and tertiary. Education is free and compulsory to age 14. The country’s roots are in Buddhist, Hindu, Christian, and Islamic traditions, with colonial reforms shaping the current system. Admission to universities is highly competitive, with only about 16% qualifying. Private and international schools, as well as madrasas and tuition centers, supplement government provision. Vocational and adult education are managed by the Tertiary and Vocational Education Commission. Debates persist over private university accreditation and the rigidity of the system.

---

## Sweden

Sweden mandates nine years of comprehensive education, organized into primary, lower, and upper secondary (gymnasium), with preschool widely available. The system is noted for inclusivity, flexibility, and high rates of enrollment in independent (free) schools. Upper secondary offers vocational and preparatory tracks, leading to a variety of higher education options. Universities operate under the Bologna Process, with standard cycles for bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral studies. PISA and PIRLS results reflect strong but fluctuating performance, influenced by demographic changes and educational reforms. Debate continues over the role of profit in independent schools.

---

## Taiwan

Taiwan mandates 12 years of education—elementary (grades 1–6), junior high (7–9), and senior high (10–12), including skill-based vocational tracks. The system emphasizes exam-based progression and discipline. Higher education includes a wide range of universities and vocational colleges, with direct university entry common for most high school graduates. Public education is dominant, but private schools and cram schools are prevalent. Education reforms focus on curriculum modernization, teacher professional development, and expansion of preschool education. Vocational education is actively promoted as an alternative pathway.

---

## Turkey

Turkey’s system includes pre-primary, eight years of compulsory primary, and secondary (general, vocational, and technical), followed by tertiary education at public or private universities. Major reforms include the introduction of a Latin alphabet, secularization, and recent expansion to 12 years compulsory schooling. The system features high public school enrollment, but private, minority, foreign, and international schools also exist. Religious and nationalist curricula have been the subject of controversy. Higher education is mainly public, with extensive participation in international student exchanges and rapidly growing numbers of universities. Vocational and technical education is a major focus, but educational quality and equity are ongoing concerns.
