# Education in Iran
Education in Iran has ancient roots dating back to pre-Islamic times with establishments like the Academy of Gondishapur. Modern education began during the Safavid era with a blend of Islamic values and scientific progress, expanding through the Qajar era with reforms led by the Educational Committee. The Literacy Corps (1969–1979) significantly improved literacy rates under the Pahlavi government. Post-1979 Islamic Revolution education was Islamized, including gender segregation and curriculum changes. Modern reforms introduced credit-based university systems and vocational training.

Iran’s education system is centralized under the Ministry of Education, covering from pre-primary to university, with compulsory and free elementary education and extensive public and private schools. Higher education includes prestigious universities with a national entrance exam (Konkur). Recent challenges include teacher shortages, rising privatization, mandatory Islamic prayer, and constitutionally restricted protests against educational laws. Efforts at reform focus on quality improvements, vocational training, and hybrid learning. Female enrollment is high, with women comprising more than 70% of university students. Public education still faces dropout issues, and private schooling has become expensive and prestigious.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan’s education system evolved through Dutch, Chinese, and Japanese influences. Public education is compulsory from primary through junior high, moving towards full 12-year compulsory education by 2014. The school year has two semesters, with a broad curriculum including Mandarin, English, native languages, and sciences. Junior and senior high schools prepare students for competitive university entrance exams with differentiated academic and vocational tracks.

Higher education includes public and private universities offering undergraduate to doctoral degrees, following mostly American-style systems. There are strong public institutions such as National Taiwan University and private universities like Fu Jen Catholic University. Vocational education is provided via junior colleges and technical universities. Private education, including pervasive cram schools and English immersion preschools, plays a significant role. International Baccalaureate programs are available, and ongoing reforms seek to promote comprehensive education.

# Education in Turkey
Education in Turkey transitioned from Ottoman-era madrasas to a secular, unified system after the Republic’s foundation. Compulsory education was extended from 5 to 8, then 12 years by 2012 reforms. Primary education spans 8 years; secondary includes diverse general and vocational tracks, with entrance to universities via national exams. Education reforms abolished separate religious schooling, though religion courses exist; however, religious education has been re-established since 1949 and remains part of curricula.

Higher education features both public and private universities, with emphasis on engineering and technical fields, some accredited internationally. Private education includes a variety of schools, including minority, foreign, and international schools. The curriculum emphasizes Turkish nationalism with some controversy over history teaching and curriculum biases, including denial of the Armenian genocide. Vocational training is supported by law with apprenticeships. International programs and student mobility participate in Erasmus programs. Education quality varies widely, and controversies include frequent curriculum changes and the role of religious content.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria’s education system comprises primary (6 years), junior secondary (3 years), senior secondary (3 years), and tertiary levels. Universal Basic Education (UBE) integrates primary and junior secondary schooling. Secondary education includes federal “unity schools,” state-owned schools, and private and religious schools, with varying teacher qualifications and funding challenges.

Examinations such as SSCE and GCE regulate progression. Tertiary education includes federal, state, and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges with significant expansion in recent decades. Vocational education remains underdeveloped with informal apprenticeships dominating. Literacy remains a major challenge with regional disparities, particularly in the North. Female education has improved but faces barriers including early marriage and insecurity. Efforts to enhance literacy and education quality include government and NGO programs. International schools exist, and A-level equivalent programs support university access.

# Education in France
France’s secular, centralized education system mandates schooling from ages 3 to 16, emphasizing a “common culture” through literature and history stemming from the Third Republic. The Ministry of National Education regulates curricula nationwide. Primary education starts with preschool (maternelle), followed by elementary school, then middle school (collège) and high school (lycée), culminating in the baccalauréat or vocational CAP.

Higher education divides between public universities and elite grandes écoles, with preparatory classes (CPGEs) feeding selective institutions. University degrees align with the European Bologna Process (Licence, Master, Doctorat). Public universities are low cost; private schools exist with state supervision. Teacher recruitment is competitive with distinct certifications. Religious instruction is largely excluded due to laïcité, and conspicuous religious symbols are banned in public schools. France hosts numerous international and private institutions. Educational attainment and enrolment are high, with over 2.9 million in higher education.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Irish education includes compulsory schooling from ages 6 to 16 (or completion of three years of second-level education). Primary education includes Junior and Senior Infants through Sixth Class; secondary education is divided into Junior Cycle, optional Transition Year, and Senior Cycle culminating in the Leaving Certificate. Schools are state-funded or voluntary, many under religious patronage with a growing number of multidenominational and Gaelscoileanna (Irish-language schools).

Third-level education is highly regarded internationally. Special education and support for disadvantaged students are provided through targeted programs (e.g., DEIS). The school calendar includes standard holidays and breaks. Homeschooling rights exist within regulation. Education quality ranks highly in international assessments. Schools are mostly mixed sex, with some single-sex institutions. Religious preference has historically affected school admissions but is undergoing reform.

# Education in England
Education is compulsory from ages 5 to 18, with stages divided into Early Years Foundation, Key Stages 1–4, and post-16 education including sixth form and further education colleges. The National Curriculum mandates a broad set of subjects. Schools include state-funded (comprehensive, academies, grammar, faith schools) and private independent schools. Inspections by Ofsted regulate quality. Post-16 education offers A-levels, vocational qualifications, apprenticeships, and T-levels.

Higher education includes universities and colleges offering bachelor’s to doctoral degrees, with tuition fees up to £9,535 per year and financial support available. England hosts prestigious universities and ranks highly in international assessments (PISA). Adult education and lifelong learning opportunities are also provided. National qualifications frameworks align with European standards.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system features primary (grades 1–6), middle (7–9), and high school (10–12) stages with compulsory schooling through grade 12. Schools are divided into state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors, each with distinctive curricula and languages of instruction (Hebrew or Arabic). Haredi schools focus mainly on Torah study with low secular matriculation rates, while Arab schools teach in Arabic emphasizing Arab history and culture. Integrated Jewish-Arab schools exist but are few.

Higher education is highly valued and supported, with matriculation (bagrut) exams and psychometric tests determining university admission. Universities are well-ranked internationally, especially in STEM fields. Education disparities exist between sectors, with Arab schools underfunded and lower matriculation rates. Gender gaps favor female students in matriculation and enrollment. Teacher strikes have occurred over pay and reforms. Textbooks and curricula receive ongoing scrutiny for biases. Education is central to Israel’s socioeconomic development.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education system evolved from informal oral traditions to formal mission and colonial schools, expanding significantly post-independence with compulsory basic education (ages 4–14) and a structure of kindergarten, primary, junior high, and senior high school, followed by tertiary institutions. Basic education is free and compulsory; senior high and tertiary levels have had fluctuating durations and reforms.

Tertiary education includes universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education, with emphasis on practical skills. Literacy and enrollment rates have improved, though challenges remain in female education, infrastructure, and vocational training, which is mostly informal and underdeveloped. Public-private partnerships support education funding and provision. ICT integration and adult literacy programs are growing. Enrollment and gender disparities persist, particularly in rural areas and higher education.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education system is overseen by multiple ministries and has a history of over 2300 years, blending Buddhist, colonial Christian, Hindu, and Islamic educational traditions. Primary and secondary education are free and widely accessible, organized into primary, junior secondary, senior secondary, collegiate, and tertiary stages. The General Certificate of Education (Ordinary and Advanced Level) exams determine progression to university.

There are government, private, semi-government, international, and religious schools, including madrasas and pirivenas (Buddhist monastic colleges). Tertiary education includes 15 state universities and private institutions offering degrees and professional qualifications, with free university education being highly competitive. Vocational education and training are provided alongside literacy and adult education programs. Private tutoring is widespread, and digital learning platforms have emerged recently.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong's education has evolved from traditional village schools and colonial missionary efforts to a modern system supporting bilingual/multicultural education. Primary and secondary education is compulsory from age 6, with curricula delivered in Cantonese or English (EMI schools). The school year has two semesters. Secondary education culminates in the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) or International Baccalaureate programs. Tertiary education includes eight publicly funded universities and several private colleges, with English as the medium of instruction.

International schools offering various curricula are common, though expensive. Recent reforms emphasize patriotism and national security education amid political changes and declining enrollments due to emigration. Adult and special education services are available, and programs support immigrant and non-Cantonese-speaking children. Education quality debates focus on rote learning and high pressure in exams, with gradual adoption of AI tools in higher education.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education dates to antiquity, with ancient institutions like Alexandria’s university and the establishment of al-Azhar in medieval times. Modern education was reformed in the 19th century under Muhammad Ali, adopting dual religious and secular systems. British occupation impacted education negatively by restricting access and emphasizing vocational training.

Post-1952 reforms expanded free public education, but challenges remain in quality, equity, and curriculum. Education is compulsory from age 4 to 14 (basic education) including kindergarten, primary, and preparatory levels, followed by secondary and tertiary education. Higher education includes extensive public and private universities, often centralized with limited autonomy. Private tutoring is widespread due to public system shortcomings, and teacher qualifications and infrastructure remain concerns. COVID-19 accelerated distance learning adoption. Al-Azhar religious education operates alongside the public system, primarily for Muslims.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden’s education system includes compulsory comprehensive schooling (grundskola) for ages 6–16, followed by optional three-year upper secondary education (gymnasieskola) with preparation for university. Preschool is widely available and subsidized. Schools offer a broad curriculum covering core subjects and multiple languages. National tests and diverse grading systems assess student progress.

Higher education follows the European Bologna model with bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees, administered by universities and university colleges. Independent (friskolor) or free schools are publicly funded but privately run, offering alternative pedagogies; they have sparked debate over profit-making and educational equality. Sweden's international assessment scores have fluctuated, with immigrant integration noted as a factor. Student aid supports access, and anti-discrimination policies exist. Vocational education is separate from academic higher education.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education has roots in Islamic boarding schools (pesantren) and colonial Dutch schools differentiated by ethnicity and language. The system evolved post-independence to offer 12 years of formal education: preschool, primary (6 years), junior high (3 years), and senior high or vocational school (3 years). Both secular public schools and religious schools under the Ministry of Religious Affairs coexist.

Curricula emphasize national identity and Pancasila principles, though pedagogy remains largely rote-based. Islamic education includes pesantren, madrasah, and Islamic schools with varying secular and religious content. Higher education comprises universities, institutes, academies, and polytechnics, aligned with the Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI).

Challenges include teacher shortages, unequal access, funding, and quality disparities, especially in remote areas. Enrollment rates are high for primary but lower for secondary and tertiary levels. The government promotes vocational training and curriculum reforms.

# Education in Norway
Norway’s education system includes compulsory elementary (barneskole, ages 6–12) and lower secondary (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16) school, followed by optional upper secondary school (videregående skole, ages 16–19). Education is largely public, with growing independent schools. Curricula cover broad subjects including languages, sciences, and social studies, with national tests in grades 3, 6, and 9.

Upper secondary offers general and vocational studies with apprenticeships. Teacher qualifications vary by level. Higher education includes universities, university colleges, and private institutions, with degrees following the Bologna system. Grading scales and admissions tests (SweSAT) are used. Student financial aid and anti-discrimination policies support access. History includes extensive reforms and expansion. Special education emphasizes inclusive schooling.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia’s education system has near-universal literacy since Soviet times, transitioning post-independence to Armenian-language instruction and inclusion in the European Higher Education Area (Bologna Process). Preschool begins around age two, followed by 12 years of compulsory free education divided into primary, middle, and high school. High school offers general and specialized tracks leading to university or vocational studies.

Public schools dominate, funded and regulated by the government, following a national curriculum; private schools operate under licensing with tuition fees contributing to quality variations. Armenia has multiple higher education institutions including Yerevan State University and specialized academies, integrating international cooperation and EU programs (Erasmus+).

Recent challenges include student absenteeism linked to child labor and efforts to provide 21st-century skills through technology centers like Tumo. Education reforms focus on quality assurance, inclusivity, and alignment with European standards.
