## Comparative Overview of Global Education Systems

Education systems in Norway, Sweden, Armenia, Hong Kong, Indonesia, Ghana, Egypt, England, Sri Lanka, France, Israel, Turkey, Ireland, Iran, Nigeria, and Taiwan reflect distinct histories, cultures, and political influences. Key reform themes across these countries include advancing inclusivity, modernization, technological integration, equity, curriculum relevance, and responsiveness to issues like nationalism, secularism, religious and minority inclusion, and evolving social identities.

---

### Key National Approaches

**Norway & Sweden:**  
Emphasize inclusivity, language skills, and technology through comprehensive compulsory schooling with subsequent academic and vocational pathways. Sweden’s system integrates marginalized groups and provides free higher education but contends with privatization and integration issues.

**Armenia:**  
Compulsory schooling builds national identity and encourages multilingualism, with Bologna-aligned higher education focused on attendance and dropout reduction.

**Hong Kong:**  
Features competitive secondary and tertiary education, ongoing reforms in workload, inclusivity, technology, and national security education.

**Indonesia & Ghana:**  
Compulsory education blends academic and religious tracks, highlighting nationalism and equity challenges. Ghana sees gender parity improvements but wrestles with TVET and university gaps.

**Egypt:**  
Mixes public and private institutions and historical traditions. Reform targets include teacher quality, employability, and digital adoption with persistent disparities.

**England & Sri Lanka:**  
Compulsory education leads to diverse secondary and tertiary options. Equity, funding, inspections, and special needs are priorities, yet both confront access and employability challenges.

**France:**  
Centralized, secular approach with rigorous academics and varied higher education, emphasizing research and university autonomy.

**Israel:**  
Streams for state-secular, religious, Arab, and Haredi populations address disparities. Higher education is subsidized, and military service is mandatory.

**Turkey:**  
Follows a 4+4+4 structure with general, vocational, and religious tracks. Reform promotes diversity, minority rights, secularism, and regional languages; higher education expands in agriculture, health, and tech.

**Ireland:**  
Church-run primaries, growing pluralism, and broad curriculum reforms support language and special needs, with enhanced infrastructure and regulated homeschooling.

**Iran:**  
Centralized control with strong investment, Islamic practice, hybrid learning, and privatization. High literacy, particularly among women; selective tracks for gifted students. Persian is the primary language, with delayed foreign language instruction.

**Nigeria:**  
The "9-3-4" structure and exams regulate progression; vocational and informal education struggles with funding and infrastructure. Literacy and AI initiatives are expanding, but research practices need improvement.

### Taiwan’s Education System

Taiwan offers twelve years of compulsory education split into elementary, junior, and senior high school, with regular (university-focused) and vocational pathways shaped by national exams and recommendations. The system stresses discipline, Mandarin, and English taught from grade 3, with military education present in high schools. The Ministry of Education regulates quality and teacher advancement via regional centers and a nationwide digital platform tracking K–12 teacher training.

Taiwan’s higher education aligns with the US model and boasts public and private universities, many historical or research-oriented, with specialized programs in engineering, medicine, law, and teaching. Tertiary studies include junior colleges, undergraduate, and graduate degrees, with vocational colleges offering from associate to doctoral tracks. High university entrance has created a graduate oversupply.

Private education includes widespread cram schools for test preparation, common public and private kindergartens, and popular accelerated or English immersion programs, sometimes sparking debate over language impacts. The “3+3 High School system” enables streamlined progression in private institutions.

Special education laws provide for students with disabilities and giftedness through multiple service modalities. Nursing programs have specific licensure pathways. Taiwan is known for Chinese language learning, attracting international students, including mainland Chinese (with 40,000 studying in Taiwan as of 2015, mainly short-term).

### Common Challenges and Trends

Across all systems, nations strive to improve access, equity, curriculum relevance, technology adoption, teacher quality, and infrastructure. Persistent issues include gaps in educational opportunities, regional and socioeconomic disparities, religious and minority inclusion, responses to national identity and secular mandates, and balancing tradition with modernization.
