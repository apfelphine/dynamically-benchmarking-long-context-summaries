# Global Education Systems: Regional Overview

**Asia** presents a diverse landscape of educational models, from Hong Kong's prestigious bilingual system to Taiwan's high-pressure, exam-centric culture. While Hong Kong expanded tertiary education to four years, it faces severe enrollment declines and brain drain following the 2019 social unrest. Conversely, Taiwan relies on a rigorous academic framework driven by the National University Entrance Exam, supporting a vast industry of cram schools and specialized tracks, though debates persist regarding English immersion's impact on native language development. Indonesia, Armenia, and Sri Lanka also struggle with systemic issues including funding shortages, teacher deficits, and high dropout rates linked to socioeconomic disparities or child labor.

In the broader Asian context, regional nuances highlight further complexities. Indonesia and Armenia grapple with systemic issues such as funding shortages and high dropout rates. Meanwhile, Turkey has implemented a standardized "4+4+4" system but faces controversies regarding nationalist indoctrination and curriculum quality. Iran maintains a centralized, female-centric system with high enrollment rates, yet it struggles with teacher shortages, ideological restrictions in social sciences, and high dropout rates.

**Europe** operates within a unified Bologna Process framework, though national models vary significantly. Norway and Sweden share a foundational compulsory structure but differ in grading scales and vocational integration. France relies on a centralized, secular model distinguishing between mass public universities and selective *grandes écoles*, while England utilizes a diverse state-funded framework with robust welfare measures and mandatory inspections to sustain global rankings. Ireland blends English and Irish-language instruction with significant infrastructure investments and legal protections for disadvantaged students, whereas the UK utilizes a diverse state-funded framework with robust welfare measures.

**Africa and the Middle East** share common challenges despite varying enrollment rates, including fragmented administrative structures and systemic inequalities driven by geography and socioeconomic status. Ghana, though offering a free 12-year program, suffers from a critically underperforming Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) sector and a literacy gap. Similarly, Nigeria operates under the Universal Basic Education (UBE) policy, mandating free schooling, but faces a stark disparity between well-resourced federal institutions and underfunded state schools, often forcing families to rely on expensive private options.

Across these regions, digital learning platforms are increasingly utilized in urban areas to bridge gaps, yet high public tuition and limited infrastructure continue to hinder equitable outcomes. A lucrative private tutoring industry in several nations creates a disconnect between policy and practice, exacerbating the divide between those with access to premium education and those without.
