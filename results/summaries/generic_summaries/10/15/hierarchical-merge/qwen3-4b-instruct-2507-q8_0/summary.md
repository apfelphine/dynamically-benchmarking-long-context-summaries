# **Merged Summary: Comparative Overview of Education Systems in Norway, Sweden, Armenia, Hong Kong, Indonesia, Ghana, Egypt, England, France, Israel, Turkey, Ireland, Iran, Nigeria, and Taiwan**

## **Nordic and Post-Soviet Systems: Norway, Sweden, and Armenia**

Norway, Sweden, and Armenia exemplify equity-focused, publicly funded education systems rooted in inclusive access and student well-being. Norway’s *folkeskole* (ages 6–16) emphasizes play-based learning in early years and transitions to a unified grading system (1–6), with fully integrated academic and vocational pathways. A landmark 1994 reform and the 2006 *Kunnskapsløftet* introduced flexible models like the "2+2" apprenticeship, achieving 73% graduation rates and 93% public school enrollment. There is no formal distinction between academic and vocational tracks, and special education is fully integrated.  

Sweden operates a nine-year *grundskola* (ages 6–16) with a standardized curriculum since 1919, unified in 1962. A grading system (A–E) was introduced in 1994, with grades added from grade 6 in 2011. National assessments occur in grades 3, 6, and 9, though test leaks remain a concern. Upper secondary *gymnasieskola* (three years) offers 18 programs—two-thirds academic (granting university eligibility), one-third vocational. Core subjects—Swedish, English, and math—are mandatory. Publicly funded *friskolor* offer diverse profiles. Despite strong early childhood education and PISA improvements, concerns persist over declining academic standards and grading bias.  

Armenia has achieved 100% literacy since 1960 and provides 12 years of free, compulsory secondary education, divided into primary, middle, and high school with general or specialized tracks. Public schools (97%) follow a national curriculum; private institutions offer specialized programs but face access barriers. Higher education includes 59 universities (26 public, 33 private), aligned with the Bologna Process and recognized by ENQA and EQAR. Challenges include rising absenteeism, dropout rates, and declining quality linked to child labor and systemic issues. After-school centers like Tumo and COAF SMART promote 21st-century skills, though high school completion remains below OECD benchmarks.

## **Hong Kong: Bilingual, Exam-Centric, and Politically Influenced**

Hong Kong’s education system is structured in three stages: Kindergarten (3 years), Primary (6 years, compulsory), and Secondary (3 years, selective), culminating in the **Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE)**. Most schools are Chinese-medium, with only 114 English Medium Instruction (EMI) schools—often elite due to higher university admission rates. Since 1997, the curriculum shifted toward Chinese-medium education, and since 2020, it has excluded sensitive topics, introduced *National Security Education Day*, and replaced *Liberal Studies* with *Citizenship and Social Development*, including mandatory field trips to mainland China.  

The system includes government, aided, private, and international schools, supported by policies like the Direct Subsidy Scheme and the English Schools Foundation. International schools (175 under ISC) offer UK qualifications (GCSEs, A-levels) and the IBDP. University admissions are managed through **JUPAS**, with English as the primary medium. Tertiary education expanded to four-year undergraduate programs in 2012. Eight universities (e.g., HKU, CUHK, HKUST) are globally ranked in the top 100. Vocational and further education are regulated by the Ministry of Education and the Vocational Training Council (VTC), with standards like the "Apprentice Ordinance" ensuring quality. Despite reforms promoting group work and open-ended tasks, the system remains exam-centric, emphasizing rote learning and strict discipline. Special education is gradually integrating, though students with severe intellectual disabilities remain excluded.

## **Large and Diverse Systems: Indonesia, Ghana, Egypt, and Turkey**

**Indonesia** has a 12-year compulsory education system (primary and secondary), structured in two semesters with a 5-day school week. Public and religious schools coexist, with teaching largely rote-based and teacher-centered. The curriculum emphasizes **Pancasila**—belief in one God, humanitarianism, unity, democracy, and social justice—though its influence has waned since 1998. Religious education is delivered through *pesantren*, *madrasah*, and *sekolah islam*. Vocational tracks (*SMK*) cover technology, health, arts, business, and agriculture. Higher education is aligned with the **Indonesian National Qualification Framework (KKNI)**. With over 50 million students and 3 million teachers, Indonesia is the 4th largest education system globally. Enrollment and survival rates are high (93.5% primary, 78.84% middle, 60.67% high school), though tertiary participation remains low (36.31%). Challenges include teacher shortages, lack of textbooks, and urban-rural inequities. Teacher training now requires senior high school diplomas for primary teachers and university degrees for higher grades.

**Ghana** provides free, compulsory 12-year basic education (ages 4–15), culminating in the **Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE)**. Secondary education includes three-year Senior High School (SHS), leading to the **West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE)**, and vocational training through **TVET** institutions. Tertiary education includes 55 institutions—6 public, 49 private universities, and 10 polytechnics—with bachelor’s degrees lasting four years. Admission to Colleges of Education requires a fee and online application, with preferences given to certain applicants. The system is managed by the **Ministry of Education**, supported by the **Ghana Education Service (GES)**, **National Testing Agency (NTC)**, and **National Council for Curriculum and Assessment (NCCA)**. A public-private partnership model prevails: the government funds salaries, textbooks, and infrastructure, while private institutions, parents, and communities contribute through fees and maintenance. Private schools are prevalent, especially in rural areas like Ga district (74.7% private). TVET faces challenges such as low completion rates, poor instructor quality, and negative public perception. Reforms by the **Council for TVET (COTVET)**—including the **National Youth Fund**, qualifications framework, and **National Apprenticeship Programme**—aim to improve access and employability. Literacy rose from 54% (1997) to 79.04% (2018), with gender disparities persisting. Education receives **23–25%** of the national budget, though donor support has declined from 8.5% to 2.5% between 2005 and 2012.

**Egypt** has deep historical roots, from ancient institutions like *Bar Ankh* and Alexandria to medieval centers such as al-Azhar and Dar al-Ilm. Modernization began under Muhammad Ali with a dual system combining religious *mansouri* schools and secular madrasas. Despite reforms like the Higher Education Enhancement Project (HEEP) and free, compulsory public education under Nasser, quality remains low—Egypt ranks 49th in reading and 34th in math internationally. The system is highly centralized, with two secondary tracks (general and technical), vocational training supported by Applied Technology Schools and the Industrial Training Council, and 54 universities (27 public, 27 private). Challenges include high student-to-teacher ratios, outdated pedagogy, reliance on rote learning, declining per-student spending due to rapid enrollment growth, and persistent youth unemployment among graduates (36.1% in 2019). The pandemic accelerated distance learning, which students found accessible and effective. Religious education is structured through Al-Azhar, with a six-year primary, three-year preparatory, and three-year secondary stage leading to automatic admission to Al-Azhar University. Agricultural education is supported by 133 secondary schools under the Ministry of Agriculture. Private tutoring is widespread (58% of families), teacher qualifications are often low, and equity across wealth groups remains a persistent issue.

**Turkey** features a standardized, secular system established through major reforms since 1923, promoted by the Ministry of National Education. Primary education (8 years, ages 6–14) is compulsory and free, with core subjects including Turkish, math, science, and foreign languages. Social studies are restructured at grade 4, and Turkish History and Kemalism are introduced at grade 8. Secondary education (3+ years, ages 15–17) offers general and vocational tracks, with students choosing academic paths (e.g., Turkish-Math, Science, Social Sciences, Foreign Languages) in 11th grade. All students receive a standardized diploma, eliminating track-based weighting for university admissions. In 2012, the "4+4+4" system (four years of elementary, four of middle, four of secondary) was introduced to enhance equity and workforce readiness. The International Baccalaureate is offered in 53 schools since 1994. In 2017, the AKP introduced a revised curriculum that removed evolution and included jihad in Islamic studies, sparking controversy over ideological influence and curriculum neutrality. Despite progress in standardization and access, debates over educational values, autonomy, and ideological content remain central. Public universities dominate due to affordability and accessibility; Turkish technical universities are globally recognized, especially in IT, health, and defense. International enrollment has surged due to low tuition and government scholarships for up to 500,000 foreign students. However, the curriculum promotes state nationalism: students recite daily oaths, study Atatürk’s legacy, and are required to write essays denying the Armenian genocide. State textbooks omit Armenians from Ottoman history, portray deportations as justified, and mandate refutations of genocide. Despite official bans, private textbooks are widely used due to poor state materials. Minority language education (e.g., Hebrew, French) is declining, even though constitutionally protected under the Treaty of Lausanne. Religious education is banned in public schools, and minority schools are gradually being assimilated into mainstream curricula.

## **Western and Global Systems: England, France, Ireland, and Israel**

**England** operates a predominantly state-funded system, with 93% of children aged 3–18 attending free state schools. Key types include academies, community schools, free schools, foundation schools, UTCs, and maths schools. All state schools follow the National Curriculum and undergo Ofsted inspections. Private schools (7% of pupils) are fee-charging, not bound by the curriculum, and inspected by ISI or Ofsted. Early years education follows the Early Years Foundation Stage, and all state-funded schools offer free school meals to low-income students. At Key Stage 4, students study arts, design and technology, humanities, business, and modern languages, along with mandatory Relationship and Sex Education and PSHE. Post-16 options include A-levels, BTECs, T Levels (combining classroom learning with industry placements), and vocational qualifications. Higher education is delivered by universities, colleges, and private institutions, with most bachelor’s degrees being three-year honours programs. Tuition fees are capped at £9,535 annually (2024–25), with state funding for disadvantaged students. International students—especially in London—are well-represented. Adult education offers access courses, distance learning, and vocational training, supported by the RQF and FHEQ frameworks. England ranks 13th globally in literacy, math, and science (average score: 503.7), with primary students placing 4th in reading. The expansion of the academy system—particularly in disadvantaged areas—has significantly improved school quality, with 88% of schools now rated as good or outstanding (up from 68% in 2010). Since 2018, schools have been funded through a national formula, receiving increased budgets: a 6% rise in 2020–21, growing to £7.1 billion by 2022–23, including £780 million specifically for Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND).

**France** operates a well-structured, secular, and mandatory public education system from age 6 to 18, rooted in 19th-century reforms by Jules Ferry. The Ministry of National Education and Youth oversees a standardized curriculum across regions, emphasizing French literature and history to reinforce national identity. Schooling is mandatory (with limited exceptions), running from September to July and including major holidays throughout the year. The system progresses from preschool to elementary, middle, and high school (lycée), where students prepare for the baccalauréat or vocational certifications like CAP. Private schools follow the national curriculum, while religious instruction is permitted in certain cases. Public schools uphold *laïcité*, banning conspicuous religious symbols, though religious instruction is allowed in Alsace and Moselle. Higher education is diverse, featuring public universities aligned with the Bologna Process (Licence, Master, Doctorat) using the ECTS credit system. Vocational training is offered through IUTs, while elite *grandes écoles*—selective, discipline-specific institutions—produce many of France’s top scientists and executives. Preparatory classes (CPGEs), especially in scientific and business tracks, prepare students for *grandes écoles*, with elite institutions like Louis-Le-Grand and Henri-IV known for rigorous standards and oral exams. Teacher recruitment involves pathways through INSPE/ESPE, with CAPES and prestigious Agrégation exams determining status and salaries. University teaching is led by research-focused *enseignants-chercheurs* and teaching-only staff. Tuition is low for EU/EEA/Swiss/Quebec students (€170–380/year), with scholarships available; elite private institutions, particularly business schools, may charge up to €40,000 annually. The system faces demographic challenges due to an aging population and growing student numbers.

**Ireland** has compulsory education from age 6 to 16 or completion of three years of second-level education. The system aligns with the European Higher Education Area (EHEA) and the National Framework of Qualifications (NFQ), ensuring coherence. Primary education spans eight years (ages 4–12), with 88.3% of schools being Catholic, though religious diversity is growing. Reforms have reduced religious bias in admissions and phased out Catholic-specific practices in some secondary schools. The **Junior Cycle** (ages 12–14) culminates in the Junior Certificate, including core subjects like English, Irish, and Maths, with optional subjects such as French and History. An optional **Transition Year** (ages 15–16) offers experiential learning, though its academic integration remains debated. The **Senior Cycle** (ages 15–19) leads to the Leaving Certificate, with exams in core and optional subjects. Over 90% of students complete the Leaving Certificate, with only 12.3% leaving after the Junior Cycle—below the EU average. School types include voluntary (57%), community (28%), comprehensive (15%), and private ("grind schools"), with Gaelcholáistí (Irish-medium schools) at 3%. Ireland ranks second in reading and 11th in maths (PISA 2022), and its tertiary education is among the world’s top 20 (US News 2022). Support systems such as the DEIS programme (now covering 1,200 schools) and a €200 million EIB investment (2023) improve access, facilities, and teacher conditions. Special needs are supported through SNA (Special Needs Assistant) services.

**Israel** features a four-track system—state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab—each with unique curricula and cultural orientations. Governed by the Israeli Pupils’ Rights Law (2000), the system prohibits sectarian discrimination and reflects a strong societal value on education as a pathway to socioeconomic mobility, rooted in Jewish traditions of scholarship. High educational attainment—particularly in STEM—is central to Israel’s high-tech economy, with 46% of Israeli Jews holding post-secondary degrees and strong performance in national matriculation exams (Bagrut). The secondary system is robust in urban areas, and students often serve mandatory military service, including university study under the *atuda* program. Despite strong tertiary outcomes, performance in international assessments like PISA is modest, and significant disparities persist—especially in the Arab education sector, where underfunding, higher dropout rates, and unequal resources limit access and outcomes. Structural inequalities remain, particularly in Haredi participation in mainstream education and Arab students’ access to quality instruction. Reform efforts include affirmative actions, improved teaching quality, and a five-year higher education plan. Nonprofit initiatives such as Aluma’s Ruad program support Arab, Druze, and Circassian students in higher education. Gender disparities persist: male students have higher high school passing rates and dropout rates, while women dominate university enrollment and advanced degrees. Challenges include frequent teacher strikes, low wages, and biased textbooks that have been criticized for promoting negative stereotypes of Arab communities.

## **Key Contrasts and Comparative Insights**

Across all systems, education reflects a complex interplay of historical legacies, national values, socio-political contexts, and economic priorities. Nordic and post-Soviet systems (Norway, Sweden, Armenia) prioritize equity, integration, and student well-being, with strong public investment and inclusive pathways. Hong Kong stands out for its academic rigor, international integration, and high-quality tertiary outcomes, though it faces challenges in political neutrality, access equity, and academic freedom. Indonesia and Ghana emphasize national unity and public-private collaboration, but grapple with teacher quality, regional disparities, and underdeveloped vocational education. Egypt, despite its historical depth, suffers from low quality, outdated pedagogy, and persistent youth unemployment. England excels in literacy, numeracy, and school quality through the academy model and increased funding, though socioeconomic and regional inequalities persist. France offers a standardized, secular system with strong national identity and elite institutions, though it faces demographic and funding challenges. Israel demonstrates high educational attainment in STEM, particularly among Jewish populations, but struggles with regional and religious disparities. Turkey has made progress in standardization and access, but its curriculum remains politically contentious, raising concerns about ideological influence and autonomy.  

Ireland promotes multilingualism, inclusive access, and constitutional protections, with strong academic performance and equitable support systems. Iran’s system is centralized and religiously grounded, with significant gender progress in higher education despite rigid curricular and social policies. Nigeria prioritizes universal access through free basic education but faces systemic challenges in quality, teacher training, and infrastructure. Taiwan features a highly structured, disciplined system with near-universal university access, strong vocational pathways, and a focus on high-tech and engineering fields, supported by robust teacher development and digital platforms.  

Ultimately, while all systems show a shift toward modernization—through curriculum reform, technology integration, or equity-focused policies—core challenges remain: ensuring equitable access, improving teacher quality, modernizing pedagogy, and addressing structural inequalities. The success of each system is deeply tied to its ability to balance national identity, social equity, and economic development in a rapidly changing global landscape.
