### **Education in Norway**

#### **History**
Norway’s education system dates back to 2000 B.C., with early schools established in Trondheim, Oslo, Bergen, and Hamar. After the Reformation (1537), Latin schools replaced cathedral schools, and compulsory reading training began in 1736. The *folkeskole* (primary school) became mandatory in 1889 after a 12-year period, later extended to nine years in 1969. The *grunnskole* (foundation school) replaced the *folkeskole* in the 1970s–80s. Private schooling became available under the Independent Schools Act of 2003, though few exist compared to public schools.

#### **Education Today**
The Norwegian school system is divided into three stages:
- **Elementary School (barneskole, ages 6–12)**
- **Lower Secondary School (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16)**
- **Upper Secondary School (videregående skole, ages 16–19)**

Compulsory education includes *barneskole* and *ungdomsskole*, with *leirskole* (camp school) stays authorized for primary and secondary education. Primary school focuses on foundational skills, while lower secondary school introduces electives and prepares students for upper secondary exams. Upper secondary education is primarily public, with vocational and general study paths.

#### **Educators**
Educators in Norway are categorized by degree:
- **Preschool Teachers (førskolelærer/barnehagelærer)**: Bachelor’s degree.
- **Adjunct Teachers (adjunkt)**: Bachelor’s degree in a subject + one-year pedagogy course.
- **Lecturers (lektor)**: Master’s degree + pedagogy course.

#### **Higher Education**
Higher education in Norway includes universities, university colleges, and private schools. Admission to most programs requires a general university admissions certificate (*generell studiekompetanse*), achieved through upper secondary studies or alternative pathways. The system is divided into bachelor’s, master’s, and PhD levels. Public institutions are state-funded, while private schools are less common but offer specialized programs.

#### **Special Education**
Norway’s first large-scale special education institution for people with intellectual disabilities was founded in 1898. Since the 1970s, the government has mandated inclusive education, with special educators and psychological services supporting students with diverse needs. Institutions must create inclusive learning environments.

#### **Grading and Examinations**
Norway uses a unique grading system:
- **Primary/Secondary**: Grades 1–6, with no plus/minus variations.
- **Upper Secondary**: Grades 1–6, with final diplomas based on teacher-assigned marks and exam grades.
- **Higher Education**: Grades 1.0–6.0 (1.0 being the lowest, 4.0+ passing).

#### **Academic Schedule**
The school year runs in two semesters, with long breaks including summer (June–August), Christmas (December–January), and fall/winter breaks (October and February). National holidays include Easter, May Day, Constitution Day, and Ascension Day.

### **Education in Sweden**

#### **History**
Swedish education expanded rapidly in the 1950s, with the *folkskola* (comprehensive school) introduced in 1962. The system now includes a nine-year *grundskola*, with reforms in the 1980s and 2000s emphasizing theoretical subjects. The *gymnasieskola* (upper secondary) offers 18 national programs, including preparatory and vocational tracks.

#### **Education Today**
Swedish education is divided into:
- **Preschool (förskola)**: Free for children aged 1–5, with optional preschool education (*förskoleklass*) for ages 6–7.
- **Comprehensive School (*grundskola*)**: Nine years, mandatory from ages 6–15, covering 16 compulsory subjects.
- **Upper Secondary School (*gymnasieskola*)**: Three years, with 18 national programs, including vocational and preparatory tracks.

#### **Private Education**
Sweden’s *friskolor* (independent schools) are funded by public money but operate independently. They are allowed to charge fees and are not required to follow the national curriculum strictly. The system has sparked debates over educational standards and inequality.

#### **Higher Education**
Sweden’s higher education system follows the Bologna Process, with degrees divided into bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral levels. Universities and university colleges offer a wide range of programs, including professional degrees in fields like engineering and medicine. The system is funded by the state, with tuition fees for international students.

#### **International Education**
Sweden has 900+ independent schools (*friskolor*), offering curricula from various countries. The system is seen as a model for public-private partnerships in education.

#### **Education Challenges**
Sweden’s education system has faced criticism for declining test scores, particularly in reading and mathematics. The government has implemented reforms to improve outcomes, including active-learning pedagogies and technology integration.

### **Education in Armenia**

#### **History**
Armenia’s education system traces back to ancient times, with the first known school (*University of Moliyili*) established in the 1700s. After the Soviet era, Armenia transitioned to a centralized education system, emphasizing Armenian history and culture. The government introduced English language programs in 2018 and Chinese language schools in 2018.

#### **Education Today**
Armenia’s education system spans:
- **Preschool**: Ages 2–6, with 808 community, 10 public, and 50 private institutions.
- **Primary/Secondary Education**: Compulsory for 12 years, divided into primary (grades 1–4), middle (grades 5–9), and high school (grades 10–12). Students can choose between general and specialized secondary education.
- **Vocational Education**: Includes technical, artistic, and vocational studies.

#### **Higher Education**
Armenia has 26 state universities and 33 licensed private higher education institutions. The country has adopted the Bologna Process and is a member of the European Higher Education Area. The government has invested in quality assurance and international cooperation.

#### **Special Education**
Armenia has a growing focus on special education, with institutions like the *COAF SMART Educational Center* providing non-formal education for rural communities. The system aims to integrate students with disabilities into mainstream education.

#### **Challenges**
Armenia faces challenges such as student absenteeism, limited vocational training, and gender disparities in higher education. The government has implemented reforms to address these issues, including scholarship programs and partnerships with international organizations.

### **Education in Hong Kong**

#### **History**
Hong Kong’s education system began with missionary schools in the 1840s, transitioning to a bilingual curriculum in the 19th century. The *Queen’s College* (1862) was the first government-funded school. The system has evolved with reforms in the 1990s, including the introduction of the *Target Oriented Curriculum (TOC)* and the *Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE)*.

#### **Education Today**
Hong Kong’s education system is divided into:
- **Pre-primary Education**: Not free, with subsidies available for eligible families.
- **Primary and Secondary Education**: Compulsory from ages 6–18, with a focus on bilingual education (English and Chinese). Schools are divided into *English Medium Instruction (EMI)* and *Chinese Medium Instruction (CMI)* schools.
- **International Education**: Over 175 international schools, offering curricula from various countries.

#### **Higher Education**
Hong Kong’s higher education system is divided into:
- **UGC-funded Universities**: Eight public universities, including the University of Hong Kong (HKU), Chinese University of Hong Kong (CUHK), and Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (HKUST).
- **Private Institutions**: Includes self-financing institutions and approved post-secondary colleges.

#### **Challenges**
Hong Kong’s education system has faced challenges such as the impact of the national security law on enrollment, declining academic standards, and the rise of private tutoring. The government has implemented reforms to address these issues, including the introduction of the *Citizenship and Social Development* subject and the use of artificial intelligence in education.

### **Education in Ghana**

#### **History**
Ghana’s education system dates back to the 1700s, with the *University of Moliyili* being one of the earliest learning centers. Missionary schools were established in the 19th century, and Ghana gained independence in 1957, leading to the introduction of free and compulsory primary education. The system has undergone several reforms, including the *Free, Compulsory and Universal Basic Education (FCUBE)* initiative.

#### **Education Today**
Ghana’s education system is divided into:
- **Basic Education**: Ages 4–14, covering kindergarten, primary, and junior high school. The curriculum includes subjects like English, mathematics, and social studies.
- **Secondary Education**: Includes academic and vocational tracks, leading to the *West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE)*.
- **Tertiary Education**: Includes universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education. The system is highly competitive, with admission based on WASSCE results.

#### **Private Education**
Ghana has a growing private education sector, with private primary and junior high schools outnumbering public schools. Private involvement in education is seen as a solution to funding gaps and improving educational quality.

#### **Challenges**
Ghana’s education system faces challenges such as funding shortages, teacher shortages, and gender disparities. The government has implemented reforms to address these issues, including the *National Council for Curriculum and Assessment (NCCA)* and the *Council for Technical and Vocational Education and Training (COTVET)*.

### **Education in England**

#### **History**
England’s education system dates back to the Middle Ages, with schools established for Latin education. The *Education Act 1870* introduced state-funded schools, and the *Education Act 1902* allowed local authorities to create secondary schools. The system has evolved with reforms in the 20th century, including the introduction of free upper second-level education in 1967.

#### **Education Today**
England’s education system is divided into:
- **Early Years**: Ages 3–5, covering nursery and reception classes.
- **Primary School**: Ages 5–11, covering key stages 1 and 2.
- **Secondary School**: Ages 11–16, covering key stages 3 and 4. Students take the *General Certificate of Secondary Education (GCSE)* exams.
- **Post-16 Education**: Ages 16–18, covering sixth form and further education colleges.

#### **Types of Schools**
England has various types of schools, including:
- **State-funded schools**: Community schools, academies, free schools, foundation schools, and voluntary schools.
- **Private schools**: Fee-charging schools, including public schools and prep schools.
- **Alternative education**: Includes homeschooling and elective home education.

#### **Higher Education**
England’s higher education system includes universities, university colleges, and private colleges. Undergraduate education typically lasts three years, with options for integrated master’s degrees. Tuition fees have increased since 2012, with fees capped at £9,535 per year for English students.

#### **Challenges**
England’s education system faces challenges such as funding shortages, teacher shortages, and the impact of COVID-19 on learning. The government has implemented reforms to address these issues, including the introduction of the *National Curriculum* and the *Office for Standards in Education (Ofsted)*.

The government has implemented reforms to address these issues, including the introduction of the *National Curriculum* and the *Office for Standards in Education (OFSEEC)*.
