# Education in Norway
Norwegian education has ancient roots, evolving from cathedral schools in the 12th century to compulsory folkeskole (primary school) by the late 19th century. Today, education consists of compulsory grunnskole (elementary and lower secondary, ages 6–16) and upper secondary school (ages 16–19). Private schools are rare and strictly regulated. The system emphasizes inclusive education and quality assurance through agencies like NOKUT. Higher education includes universities and colleges offering bachelor’s to doctoral degrees. Vocational education blends school and apprenticeships. Grading varies by level, with national exams employed selectively. The academic year spans August to June with breaks for Christmas, summer, and other holidays.

# Education in Sweden
Sweden’s education system includes nine years of compulsory comprehensive school covering diverse subjects, followed by a strong upper secondary level (gymnasieskola) with preparatory or vocational tracks. Preschool is widely accessible and subsidized. Grading starts officially in grade 6 with national tests at strategic stages. The system offers extensive higher education aligned with the Bologna Process, comprising universities and university colleges, with various degree levels including professional programs. Independent schools, publicly funded since the 1990s, comprise about 10% of students and have prompted debate on equity and standards. Sweden performs near the OECD average on international assessments, with immigrant influx impacting outcomes.

# Education in Armenia
Armenia provides free, compulsory K-12 education split into preschool, primary (grades 1-4), middle (5-9), and high school (10-12) including general and specialized streams. Entrance exams determine higher education access. Public schools predominate, with private schools operating under ministry licensing and quality standards. The higher education sector includes state and private universities, many aligned with the Bologna Process, participating in European mobility programs. Challenges include student absenteeism and dropout linked to child labor. Several international and private institutions, alongside vocational and non-formal education centers, supplement the system.

# Education in Hong Kong
Hong Kong's education evolved from early missionary schools to a bilingual British-influenced system. Preschool education is partially subsidized, while primary and secondary education are compulsory and free in the public sector. The academic structure includes kindergarten through senior secondary with the HKDSE exams. English and Chinese are primary instructional languages, with a prestige divide between EMI and CMI schools. Higher education features eight UGC-funded universities and various private institutions. Recent political and social changes have impacted enrollments, curriculum content, and teacher retention. A robust international school sector exists, offering diverse curricula. Adult education and special programs for immigrant children supplement formal education.

# Education in Indonesia
Indonesia’s education system has Islamic, Dutch colonial, Japanese occupation, and modern national influences. Formal education spans early childhood (PAUD), 12 years of compulsory schooling (primary and secondary), and higher education with academic and vocational tracks. Public schools predominate but private religious schools exist. Vocational education includes pesantrens (Islamic boarding schools) and madrasahs, blending religious and secular curricula. Higher education includes various institutions such as universities and polytechnics, organized under the Indonesian National Qualification Framework. Challenges include funding disparities, teacher shortages, and educational quality, especially in remote areas.

# Education in Ghana
Ghana’s education evolved from informal oral traditions and missionary schools to a formal system with compulsory basic education (ages 4–15), divided into kindergarten, primary, and junior high school. Secondary education includes academic and vocational tracks culminating in the WASSCE exams. Tertiary education comprises universities, polytechnics, and specialized colleges, with growing private sector involvement. Efforts focus on literacy, gender parity, and vocational training, though challenges remain in equity and quality. The system is decentralized, funded partly by donor agencies. Non-formal and adult education programs address literacy and skills development.

# Education in Egypt
Egypt’s education traces back to ancient times with institutions like al-Azhar and the Theological School in Alexandria. Modern reforms began under Muhammad Ali with a dual system of religious and secular schools. British occupation curtailed expansion until post-1952 reforms introduced free public education. The current system includes compulsory basic education (ages 4–14), secondary education (general and technical tracks), and extensive tertiary institutions. Challenges persist in quality, equity, and reliance on private tutoring. The Al-Azhar system runs parallel for religious education. Agricultural education and technical training are developing. COVID-19 accelerated adoption of distance learning methods.

# Education in England
English education evolved from medieval church-run institutions to a state-funded system with compulsory education from ages 5 to 18. The system comprises early years, primary, secondary (inclusive of comprehensive, grammar, and academies), and post-16 education through sixth forms and further education colleges. The National Curriculum mandates core and foundation subjects, culminating in GCSE exams. Higher education includes universities with undergraduate to doctoral levels, with fees introduced since 1998 but significant financial aid available. Private schools serve a minority. Ofsted oversees school quality. Vocational and apprenticeship routes complement academic studies, and adult education provides lifelong learning opportunities.

# Education in Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka’s education system, with ancient Buddhist roots, was modernized during British colonial times. It is administered across multiple ministries, covering primary, secondary, collegiate, and tertiary levels. Primary education lasts five years, junior secondary four years, senior secondary two years culminating in G.C.E. Ordinary Levels, followed by collegiate years for G.C.E. Advanced Levels, determining university entrance. Most schools are government-run with some private, international, and religious institutions. Higher education includes public universities and a limited private sector. Tuition is free at the university level but highly competitive. Vocational education and widespread tuition centers coexist with criticisms of rigidity and competitiveness in the system.

# Education in France
France’s education system, shaped by 19th-century reforms, emphasizes secular, free, and compulsory schooling. The structure includes preschool (maternelle), elementary (école élémentaire), middle school (collège), and high school (lycée), leading to national exams like the baccalauréat. Education is centralized under the Ministry of National Education with a uniform curriculum. Higher education is divided between public universities and prestigious grandes écoles, with preparatory classes (CPGEs) highly selective. Private schools exist under contract or independent. Tuition is low in public institutions with financial aid available. France hosts a large international school community and embraces the Bologna Process for higher education.

# Education in Israel
Israel’s education system has three tiers: primary, middle, and high school. Schools are divided into state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab sectors, the latter emphasizing Arabic language and culture. Compulsory education spans kindergarten through 12th grade. High school matriculation (bagrut) exams and the Psychometric Entrance Test govern university admissions. The system emphasizes high educational attainment and is linked to economic development, particularly in science and technology. Challenges include disparities between sectors, resource allocation, and teacher pay. Arab and Haredi sectors have historically lagged in outcomes, though reforms and funding attempts continue.

# Education in Turkey
Turkey’s education system was unified in 1924, secularized, and expanded from primary through higher education under state control. Compulsory education increased from 5 to 12 years by 2012. Primary and secondary education cover core subjects with options at upper levels; vocational paths coexist. Universities are numerous with public and private institutions, including technical schools meeting international standards. Religious education was removed and then reintroduced as a class in schools. Foreign languages, mainly English, are taught from early grades. Education promotes nationalism via curricula and symbols. The system faces criticism regarding frequent reforms, textbook quality, and curriculum bias. Private schools and international programs also exist.

# Education in the Republic of Ireland
Ireland’s education system includes compulsory education from ages 6 to 16 with primary, secondary (junior and senior cycles), and higher education levels. The system emphasizes English as the main instruction language, with Irish-language schools widespread. Secondary education culminates in the Leaving Certificate exam. Primary and secondary schools are mainly state-funded but often under church patronage, with increasing multidenominational options. Higher education is well-ranked internationally with broad offerings. Special needs education and programs addressing disadvantaged areas exist. The academic year includes standard holidays, and homeschooling is permitted under regulation.

# Education in Iran
Iran’s education dates back to ancient Persia and evolved through Islamic and modern reforms. It includes compulsory elementary and lower secondary levels, with upper secondary and higher education divided into theoretical, technical, and vocational branches. The education system is centralized under the Ministry of Education. Higher education offers bachelor’s to doctoral degrees, with admission based on national exams. Religious education and gender segregation are prominent. Iran has a high female enrollment rate in universities. Challenges include teacher shortages, textbook quality, privatization, and political influence on curricula. Language instruction prioritizes Persian and Arabic, with growing interest in English and French.

# Education in Nigeria
Nigeria provides six years of primary education, three years each of junior and senior secondary education, and tertiary education including universities and vocational institutions. The Universal Basic Education scheme makes nine years compulsory and free. Secondary education includes federal, state, and private schools with varied teacher qualifications and infrastructure. Several standardized exams (WAEC, NECO) certify completion. Private and international schools supplement public education. Challenges include regional disparities, low literacy rates especially in the North, gender inequality, and underfunded vocational training. Government and NGOs promote female education and literacy programs. Advanced level programs like IJMB and JUPEB offer university entry alternatives.

# Education in Taiwan
Taiwan’s education system is influenced by multiple historical regimes and includes 12 years of compulsory education from primary through junior high. The system is standardized, with public and private schools offering academic and vocational tracks. Senior high school offers general and skill-based programs leading to university entrance exams. Higher education includes universities and junior colleges awarding associate to doctoral degrees. Private education and cram schools are widespread. Teachers undergo in-service training and credentials. Military education is integrated into secondary schooling. Language instruction focuses on Mandarin, with English and native languages taught. Vocational education is promoted alongside academic paths. Special education and international programs are available.
