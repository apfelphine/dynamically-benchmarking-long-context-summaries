## Education in India

India's education system has transitioned from traditional models to a complex, decentralized framework managed by both central and state governments, resulting in regional diversity in curricula and administration. Major reforms like the Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan and the National Education Policy 2020 have improved access, increased literacy rates to over 77%, and helped close gender gaps. Prestigious institutions such as IITs, IIMs, and AIIMS have elevated higher education, while recent trends favor flexible, multidisciplinary learning. Key challenges persist, however, including regional and gender disparities, infrastructure limitations, rote-based pedagogy, dropout rates among marginalized groups, and a persistent digital divide.

## Education in China

China has achieved near-universal literacy and expanded nine-year compulsory education, propelled by reforms that emphasize curricular standardization, decentralization, and equity. Vocational education and higher learning have grown, with universities enhancing their global stature and partnerships. Technology supports lifelong learning and outreach to remote areas. Nonetheless, the system faces rural-urban divides, resource imbalances, academic pressure, censorship, and access barriers for minorities and gender groups. Ongoing reform efforts target these issues and increasingly regulate private and supplemental education to foster equality and innovation.

## Education in Greece

Greece mandates nine years of compulsory public education, followed by optional academic and vocational secondary tracks linked to national exams and European standards. English is widely taught and new initiatives, including International Baccalaureate programs, are expanding options. Vocational pathways such as EPAL and EPAS support diverse career choices and lifelong learning, complemented by increasing digital modernization. Persistent issues include educator misconduct, bullying, corruption, school violence, and violations of tuition regulations.

## Education in Germany

Germany’s decentralized education system entrusts each federal state with control over curricula and policies. The structure comprises optional Kindergarten, compulsory primary school, and tiered secondary schools (Gymnasium, Realschule, Hauptschule, comprehensive and special-needs). Homeschooling is prohibited and strict separation of church and state is maintained. Reforms emphasize inclusivity, digital literacy, bilingual education, and practical teacher training, yielding high literacy rates and strong student outcomes. However, attainment remains closely tied to the type of school, region, and parental background.

## Social Inequality & Tertiary Education in Germany

Socioeconomic status and parental education heavily influence academic outcomes in Germany, with children from migrant and working-class families often underperforming, except certain Asian groups. Access to academic secondary education (Gymnasium) and tertiary institutions remains uneven, sparking debates over tracking and affirmative action. Early interventions, including quality preschool and home visits, demonstrate positive effects for disadvantaged groups. Internationalization has expanded, but schools continue to face resource and digital infrastructure gaps. Higher education, dominated by tuition-free public universities and research institutes, offers broad access but systemic disparities persist.
