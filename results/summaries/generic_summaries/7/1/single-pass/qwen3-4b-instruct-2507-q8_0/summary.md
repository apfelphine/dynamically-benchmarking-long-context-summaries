**Education in India** is a concurrent-list subject, shared between the central and state governments, with a historically fragmented system due to state autonomy. The 10+2 structure has been replaced by the 5+3+3+4 model under the 2020 National Education Policy (NEP), emphasizing foundational learning and critical thinking. The system includes a large public sector, with government schools serving the majority of students, and a growing private sector, especially in urban areas. While enrollment rates are high, quality remains a major concern—teachers face high absenteeism (up to 25%), and students often lack foundational literacy and numeracy. Private schools are often seen as offering better outcomes due to better infrastructure and accountability, though critics argue they exclude the poor and reflect socioeconomic advantages. The system faces persistent challenges such as regional disparities, gender gaps (especially in rural areas), poor facilities (e.g., lack of toilets), and curriculum issues like rote learning. Despite progress in access, quality, teacher training, and inclusion efforts (e.g., Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan), structural issues like underfunding, teacher shortages, and the lack of a robust quality assurance framework persist. The NEP 2020 aims to improve outcomes through language policy (mother tongue until Class 5), a focus on inclusive education, and a shift toward holistic, experiential learning. Vocational education is expanding, but graduates often lack industry-relevant skills. Higher education is vast (with over 150 central and state universities), yet faces criticism for producing graduates with limited employability. The system is marked by significant inequality, with disparities in access, quality, and outcomes between rural and urban areas, and among different socioeconomic and gender groups.
