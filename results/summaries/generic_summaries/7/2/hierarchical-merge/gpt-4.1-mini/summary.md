# Education in China

Since 1949, China has developed a comprehensive, multi-tiered education system emphasizing modernization, equity, and ideological education. The Nine-Year Compulsory Education policy significantly raised enrollment, with about 96% primary attendance by 1985, though rural areas still face challenges like resource shortages, higher dropout rates (especially among girls), and health concerns. Curriculum reforms balance core academics with Marxist and socialist ideology, aiming to reduce student stress and encourage critical thinking. The system mandates junior secondary education, offering diverse senior secondary paths, including vocational tracks which struggle with prestige and funding.

China boasts the world’s largest higher education system, with over 3,000 institutions and a gross enrollment rate surpassing 58%. Reforms promote merit-based admissions and align universities with national scientific and technological goals through programs like the "Double First-Class Construction." Distance learning and adult education expand access, particularly in rural areas. Challenges remain, including exam-centered assessments, inequality affecting minorities and migrant children due to the Hukou system, strict Communist Party curriculum control, and social issues such as internet addiction and school meal safety.

# Education in Germany

Germany’s decentralized education system begins with primary school (Grundschule) and early tracking into differentiated secondary schools: Gymnasium (university preparatory), Realschule (intermediate), Hauptschule (vocational), Gesamtschule (comprehensive), and Sonderschule (special needs). The Gymnasium concludes with the Abitur exam, qualifying students for university entry. Vocational education is integral, combining classroom learning with apprenticeships. While efforts toward inclusion continue, many special-needs students attend separate schools.

School days generally start between 7:30 and 8:15 a.m. with 45-minute lessons; older students have longer days with curricula covering foreign languages, sciences, arts, and ethics or religion. Formal school uniforms are uncommon, and corporal punishment is banned. About 9.2% of students attend private schools under state standards, with numerous international schools offering English curricula. Despite high literacy rates and respected institutions, Germany faces challenges with socioeconomic disparities linked to early tracking, aging infrastructure, and slow digitalization in classrooms. Higher education remains largely public, low-cost, research-intensive, but also contends with social inequalities and infrastructure issues.

# Education in India

India’s education system follows the National Education Policy 2020, introducing a “5+3+3+4” curricular structure to foster holistic development and strong foundational literacy. Primary enrollment is high, but secondary attendance and access to higher education remain uneven. India has a vast higher education network, including prestigious institutions like the IITs and IISc, yet suffers from challenges such as inadequate infrastructure, teacher absenteeism, rote learning, regulatory weaknesses, and uneven quality—particularly in private schools.

Recent reforms emphasize mother tongue instruction, multidisciplinary higher education, and increased funding to address social inequities impacting rural populations, girls, and marginalized communities. Initiatives also target digital education, nutrition, and adult literacy, although federal governance complicates uniform policy implementation.

# Education and Vocational Training in Greece

Greece’s education system starts with compulsory two-year preschool (ages 4–6), six years of primary school, and three years of lower secondary (Gymnasium) with automatic progression. The school year runs September to June with about five hours of classes daily; English instruction begins in kindergarten. The system includes special and experimental schools, with formal assessment evolving from informal evaluation in early years to graded exams by the end of primary school.

Aligned with the Hellenic Qualification Framework and European standards, compulsory education covers primary and lower secondary levels. Upper secondary offers diverse academic and vocational tracks culminating in the High School Diploma (Apolytirio Lykeiou, EQF Level 4). Vocational education features prominently through dual-system Vocational Schools (EPAS), Vocational Training Schools (ESK), and advanced two-year programs (SAEK). From 2026, some public schools will offer the International Baccalaureate Diploma Programme in English. Higher education is mainly tuition-free in public universities, supplemented by private colleges with accredited international degrees. Lifelong learning—including MOOCs and the National Skills Competition—supports vocational excellence.

Despite structural progress, Greece faces challenges such as corruption, misconduct, and weak prosecution within the education sector. Tuition fees for some postgraduate and vocational programs had conflicted with constitutional guarantees of free education, though reforms since 2013 address these issues. Corporal punishment was abolished by 2005. Ongoing efforts focus on flexible educational pathways, inclusion, quality assurance, and workforce readiness amid continuing reforms.
