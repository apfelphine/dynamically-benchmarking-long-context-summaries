# Education in Greece

Education in Greece is structured into preschool, primary, lower secondary (Gymnasium), and upper secondary education (Lyceum), followed by tertiary education at universities and specialized academies. Preschool is compulsory for ages 4–6, integrated with primary schools, and includes foreign language learning starting with English at kindergarten. Primary education lasts six years (ages 6–12), followed by compulsory lower secondary school (Gymnasium) for three years (ages 12–15). Upper secondary education (Lyceum) lasts three years (ages 15–18) and is non-compulsory, culminating in national examinations (Pan-Hellenic Examinations) essential for university admission.

The system includes diverse educational types such as experimental, special, music, art, vocational (EPAL), and evening schools. Vocational education offers a dual system combining school and workplace training, with pathways to higher education via apprenticeship and certification levels aligned with the Hellenic Qualification Framework (HQF). Greece also supports adult education and lifelong learning with second-chance schools and online platforms. Despite extensive frameworks, the Greek education sector faces challenges including corruption reports, historically permitted corporal punishment (now banned), bullying issues, and controversies over postgraduate tuition fees, which conflict with constitutional provisions for free education.

# Education in India

India’s education system operates under both central and state governance, exhibiting significant regional variation. The 2020 National Education Policy restructured schooling into a 5+3+3+4 model spanning foundational (pre-primary), preparatory, middle, and secondary stages, emphasizing holistic and activity-based learning with mother-tongue instruction up to grade 5. Pre-primary education is optional; primary education covers ages 6–14, and secondary education (grades 9–12) is divided into lower (9–10) and senior (11–12) stages with academic streams (Science, Commerce, Humanities). Higher education includes vocational training and university degrees, governed by bodies like UGC and AICTE.

India has a vast network of government, government-aided private, and unaided private schools, with a growing private sector influenced by demand for English education. Literacy rates have improved considerably, though challenges persist in quality, teacher absenteeism, infrastructure, gender disparities, and rural access. NGOs play a significant role in education outreach. The system faces issues such as grade inflation, overemphasis on engineering and medical fields, examination pressure, and corruption. The government has launched programs targeting universal education, scholarships, midday meals, and teacher training, alongside open and distance learning to expand access. Home-schooling is legal but less common.

# Education in Germany

Germany’s education is state-controlled with a tripartite secondary system: Gymnasium (academic track to university), Realschule (intermediate), Hauptschule (vocational preparation), plus Gesamtschule (comprehensive) and special schools for disabilities. Preschool (Kindergarten) is voluntary, serving ages 2–6. Primary education covers grades 1–4 (or 6 in some states), followed by secondary education with early tracking at grade 5 or 7 depending on the state. Apprenticeship (Duale Ausbildung) combines vocational school and in-company training.

German universities are mostly public with low or no tuition fees and are highly ranked internationally, emphasizing research and academic freedom. Admissions typically require the Abitur or equivalent. Private schools (Ersatzschulen) exist under constitutional protection and must meet minimum standards. The education system faces debates over early tracking, social inequality, and provision for disadvantaged groups. Literacy rates exceed 99%. Germany actively supports bilingual education, digital integration, and inclusive education. Corporate and educational research institutes complement universities. PISA assessments indicate mixed performance, with socioeconomic factors strongly influencing outcomes.

# Education in China

China provides compulsory nine-year education (6 years primary, 3 years junior secondary) with extensive efforts to universalize literacy and expand vocational training. Education reforms since 1978 have emphasized modernization, science and technology education, and diversification of pathways including formal, vocational, and adult education. Senior secondary education (grades 10–12) includes both academic and vocational tracks leading to university or employment.

China’s education system is highly centralized with curriculum and materials tightly controlled by the Communist Party, including mandatory ideological education and censorship. The gaokao university entrance exam remains a critical determinant for higher education access. English is typically taught starting around age 10, though teaching methods are criticized for rote learning.

Private education has grown, but since 2021 strict controls on for-profit tutoring and foreign influence have been enacted to reduce educational costs and inequality. Online education has expanded rapidly, supported by national infrastructure initiatives.

China ranks among the leaders in global university quality and student achievement in international assessments, especially in selected urban regions. However, disparities exist between rural and urban areas, with challenges in educational equity for minorities, migrant children, and disabled students. The government continues efforts to improve teacher training, ICT integration, and adult education, while facing criticism for intense academic pressure, regional inequality, and political control over content and academic freedom.
