# Education in Germany

Germany’s education system is state-controlled, with compulsory schooling from ages 6 to 16. Secondary education is divided into lower and upper levels, with multiple school types: Gymnasium (prepares for university, ends with Abitur), Realschule (intermediate, ends with Mittlere Reife), Hauptschule (vocational focus, ends with Hauptschulabschluss), Gesamtschule (comprehensive school), and special needs schools (Förderschulen). Students may pursue vocational training through the dual system combining classroom and in-company training. Literacy exceeds 99%, but social class and immigrant status strongly influence academic success. The system favors early selection, with substantial debate on tracking versus comprehensive schooling.

Historically, education evolved from Prussian compulsory Lutheran schooling to a centralized Imperial system featuring various secondary school types. Post-World War II, education was decentralized among states, leading to regional variation. Recent reforms aimed at bilingual education, digitalization, and reducing school years have been implemented. Universities are mostly public with low or no tuition fees, distinguished into research universities (Universitäten) and applied sciences universities (Fachhochschulen). Entrance mostly requires the Abitur, with alternative pathways through vocational qualifications. Germany has a strong research culture supported by renowned institutes and large state-funded programs.

# Education in Greece

Greece offers compulsory preschool (ages 4–6), six years of primary education, and three years each of compulsory lower secondary (Gymnasium) and non-compulsory upper secondary (Lyceum). Foreign language education begins early, with English as core from preschool and options like French or German in secondary. Secondary education includes various school types: general, vocational, special, and experimental, with national exams (Pan-Hellenic Examinations) determining university eligibility.

Vocational training is available through schools such as EPAL and experimental programs, many aligned with European frameworks. Higher education consists of public autonomous universities and specialist academies; private HEIs are now permitted under strict regulation. Greece also supports non-formal education, digital platforms, and lifelong learning centres. Challenges include prolonged school closures, corruption, bullying, and previously allowed corporal punishment, now banned.

# Education in China

China’s education has rapidly evolved from low literacy at the PRC's founding to a vast compulsory system covering nine years (six years primary and three years junior secondary) and extensive vocational and higher education sectors. Since the 1980s, reforms have focused on modernization, technical training, and expanding university enrollment. The system emphasizes state control, ideological education, and has been critiqued for rote memorization and regional inequalities, especially between urban and rural areas.

Secondary education is divided into compulsory junior secondary and non-compulsory senior secondary, with entrance exams (Zhongkao) gatekeeping advanced schooling. Vocational education has developed alongside general education but struggles with quality and societal perception. Private schools and international programs are increasing, though the government restricts foreign influence. Higher education is the largest globally, with significant government investment and a focus on elite institutions under the Double First-Class plan. Adult education, online learning, ICT integration, and teacher training are expanding. The government enforces strict control over curricula and academic freedom, including censorship, to promote socialist values. Challenges include intense competition, regional disparity, stress on memorization, and discrimination against minorities and disabled students.

# Education in India

India’s education system is a complex mix of central and state governance, characterized by vast regional disparities and rapid growth in enrollment. The National Education Policy 2020 restructures schooling into a 5+3+3+4 model, emphasizing foundational learning and critical thinking, with a focus on mother tongue instruction. Pre-primary education is growing but not universal, and primary education is compulsory with schemes like the Midday Meal addressing nutrition and attendance.

Secondary education is exam-oriented, divided into lower (classes 9-10) and higher secondary (11-12) phases, with streams in science, commerce, and humanities. Vocational education is expanding but remains limited. Higher education is the third largest globally, overseen by bodies like UGC and AICTE, but faces issues of quality, accreditation, and overemphasis on engineering and medicine. Private schools are prevalent, especially in urban areas, often offering English-medium instruction.

Challenges include teacher absenteeism, curriculum rigidity, gender and rural-urban inequalities, and corruption concerns. NGOs play a significant role in supporting marginalized groups. Coaching institutes are widespread, sometimes linked to student stress and suicides. Legislative reforms have made education a fundamental right for 6-14-year-olds, with increased government expenditure planned but still insufficient. The system balances traditional contexts and modern demands, aiming for inclusivity and quality improvements.
