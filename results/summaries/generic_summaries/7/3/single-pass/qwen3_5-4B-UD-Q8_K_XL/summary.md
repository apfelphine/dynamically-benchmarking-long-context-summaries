# Education Systems in Germany, Greece, China, and India

## Germany
Germany's education system is decentralized, with primary responsibility lying with the sixteen federal states (*Länder*). It features a tripartite secondary system: the *Gymnasium* (academic track leading to university), the *Realschule* (intermediate track), and the *Hauptschule* (vocational track), alongside comprehensive *Gesamtschulen*. A highly respected dual education system (*Duale Ausbildung*) combines vocational apprenticeships with theoretical instruction at vocational schools. While PISA scores have fluctuated, Germany consistently performs well internationally. The system emphasizes religious education provided by churches and has strict regulations regarding homeschooling, which is illegal. Recent reforms aim to increase inclusion and digital integration, though significant disparities based on socioeconomic status persist.

## Greece
Greece operates a unified framework where compulsory education lasts nine years, comprising six years of primary school and three years of lower secondary education (*Gymnasium*). Upper secondary education (*Lyceum*) is non-compulsory and lasts three years, culminating in the Pan-Hellenic Examinations for university admission. The system includes specialized tracks for arts, music, and vocational training (*EPAL*), as well as evening schools for adults. Public universities generally offer free tuition, though postgraduate programs and certain private institutions may charge fees. Vocational education is highly developed, with a focus on dual training models and a National Qualifications Framework aligned with European standards.

## China
China's education system has undergone massive expansion since 1949, with nine years of compulsory education now effectively covering 85% of the population. The structure consists of six years of primary school and three years of junior high, followed by three years of senior high. The *Gaokao* (National College Entrance Examination) remains the central mechanism for university admission, driving intense academic competition. While primary and secondary enrollment rates are near universal, significant regional disparities exist between urban centers and rural areas. The government heavily emphasizes science, technology, and vocational training, although recent reforms aim to reduce homework burdens and curb the private tutoring industry. Higher education is the largest in the world, with a strong focus on elite institutions like the "Double First-Class" universities.

## India
India's education system is a concurrent subject shared by the central and state governments, leading to vast variations in policy and quality. The traditional structure follows a 10+2 pattern (five years primary, three years middle, two years secondary, two years higher secondary). Challenges include low literacy rates in certain regions, high teacher absenteeism in government schools, and a heavy reliance on private coaching institutes for competitive entrance exams. The government has launched major initiatives like the Right to Education Act (2009) and the Midday Meal Scheme to improve access and nutrition. A new National Education Policy (2020) proposes a 5+3+3+4 model to focus on foundational learning and critical thinking, aiming to transform the system by 2030.
