**Education in Nigeria** operates within a decentralized framework comprising federal, state, and private sectors, with the Universal Basic Education (UBE) Act of 1999 establishing free, compulsory education for nine years (primary and junior secondary). The system follows a 6-3-3-4 structure, leading to the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) administered by the West African Examination Council (WAEC) and the National Examination Council (NECO), which are prerequisites for university admission. Secondary education is further categorized into federal government colleges, state-owned schools, and expensive private institutions, with teacher qualifications ranging from a National Certificate in Education to a bachelor's degree. Tertiary education is dominated by public universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education, though the proliferation of private universities has increased since the 1990s. Challenges persist in rural areas due to infrastructure deficits, insecurity, and a shortage of qualified teachers, while literacy rates remain low, particularly in the north. Vocational education is overseen by the National Board for Technical Education, though it faces criticism for outdated curricula and low completion rates.

**Education in Iran** is highly centralized under the Ministry of Education, which controls curriculum, financing, and administration from kindergarten through university. The system emphasizes Islamic values, with mandatory religious instruction integrated into the curriculum and schools segregated by gender since the 1979 Islamic Revolution. Compulsory education covers six years of primary school and three years of lower secondary school (grades 7–9), while upper secondary education (grades 10–12) is not compulsory but has a high enrollment rate. Admission to public universities is based on the competitive *Konkur* exam, and graduates are often required to serve the state for a period equivalent to their studies. Higher education institutions include universities, institutes, and conservatories, with technical fields enjoying strong international recognition. Despite high government spending, the system faces issues such as teacher shortages, poor infrastructure in remote regions, and a disparity between public and private education quality.

**Education in France** is characterized by a highly centralized, secular (*laïque*) system regulated by the Ministry of National Education. Compulsory education spans from ages 3 to 16, covering primary, middle school (*collège*), and high school (*lycée*), all following a unified national curriculum. Higher education is divided between *grandes écoles* (elite, selective institutions) and public universities, with tuition fees remaining very low for EU citizens but rising for international students. The system places significant emphasis on student integration and a "common culture," though debates continue regarding the treatment of immigrant students and the role of religious symbols. France participates actively in international educational programs like Erasmus+, attracting hundreds of thousands of foreign students annually.

**Education in Taiwan** features a 12-year compulsory system (primary and junior high) followed by a selective senior high school track or vocational path. Access to university is primarily controlled by the National University Entrance Examination, though a direct entry system exists for students completing the Interim Joint Matriculation Board (IJMB) or Joint Universities Preliminary Examinations Board (JUPEB) programs. The system is highly competitive, with a strong emphasis on academic achievement and cram schools (*buxiban*) being a significant part of the educational landscape. Tertiary education includes a mix of public and private universities, with engineering being a dominant field. Recent reforms have aimed to reduce the pressure on students and improve vocational training recognition.

**Education in England** is governed by the Office for Standards in Education (Ofsted) and follows a structured National Curriculum from ages 5 to 16, divided into Key Stages. Compulsory education continues until age 18, with students required to remain in full-time education or training. The system includes state-funded schools (academies, community schools, free schools) and private independent schools, with a strong emphasis on standardized testing (GCSEs and A-levels). Higher education is highly competitive, with universities ranked by the Russell Group and significant funding for international students. Adult education and lifelong learning are supported through various colleges and distance learning options.

**Education in Israel** is divided into four sectors: state-secular, state-religious, independent religious (Haredi), and Arab. Compulsory education lasts until age 18, with the *Bagrut* (matriculation) exam serving as the gateway to university, military service, and employment. The system is praised for its high levels of scientific and technological education, yet it faces challenges regarding the Haredi sector's low academic participation and disparities in resource allocation between Jewish and Arab schools. Higher education is subsidized by the government, with students receiving significant tuition discounts after military service.

**Education in Sweden** operates under a comprehensive nine-year *grundskola* system with mandatory attendance from ages 6 to 16, followed by optional upper secondary education (*gymnasieskola*). The system emphasizes equality, free preschool education, and a focus on student well-being over competitive grading. Higher education is free for EU/EEA citizens, divided between universities and university colleges, with a strong integration of vocational training. Independent schools (*friskolor*) have been granted public funding since 1992, though debates continue regarding their impact on equity and academic standards.

**Education in Sri Lanka** has a centralized structure managed by the Ministry of Education, with free primary and secondary education. The system is highly competitive, with admission to state universities determined by the GCE Advanced Level (A/L) examination, leading to a high rate of graduate unemployment. There is a significant presence of private schools and international institutions catering to the expatriate community, while religious schools (*pirivenas* and *madrasas*) play a role in religious education. Recent reforms have attempted to decentralize control to provincial councils, but challenges regarding equity and quality persist.

**Education in Norway** features a unified nine-year *grunnskole* system with free compulsory education from ages 6 to 16, followed by optional upper secondary education (*videregående skole*). The upper secondary system offers both general and vocational pathways, with a strong emphasis on practical training and apprenticeships. Higher education is free for EU/EEA citizens and divided between universities and university colleges, with a rigorous selection process based on upper secondary grades. The system is known for its focus on student well-being, long summer vacations, and high international performance in reading and mathematics.

**Education in Egypt** is a highly centralized system divided into basic (primary and preparatory), secondary, and tertiary levels. Compulsory education covers nine years, but access to higher education is competitive and expensive, with a significant "wealth gap" affecting enrollment. The system faces severe challenges, including low teacher salaries, poor infrastructure, and a heavy reliance on private tutoring to supplement public education. While the government has implemented reforms to improve quality and access, disparities between urban and rural areas remain pronounced.

**Education in Armenia** is free and compulsory for 12 years, transitioning from a centralized Soviet model to a more decentralized system post-1991. The curriculum emphasizes Armenian language and culture, with English learning growing rapidly. Higher education is highly competitive, with state universities offering free education but limited seats. The system includes a mix of public and private institutions, with a focus on fields such as medicine, engineering, and information technology. Challenges include student absenteeism linked to child labor and a need to modernize teaching methods.

**Education in Indonesia** is a 12-year compulsory system with a focus on secular nationalism through the *Pancasila* curriculum. The system is highly decentralized, with a mix of state, private, and Islamic schools (including *pesantren*). Primary and middle schools are free, while high school involves nominal fees. The system faces challenges regarding teacher shortages, funding, and equity between Java and the outer islands. Vocational education is integrated into the senior high school level, offering diverse pathways for technical and professional training.
