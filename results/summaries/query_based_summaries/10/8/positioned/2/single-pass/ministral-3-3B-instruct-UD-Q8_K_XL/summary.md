Education in Nigeria spans a structured system from primary to tertiary education, emphasizing both formal and informal learning.

**Primary Education** begins at age 5, lasting six years, culminating in a first school-leaving certificate. Subjects include Mathematics, English, religious knowledge, agricultural science, and indigenous languages. The Universal Basic Education (UBE) program, introduced in 1999, aims to make education free, compulsory, and a right for all children, covering nine years of schooling (six years of primary and three years of junior secondary school). The UBE is overseen by the Universal Basic Education Commission (UBEC).

**Secondary Education** follows, structured into Junior Secondary School (JSS) and Senior Secondary School (SSS). The 6-3-3-4 system, introduced in 1982, divides secondary education into three years of JSS and three years of SSS, with students taking core subjects like English, Mathematics, and Social Studies, complemented by electives. Federal-sponsored secondary schools, often boarding schools, are merit-based and offer competitive admissions. State-sponsored schools, while free, require students to pay for books and miscellaneous expenses. Private and religious schools cater to those seeking specialized education, often with higher fees.

**Promotional Examinations** include the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE), administered by the West African Examination Council (WAEC), and the General Certificate of Education (GCE). These exams determine eligibility for tertiary education. The National Examination Council (NECO) also conducts SSCE and GCE exams.

**Tertiary Education** includes universities, polytechnics, colleges of education, and vocational training institutions. Universities are divided into tiers, with the first generation universities being fully funded by the federal government. Admission to universities requires passing the Unified Tertiary Matriculation Examination (UTME) or meeting direct entry criteria. Vocational education, managed by the National Board for Technical Education, focuses on practical skills, though challenges like outdated curricula and teacher shortages persist.

**Vocational and Informal Education** are significant components, with apprenticeships and informal programs offering skills training. Despite efforts to improve quality, Nigeria faces issues like high illiteracy rates (31% as of 2022), regional disparities, and reliance on private tutoring. The government has introduced initiatives like the National Digital Literacy Program to enhance digital skills and improve access to education.
