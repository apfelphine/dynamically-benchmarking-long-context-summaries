## Education in Nigeria: Structure, Policies, and Challenges

Education in Nigeria is structured into distinct levels: primary, secondary, and tertiary, overseen mainly by government agencies such as the Universal Basic Education Commission (UBEC) and National Universities Commission (NUC). Primary education starts at age 5, lasts six years, and is followed by six years of secondary education (three years each for junior and senior levels) under the 6-3-3-4 or revised 9-3-4 system. Entry into secondary school requires passing a Common Entrance Examination, and core subjects at both primary and secondary levels include English, Mathematics, and major indigenous languages. The Universal Basic Education scheme mandates nine years of free, compulsory schooling with continuous assessment. Public, state, and federal secondary schools differ in funding, teacher qualifications, and resources, while private and religious schools often offer superior facilities and attract qualified staff. The West African Examination Council (WAEC) and National Examination Council (NECO) administer standardized exams that determine university eligibility.

Tertiary education encompasses universities, polytechnics, monotechnics, and colleges, with a mix of federal, state, and private institutions. Admission relies on standardized exams like JAMB and SSCE, with advanced programs such as IJMB and JUPEB providing alternative university entry routes. Vocational education, administered by the National Board for Technical Education and NABTEB, and informal literacy initiatives also play prominent roles. Challenges persist in teacher shortages, funding, literacy, and regional disparities—particularly in the north—exacerbated by poverty, insecurity, and sociocultural barriers to female education. Despite ongoing reforms and government partnerships with organizations like UNESCO and UNICEF targeting mass literacy, digital skills, and girls’ education, progress is constrained by instability and systemic issues. Efforts to expand access and improve quality continue, reflected in rising enrollment, increased numbers of educational institutions, and active policy interventions aimed at equity and inclusion.
