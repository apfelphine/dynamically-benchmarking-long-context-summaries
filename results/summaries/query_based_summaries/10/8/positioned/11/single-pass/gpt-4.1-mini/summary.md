## Education in Nigeria

Education in Nigeria consists of primary, secondary, and tertiary levels under a 9-3-4 system refined from earlier models. Primary education starts at around age 5 and lasts six years, culminating in a first school-leaving certificate. The Universal Basic Education (UBE) program integrates six years of primary and three years of junior secondary school, making basic education free, compulsory, and a right for every child. Subjects include Mathematics, English, religious knowledge, agricultural science, and indigenous languages, with private schools offering additional subjects such as Computer Science and French.

Secondary education spans six years (three years each for Junior Secondary School and Senior Secondary School), with a curriculum combining core subjects and electives. Federal government colleges ("unity schools") and state-sponsored secondary schools offer varying quality and resources, with federal schools generally regarded as elite. Private and religious schools tend to have smaller classes and better facilities but are expensive. Students take national examinations like the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) conducted by the West African Examination Council (WAEC) and the National Examination Council (NECO). Technical colleges and vocational education operate alongside general secondary schools, with vocational training emphasized to combat unemployment and poverty, but face challenges such as low completion rates and inadequate resources.

Tertiary education includes public and private universities, polytechnics, monotechnics, and colleges of education, with admissions based on secondary school exam results. University programs range from four to six years depending on the field of study. Nigeria has over 150 universities, with first, second, and third generation institutions created to meet educational demand. Private universities have grown since 1993 under government regulation. Vocational and informal education remain significant, including apprenticeships and community literacy programs. Literacy rates remain low relative to global standards, especially in northern regions, prompting government and international efforts to increase literacy and digital skills. Female education faces challenges from cultural barriers, poverty, and security issues, though initiatives like UNICEF’s Girls’ Education Project have been implemented. Educational quality and access disparities persist, especially between urban and rural areas, and between genders.
