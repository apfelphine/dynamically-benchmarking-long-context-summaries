**Education in Nigeria**

Nigeria's education system is structured into primary, secondary, tertiary, and informal education, with a focus on national policy and equity. Primary education spans six years, beginning at age 5, and includes core subjects such as Mathematics, English, Religious Knowledge, and indigenous languages. Students must pass a Common Entrance Examination to gain admission to government or private secondary schools. The Universal Basic Education (UBE) policy, introduced in 1999, mandates nine years of compulsory, free, and accessible schooling (6 years primary and 3 years junior secondary), monitored by the Universal Basic Education Commission (UBEC).

Secondary education follows a 6-3-3-4 system, with students progressing through 3 years of Junior Secondary School (JSS) and 3 years of Senior Secondary School (SSS). Core subjects include English, Mathematics, Social Studies, and Science, with elective subjects based on student interests. Students take the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE), administered by the West African Examination Council (WAEC), which is a prerequisite for university admission. Private, religious, and independent secondary schools are expensive, with fees rising sharply (up to 50–70% in 2024), but offer smaller class sizes and better facilities. Federal government schools, known as "unity schools," are prestigious and merit-based, while state schools face challenges such as underfunding and teacher shortages.

Tertiary education includes public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education, with a total of 153 universities registered. The government has expanded access through the establishment of new private universities, including 9 in 2015 and 20 new ones in 2021. Admission to universities requires passing the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB) examination, with entry into degree programs based on merit and subject performance. Vocational education is administered by the National Board for Technical Education and offers National Technical Certificates, supported by apprenticeships to instill practical skills. Literacy rates remain low (62.02% in 2018), with regional disparities in the North-East and North-West, and efforts are underway to improve digital literacy to 95% by 2030. Female education faces challenges such as teen pregnancy, religious beliefs, and poverty, though initiatives like the Girls' Education Project have improved access. Rural education suffers from poor infrastructure, insecurity, and non-payment of staff. The system emphasizes national integration and equity, with ongoing reforms to address quality, access, and teacher training.
