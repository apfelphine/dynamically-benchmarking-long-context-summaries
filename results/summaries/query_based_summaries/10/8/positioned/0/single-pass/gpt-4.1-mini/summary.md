## Education in Nigeria

Education in Nigeria is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a growing emphasis on vocational and informal education. Primary education starts around age 5 and lasts six years, culminating in a first school-leaving certificate. The Universal Basic Education (UBE) program mandates free, compulsory nine-year basic education, combining primary and junior secondary schooling, overseen by the Universal Basic Education Commission (UBEC). Secondary education follows a "6-3-3-4" system: six years divided into three years each of junior and senior secondary school. Students sit for the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) administered by the West African Examination Council (WAEC) and the National Examination Council (NECO). Federal, state, and private schools operate at this level, with federal schools generally maintaining higher standards and stricter teacher qualifications than state schools; private schools often offer higher fees, better resources, and qualified staff.

Tertiary education includes universities (federal, state, and private), polytechnics, monotechnics, and colleges of education. As of 2017, Nigeria had over 150 universities regulated by the Nigerian University Commission, with increased liberalization allowing private institutions since 1993. Entry to universities typically requires credit passes in SSCE/GCE and satisfactory Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB) scores. Advanced level programs like IJMB and JUPEB provide alternative university admission routes. Vocational education, overseen by the National Board for Technical Education and the National Business and Technical Examinations Board, offers certificates and apprenticeships aimed at addressing skills gaps, though challenges persist with teacher shortages and outdated curricula. Informal education and literacy programs play a key role but suffer from funding deficits. Female education faces barriers including poverty, cultural factors, and insecurity, but UNICEF and government initiatives seek to improve enrollment and retention. Literacy rates remain low, especially in northern regions, but the government is actively pursuing policies to enhance both traditional and digital literacy.
