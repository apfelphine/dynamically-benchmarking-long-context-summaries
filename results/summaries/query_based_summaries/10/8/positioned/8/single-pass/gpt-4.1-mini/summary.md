**Education in Nigeria**

Education in Nigeria is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a formal emphasis on Universal Basic Education (UBE) covering 9 years: 6 years of primary and 3 years of junior secondary education, made free and compulsory under the Universal Basic Education Commission. Primary education begins around age 5, covering core subjects like Mathematics, English, Religious Knowledge, Agricultural science, and indigenous languages, with private schools often including Computer Science and French. Secondary education spans 6 years—3 years junior secondary (JSS) and 3 years senior secondary (SSS)—with a curriculum comprising core subjects and electives, culminating in national examinations like the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) administered by WAEC and NECO. Federal-sponsored "unity schools" and state-sponsored secondary schools differ in funding, reputation, and teacher qualifications, with many state schools suffering from understaffing and unqualified teachers. Private secondary schools, generally more expensive, offer better infrastructure, smaller class sizes, and higher qualified teaching staff.

Tertiary education comprises public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education, regulated and accredited by bodies like the Nigerian Universities Commission. Admission requires senior secondary certification and passing the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB) exam. Universities are categorized into first, second, and third generations based on establishment history, along with state and private universities. Vocational education is managed by the National Board for Technical Education with formal and informal (apprenticeship) pathways, but challenges include teacher shortages and outdated curricula. Informal education and literacy programs exist but are hindered by decentralization and funding issues. Female education faces barriers such as poverty, early marriage, and regional insecurity, but initiatives like UNICEF’s Girls' Education Project aim to improve access. Literacy rates remain low at around 62%, with disparities between regions and genders being significant despite ongoing governmental and international efforts to improve general and digital literacy.
