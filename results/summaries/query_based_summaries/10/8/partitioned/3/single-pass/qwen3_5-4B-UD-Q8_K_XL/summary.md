# Education in Nigeria

Nigeria's education system is characterized by a complex structure involving primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, often operating under the "6-3-3-4" model, though reforms have shifted toward a "9-3-4" system to streamline basic education. The government designates education as a fundamental right through the Universal Basic Education (UBE) policy, which mandates free and compulsory schooling for nine years (ages 4–13), aiming to eliminate illiteracy and ensure equitable access. Despite this legal framework, the system faces significant challenges, including severe funding shortages, a shortage of qualified teachers, and poor infrastructure, particularly in rural areas and the North. These issues contribute to low literacy rates, which remain significantly higher in the North compared to the South, and hinder the quality of instruction across both public and private sectors.

The tertiary education sector is heavily dominated by the federal government, with universities categorized into first, second, and third generations based on their establishment dates and funding sources. While the government maintains control over standards and funding, a growing number of private universities have been approved to address demand, although the overall quality of higher education varies widely. Vocational and technical education, managed by bodies like the National Board for Technical Education, plays a crucial role in addressing unemployment but suffers from outdated curricula and a lack of industry alignment. Furthermore, the system relies on a dual examination framework administered by the West African Examination Council (WAEC) and the National Examination Council (NECO) to determine student progression and university admission, with ongoing efforts to integrate digital literacy and modernize the curriculum to meet global standards.
