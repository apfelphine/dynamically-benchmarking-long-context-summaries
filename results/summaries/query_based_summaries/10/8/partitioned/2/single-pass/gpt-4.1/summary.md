## Education in Nigeria

Education in Nigeria is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with formal schooling beginning at around age five. The Universal Basic Education (UBE) program ensures nine years of free, compulsory schooling combining six years of primary education and three years of junior secondary school. Primary school subjects include Mathematics, English, Religious Knowledge, and indigenous languages, with private schools additionally offering subjects like Computer Science and French. Students transition to secondary education following a common entrance examination. Secondary education comprises three years of Junior Secondary School (JSS) and three years of Senior Secondary School (SSS), covering a broad curriculum with core and elective subjects. Federal and state governments, as well as private and religious organizations, run secondary schools, though state schools often face challenges due to underfunding and understaffing.

Tertiary education encompasses universities, polytechnics, monotechnics, and colleges of education, regulated by government bodies such as the National Universities Commission (NUC). Admission typically follows the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB) examinations, though alternative advanced-level programs like IJMB and JUPEB offer direct entry to higher levels. Nigeria has a significant number of public and private universities, polytechnics, and colleges, with recent increases in private sector participation. Vocational education is overseen by the National Board for Technical Education, and the government supports apprenticeships and informal education to address labor market needs, although implementation has faced challenges. The country also has a strong international education presence, with hundreds of international schools, and is making efforts to boost literacy rates—including digital literacy—with policies focused on equitable access, adult literacy programs, and targeted initiatives for vulnerable populations. Despite progress, regional disparities, funding issues, and socio-economic barriers remain persistent challenges.
