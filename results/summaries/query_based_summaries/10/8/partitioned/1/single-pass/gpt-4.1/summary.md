## Education in Nigeria – Summary

**Structure and Policy:**  
Education in Nigeria comprises primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary education typically starts at age 5 and lasts six years, focusing on core subjects such as Mathematics, English, local languages, and religious knowledge. In 1999, Universal Basic Education (UBE) was introduced, mandating 9 years of free, compulsory schooling—6 years of primary school followed by 3 years of junior secondary school. Continuous assessment determines progression, and the system aims to be inclusive, covering vulnerable groups. Secondary education follows the 6-3-3-4 (later 9-3-4) structure—three years each of junior and senior secondary school, with curricula catering to both academic and vocational routes. Unity schools (federal colleges), state schools, and private institutions all exist, with unity schools generally seen as elite. Entry into tertiary education is competitive, requiring SSCE/GCE credits and performance in the JAMB entrance exam. Nigeria has a large, expanding tertiary sector, including federal, state, and over 99 private universities, with program lengths varying by discipline.

**Challenges and Developments:**  
Education accessibility faces challenges such as infrastructure deficits, understaffing, teacher qualification disparities, and widespread illiteracy (as of 2022, literacy rate at ~69%). Regional disparities are significant, especially in northern states. Female education has progressed but is still hindered by factors including poverty, early marriage, and insecurity. The government and NGOs sponsor initiatives like Girls' Education Project and cash transfer programs to boost female enrollment and retention. Vocational and informal education are crucial for employment and community development, yet suffer from funding and political will limitations. Ongoing efforts target digital literacy and aim for 95% digital literacy by 2030. Examination systems include SSCE and GCE, administered by WAEC and NECO, and advanced programs (IJMB, JUPEB) offer alternative university entry paths. Education is recognized as a national development instrument, but investment and outcomes vary widely by region, socioeconomic status, and gender.
