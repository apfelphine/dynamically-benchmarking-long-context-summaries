## Education in Nigeria: Structure, Access, and Challenges

Education in Nigeria follows a structured path: six years of primary school, three years of junior secondary, three years of senior secondary, and four years of tertiary education (the 6-3-3-4 system, recently revised to 9-3-4). Primary education starts at age five and features instruction in Mathematics, English, religious studies, agricultural science, and indigenous languages, with private schools adding subjects like Computer Science and French. The Universal Basic Education (UBE) program aims to provide nine years of free, compulsory schooling, monitored by the Universal Basic Education Commission (UBEC). State, federal, private, and religious schools offer secondary education, with teacher qualifications varying and federal schools generally regarded as elite.

Nigeria's post-secondary system includes universities, polytechnics, monotechnics, and colleges of education, with federal, state, and private institutions. Admission requirements include the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) results and national entrance exams administered by agencies like WAEC and JAMB. Advanced level programs such as IJMB and JUPEB offer alternative routes to university admission. Vocational education and informal apprenticeships are widespread, though technical education suffers from challenges such as teacher shortages and outdated curricula. Access to education is hampered by regional disparities, gender biases, poor infrastructure—especially in rural areas—and insecurity. Literacy remains a significant challenge; government initiatives aim to boost literacy rates and digital skills, though many funding and policy hurdles persist.

## Equity, Female Education, and Informal Learning

Nigeria continues to face equity issues, particularly in female education. Barriers include poverty, early marriage, religious beliefs, and insecurity from militant groups. Efforts—such as the Girls' Education Project and UNICEF-backed cash transfer programs—target increased enrollment and retention for girls. Informal education and adult literacy initiatives are active, but funding constraints and inconsistent policy support limit their impact. Literacy rates vary widely by region; while recent government efforts focus on mass literacy and digital skills, millions of Nigerian children remain out of school, most acutely in the North. Vocational and informal education are significant, with apprenticeships valued for skill development and community cohesion, though national strategy implementation faces political and financial setbacks.
