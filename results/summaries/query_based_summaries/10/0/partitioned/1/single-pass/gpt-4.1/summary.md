## Education in Sweden

The Swedish education system begins with preschool, which is available to children from age one whose parents are working, studying, unemployed, or on parental leave. From age three, all children are eligible for at least three hours of free preschool education daily, with additional hours incurring income-based fees. Preschool emphasizes foundational development for later schooling and the promotion of fundamental values, such as equality, through pedagogical activities like play, singing, and drawing. Compulsory education consists of nine years of "grundskola," typically for children aged 6/7 to 15/16, covering 16 core subjects and offering a non-graded student choice subject. Grading starts in year 6, using a criteria-referenced letter system from A (highest) to F (fail), with grades numerically converted for upper secondary admission. National tests in key subjects are administered in grades 3, 6, and 9 to ensure consistent assessment. Special types of schooling exist for indigenous Sámi children, students with intellectual or other disabilities, and adults (komvux). Homeschooling is only allowed for those unable to participate in regular classes due to severe illness or other reasons.

Upper secondary education ("gymnasieskola") is elective but widely attended, lasting three years and divided into 18 national programs—approximately two-thirds preparatory and one-third vocational. All students must complete a diploma project, and eligibility for these programs depends on passing core subjects. Higher education, following the Bologna Process framework, is offered at public universities and university colleges, with degrees structured in three cycles: bachelor's (first cycle), master's (second cycle), and doctoral (third cycle). Admission is based on completion of upper secondary school or equivalent, with additional language proficiency requirements for Swedish and international students. Sweden also offers higher vocational education, independent schools ("friskolor"), and measures to support equality and prevent discrimination within educational institutions. Performance in international assessments such as PISA and PIRLS has fluctuated, with recent concerns about standards, grading practices, and the impact of immigration.
