**Education in Sweden**

Sweden's education system is structured into compulsory and post-compulsory levels, with a strong emphasis on equity, inclusivity, and student-centered learning.

**Preschool (ages 1–6):**  
All children aged 1–6 are eligible for free, state-funded preschool education, with at least three hours per day. The focus is on play-based learning, social development, and early language and numeracy skills. Fees are only charged for children under three or over three years of age, based on family income.

**Comprehensive School (Grundskola, ages 6–16):**  
Mandatory for all children, the nine-year comprehensive school offers a balanced curriculum with 16 compulsory subjects, including Swedish, mathematics, physical education, English, handicrafts, music, visual arts, technology, physics, chemistry, biology, history, social studies, religion, and geography. Students take a national test in grades 3, 6, and 9 to assess progress. In grade 6, students can choose a foreign language course, with over 85% of grade 9 students studying a third language. A student's total score for university applications is calculated from subject grades (A–F), with a maximum of 340 points.

**Upper Secondary Education (Gymnasieskola, ages 16–19):**  
Students attend a three-year program divided into 18 national programs, including preparatory (leading to university) and vocational tracks. Students must pass 12 subjects (for preparatory) or 8 (for vocational), including core subjects in Swedish, English, and mathematics. A diploma project is required in all programs, focusing on planning, execution, and reflection. Students who do not meet eligibility criteria can enter an introductory program.

**Higher Education:**  
Degrees are aligned with the Bologna Process. Students can earn a Bachelor's (three years), Master's (two years), or PhD (four years). The grading system varies by institution but uses a letter-based scale (A–F). Admission to higher education is based on grades from upper secondary school. Private schools, known as "independent schools" (friskolor), are publicly funded and offer alternative pedagogies (e.g., Montessori, international). In 2023, concerns arose over potential grade inflation and inequality in independent schools, leading to an investigation by the Ministry of Education.
