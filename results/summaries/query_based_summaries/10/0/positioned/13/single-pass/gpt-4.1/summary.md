## Education in Sweden

Education in Sweden is structured around a publicly funded, comprehensive system that includes preschool (Förskola) for ages 1–6, a mandatory nine-year Grundskola (compulsory school) for ages 7–16, and optional three-year upper secondary school (Gymnasieskola) for ages 16–19, with a 99% enrollment rate. Grundskola covers 16 core subjects including Swedish, mathematics, and English, and features national tests at multiple stages. Grading uses a criterion-referenced A–F system, beginning in year 6. Upper secondary education offers 18 national programmes, split into vocational and university preparatory tracks, with admission based on comprehensive school grades. Introductory programmes are available for students not meeting eligibility requirements.

Higher education comprises universities and university colleges, organized according to the Bologna Process into bachelor's, master’s, and doctoral cycles. Admission requires completed secondary education; applicants may also use results from the Swedish Scholastic Aptitude Test (SweSAT). University grading systems vary but are convertible to the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS). Swedish and English language proficiency is required for entry. Public universities charge tuition only for non-EU/EEA/Swiss students; domestic students are supported by state loans and grants. Alongside general academic degrees, professional degrees are offered for fields like engineering and medicine. Vocational education is also available through independent institutions.

Sweden supports a unique system of publicly funded independent schools (friskolor) alongside municipal schools, with 15% of primary and 30% of upper secondary pupils enrolled in friskolor as of 2023. Both types receive funding based on municipal per-pupil allocations. All students are guaranteed equal access, and discriminatory or selective admissions are prohibited. Education is governed at the national level, with a strong emphasis on anti-discrimination policies and accessibility. Swedish students have performed near the OECD average in international assessments such as PISA, though concerns have been raised about recent trends in educational standards and equity.
