# Summary: Education in Sweden

The Swedish education system is built around a comprehensive nine-year compulsory school (grundskola) covering ages 6–16, divided into lower, middle, and upper stages. After compulsory education, most students attend a three-year elective upper secondary school (gymnasieskola), which offers both preparatory and vocational national programmes. The curriculum mandates 16 compulsory subjects including Swedish, mathematics, English, sciences, social studies, and physical education, with options for additional languages and student-chosen subjects. Students receive grades starting in year 6, using a system from A to F, influencing upper secondary admission. National standardized tests administered in grades 3, 6, and 9 aim to ensure assessment uniformity.

Higher education in Sweden follows the Bologna Process with bachelor's (180 credits), master's (60 or 120 credits), and doctoral levels. Admission generally requires completion of upper secondary school or equivalent. Universities award academic and professional degrees, with grading systems varying but compatible with European standards. Swedish students can take the SweSAT aptitude test for university admission. Higher vocational education exists separately, focusing on subject-specific, practice-oriented programs without awarding traditional degrees. Student financial aid is available via the National Board of Student Aid, with free tuition for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens.

Since the 1990s, independent (free) schools have expanded with public funding allocated per student; these schools must follow national curricula and are not allowed to charge extra fees or discriminate in admissions. Independent schools have sparked debate over religious affiliations, profit motives, and educational equality. Sweden’s PISA and PIRLS results have fluctuated, partly attributed to immigrant influx and testing irregularities.

Preschool education is available free for children aged 3 and over for at least three hours daily, with subsidized fees for additional hours. Education includes provisions for special needs, including integration and individualized support. Sweden emphasizes anti-discrimination policies and inclusive learning environments at all levels.

Overall, Sweden’s education system is marked by broad access, comprehensive curricula, multiple pathways including vocational and academic tracks, and significant public investment, balanced with ongoing debates about school types, equality, and assessment quality.
