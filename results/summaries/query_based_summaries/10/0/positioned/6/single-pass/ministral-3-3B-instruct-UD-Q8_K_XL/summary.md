Sweden’s education system is characterized by its **free, compulsory, and inclusive** approach, emphasizing both academic and practical learning. Education is divided into three main stages:

1. **Comprehensive School (Grundskola, ages 6–16):**
   - Mandatory for all children, lasting nine years, with free tuition.
   - Covers core subjects like Swedish, mathematics, English, science, and social studies, supplemented by electives such as foreign languages.
   - Grading starts in grade 6, using letters (A–E) and numerical values (E=10, D=12.5, C=15, B=17.5, A=20), with a total score calculated from 16–17 subjects.
   - National tests are administered in grades 3, 6, and 9 to assess student progress.

2. **Upper Secondary Education (Gymnasieskola, ages 16–19):**
   - Voluntary but highly enrolled (99% participation rate), lasting three years.
   - Divided into **preparatory** (e.g., social sciences, business) and **vocational** programs (e.g., engineering, healthcare).
   - Students take standardized exams (e.g., SweSAT) for university admission, with eligibility based on passing core subjects (Swedish, English, mathematics).
   - Includes a diploma project, where students complete a research-based project.

3. **Higher Education:**
   - Free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens; tuition varies for international students.
   - Degrees follow the **Bologna Process** framework: bachelor’s (180 credits), master’s (120 credits), and doctoral levels (240 credits).
   - Universities and university colleges offer diverse programs, with some specialized in professional fields (e.g., medicine, engineering).
   - Grading systems vary but include pass/fail (G/U), three-grade (VG/G/U), or seven-grade scales (A–F).

**Key Features:**
- **Free education** at all levels, funded by taxes.
- **Inclusive policies**, including support for students with disabilities and special needs.
- **Emphasis on critical thinking** over rote memorization, with reforms promoting active learning.
- **High enrollment rates** in upper secondary and tertiary education, though challenges remain in addressing inequality and improving teacher quality.

Sweden’s system is widely regarded for its **equity, accessibility, and focus on lifelong learning**, though debates persist over the effectiveness of independent schools (friskolor) and the impact of immigration on educational outcomes.
