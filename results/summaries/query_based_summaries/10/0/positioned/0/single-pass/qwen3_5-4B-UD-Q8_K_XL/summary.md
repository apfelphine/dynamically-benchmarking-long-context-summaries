Education in Sweden is characterized by a free, comprehensive, and secular system where attendance is mandatory from age six to sixteen, followed by optional upper secondary education. The structure includes preschool, compulsory primary school (grundskola) divided into three stages, and a three-year upper secondary school (gymnasieskola) with both preparatory and vocational tracks. Higher education is divided into universities, university colleges, and higher vocational schools, all operating under the Bologna Process with degrees at the bachelor's, master's, and doctoral levels. While the state funds the system, independent schools (friskolor) have been permitted since 1992, receiving public funding based on enrollment but remaining non-selective. The system emphasizes equal opportunity, though challenges such as declining PISA scores, teacher shortages, and issues regarding violence guidelines have prompted ongoing reform.

Grading in Swedish schools utilizes a letter system (A–E) for passing and failing, with an additional dash for non-graded students due to truancy. National tests are administered in primary and upper secondary levels to ensure standardized assessment, though their results are not used for grading before year six. The curriculum includes 16 core subjects, with students required to pass English, Swedish, and mathematics to proceed to secondary school. Access to higher education requires demonstrating proficiency in Swedish and English, often supplemented by the SweSAT aptitude test. Financial aid is provided by the National Board of Student Aid (CSN), with tuition free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens, while fees apply to others based on family income.

Primary and secondary education are compulsory, with public education being free. The system is highly competitive, driven by the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) and the Joint University Programmes Admissions System (JUPAS). Recent political changes have led to curriculum shifts emphasizing patriotism and national security, including new subjects like Citizenship and Social Development. Higher education is predominantly English-medium, with a mix of public and private universities, including the eight UGC-funded institutions. However, the sector faces challenges such as high tuition costs, an exodus of students and staff due to political instability, and debates over the medium of instruction between Chinese and English.

In Nigeria, the education system is structured around the 9-3-4 framework: nine years of basic education (primary and junior secondary), three years of senior secondary school, and four years of tertiary education. Primary education is free and compulsory under the Universal Basic Education (UBE) law, while secondary education is divided into state-sponsored, federal government, and private sectors. Admission to secondary schools is determined by the Common Entrance Examination (BECE), and progression to universities is based on the Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSCE) or General Certificate of Education (GCE). The tertiary sector includes universities, polytechnics, and colleges of education, with varying quality and funding levels across federal, state, and private institutions. Vocational training is also significant, though challenges persist regarding teacher qualifications and infrastructure.

Education in the Republic of Ireland is free for secondary levels since 1967 and compulsory from ages six to sixteen. The system is primarily managed by the state but heavily influenced by the Catholic Church, with the majority of schools being Catholic-run. Students progress through the Junior Cycle, Transition Year (optional), and Senior Cycle, culminating in the Leaving Certificate examination. The curriculum includes Irish as a mandatory subject, with Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools) offering education in the Irish language. Higher education is accessible at third level, with universities receiving significant government funding. The system faces ongoing efforts to address educational disadvantage through the Deis scheme, which provides resources to lower-income schools, and to increase diversity in the student body.
