## Education in Sweden

Education in Sweden is structured into several sequential stages, beginning with optional preschool (Förskola) for children ages 1–6, followed by a free preschool class (Förskoleklass) at age 6–7. Children then attend compulsory primary school (Grundskola) for nine years from ages 7–16, progressing through lower, middle, and upper stages. Upon completion, students may transition to upper secondary school (Gymnasieskola), which typically lasts three years and offers 18 national programs—divided into preparatory and vocational tracks—with a 99% enrollment rate. Admission to upper secondary programs is competitive, based on grades earned in compulsory school. Assessment includes regular grading beginning in year 6, national tests at key stages, and a merit score that determines eligibility for further education.

Higher education in Sweden encompasses bachelor's, master's, and doctoral cycles at universities and university colleges, following the Bologna Process system. Admission requires completion of upper secondary education and demonstrated proficiency in Swedish and English; some programs also require the Swedish Scholastic Aptitude Test (SweSAT). Degree structures include Higher Education Diploma (2 years), Bachelor's (3 years), Master's (1–2 years), Licentiate (2 years), and Doctoral (4 years). Grading systems vary between institutions but are standardized against European criteria. Swedish students are eligible for financial aid, and higher education is free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens.

The school system also includes publicly funded independent schools (friskolor), which educate a substantial share of pupils and operate alongside municipal schools. Debates about educational standards, equality, and grade inflation in friskolor have risen, leading to government reviews. Performance in international assessments (PISA, PIRLS) fluctuates—recent years show recovery after earlier declines, partially attributed to demographic shifts. Sweden maintains specialized schools for indigenous Sámi students and those with disabilities, as well as adult education programs. The system emphasizes non-discriminatory access, oversight against educational bias, and integration of vocational and academic opportunities at all levels.
