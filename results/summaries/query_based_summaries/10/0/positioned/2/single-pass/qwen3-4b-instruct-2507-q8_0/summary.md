# Education in Sweden

Sweden's education system is structured into primary, upper secondary, and higher education levels. Primary education, known as *grundskola*, is mandatory and spans nine years (ages 6–16), with students taking 16 compulsory subjects including Swedish, mathematics, physical education, English, handicrafts, music, visual arts, technology, physics, chemistry, biology, history, social studies, religion, geography, and home economics. Students receive grades from year 6 onward, with a grading system using A to E, where A and B are passing grades. The national curriculum includes standardized tests at grades 3, 6, and 9, which are used to ensure equitable assessment.

Upper secondary education, known as *gymnasieskola*, is elective and typically lasts three years (ages 16–19). Students can choose from 18 national programs, including preparatory and vocational tracks. The system emphasizes a diploma project, a significant academic component where students plan, execute, and reflect on a project. Students must pass 8 of the 12 subjects required for vocational programs and 12 for preparatory programs, with core subjects including English, Swedish, and mathematics. Admission to upper secondary education is based on performance in primary school grades, and students who do not qualify can attend introductory programs.

Higher education in Sweden is based on the Bologna Process, with degrees divided into bachelor's, master's, and doctoral levels. Bachelor's degrees take three years, master's degrees two years, and doctoral degrees four years. Institutions include public universities and university colleges, with a strong emphasis on research and quality. The system is free for Swedish, EU/EEA, and Swiss citizens, though students from other countries may face tuition fees. The grading system varies by institution but is generally aligned with European standards. The Swedish model includes a significant number of private schools, known as *friskolor*, which are publicly funded and follow a national curriculum, though they have faced criticism over grading practices and educational standards.

Preschool education is available to all children whose parents are working, studying, unemployed, or on parental leave, with free access to at least three hours of education daily for children aged 3–7. Preschool is offered in both public and private settings, with a focus on play-based learning and social development. The system aims to support working parents and promote equal opportunities for all children.

The Swedish education system is known for its emphasis on equity, inclusion, and student well-being. It has consistently ranked highly in international assessments such as PISA, with strong performance in reading, mathematics, and science. The system promotes active learning, group projects, and a balanced curriculum, though it has faced criticism for the high workload and the use of standardized testing. Recent reforms have focused on reducing class sizes, improving teacher training, and ensuring access to quality education for all students, including those with special needs.
