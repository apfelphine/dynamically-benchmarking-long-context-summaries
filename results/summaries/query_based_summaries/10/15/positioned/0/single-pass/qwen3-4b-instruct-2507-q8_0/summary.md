**Education in Norway**

Norway's education system is structured into three main levels: elementary (barneskole, ages 6–12), lower secondary (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16), and upper secondary (videregående skole, ages 16–19). Elementary and lower secondary education are compulsory and collectively referred to as *grunnskole*. The system has evolved from early cathedral schools in the 11th century to modern public schools, with a strong emphasis on inclusivity and equal access. 

Private schooling, introduced by the 2003 Independent Schools Act, is available but limited, requiring adherence to international curricula or alternative pedagogical/religious approaches and being heavily grant-aided. Students are not allowed to be selected based on academic ability.

Grading in Norway uses a scale from 1 to 6, with 6 being the highest and 2 the lowest passing grade. Final diplomas list teacher-assigned grades, and students may be selected for exams (*trekkfag*) to assess subject mastery. 

Upper secondary education is primarily public, with 93% of students attending public schools. The 1994 *Reform 94* merged vocational and general education paths, ensuring students can transition between them without losing credits. The *Kunnskapsløftet* reform (2006) introduced a mandatory IT integration and allowed students to choose electives, including practical ones (*arbeidslivsfag*). Students graduate with a *russ* diploma and celebrate with festivities before final exams.

Higher education is accessible through public and private institutions. Admission typically requires a *generell studiekompetanse*, achieved through general studies or a law of 23/5 for those over 23. Universities and university colleges offer degrees in arts, humanities, and sciences, with a focus on research and academic rigor. The Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (NOKUT) ensures quality standards.

Special education is provided in local schools, with the *Educational/Psychological Service* offering support to students with learning difficulties. The system emphasizes inclusive learning, with schools required to create flexible, accessible environments.

The system has seen improvements in international assessments, with Norwegian students performing well in PISA in 2009, though some areas, like mathematics, lag behind Shanghai. Criticism has been raised regarding grading practices and the perceived focus on low-achieving students.
