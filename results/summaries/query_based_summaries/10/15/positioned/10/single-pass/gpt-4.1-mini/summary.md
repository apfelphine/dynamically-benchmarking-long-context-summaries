# Summary of Education in Norway

Education in Norway has a long history dating back to 2000 B.C., with formal schools established after the Reformation in 1537. Compulsory reading was mandated from 1736, and the folkeskole (people's school) became mandatory for seven years in 1889, extended to nine years in 1969. The current system consists of three parts: elementary school (barneskole, ages 6–12), lower secondary school (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16), and upper secondary school (videregående skole, ages 16–19). Elementary and lower secondary school together form the compulsory "grunnskole." Private schools exist but are few and regulated under the Independent Schools Act of 2003.

Elementary students start learning social skills, arithmetic, language, and basics in various subjects, without official grades. From lower secondary school, grading commences, and students choose electives including foreign languages. Upper secondary education offers general (academic) and vocational tracks, with reforms since 1994 unified previous branches into a system allowing credit transfer between study paths. Public schools dominate, with over 90% attendance at upper secondary level. Graduation rates stood at 73% in 2017.

Teacher qualifications vary by level, requiring university degrees and pedagogy training. Higher education is provided mainly by public universities and university colleges offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, with some private institutions specialized in certain fields. Admission typically requires a university entrance certificate achieved through upper secondary education. Norway employs multiple grading scales at different education levels. Quality assurance is overseen by the Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (NOKUT).

The academic year runs from mid-August to June, divided into two semesters, with holidays including summer break, Christmas, and several national holidays. Special education has shifted from separate institutions to mostly integrated schooling since the 1970s, supported by specialist services. Norway allows “camp school” stays for primary and secondary pupils as part of the curriculum.

Overall, Norwegian education emphasizes accessibility, comprehensive schooling, and a broad curriculum balanced with vocational opportunities and inclusive education policies.
