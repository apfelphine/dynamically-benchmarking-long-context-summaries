**Education in Norway**

Norway's education system is structured into three main levels: elementary (barneskole, ages 6–12), lower secondary (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16), and upper secondary (videregående skole, ages 16–19). The first two levels are compulsory and free, while upper secondary education is optional. 

Elementary school begins at age 6 and includes foundational subjects such as reading, writing, mathematics, and social studies. Students are assessed without formal grades, and teachers provide informal feedback. In lower secondary school, students are graded, and their grades influence their eligibility for upper secondary school. Elective subjects are offered, including languages like German, French, and Spanish. 

Upper secondary education is divided into general and vocational paths. The general path prepares students for higher education, while the vocational path focuses on practical skills. A "2+2 model" is used in vocational studies: two years of classroom training followed by two years of apprenticeships. Students may also pursue a diploma project in their final year. 

Teachers are categorized by level: preschool teachers (førskolelærer), adjunct teachers (adjunkt), and lecturers (lektor). Higher education in Norway includes universities and university colleges, with degrees such as bachelor’s, master’s, and PhD. Admission is based on general university admissions (generell studiekompetanse), and students may re-sit exams to improve grades. 

The grading system varies by level, with a scale from 1 to 6 in primary and secondary education, and a letter-based system in higher education. Examinations are administered at the end of secondary school, and students may be selected to sit a random exam (trekkfag). 

The academic year runs from August to June, with summer vacation lasting eight weeks. National holidays include Easter, Christmas, and Constitution Day. Special education is integrated into mainstream schools, with support from educational psychologists and social welfare workers. 

Private schools are limited and must follow national curricula or offer a religious or pedagogical alternative. The system emphasizes equality, inclusivity, and high-quality teaching. Finland and Norway are recognized for their high-quality education, with Norway ranking among the top performers in international assessments.
