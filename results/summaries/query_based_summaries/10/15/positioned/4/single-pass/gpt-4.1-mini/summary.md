# Education in Norway

Education in Norway has a long history, dating back to as early as 2000 B.C., with cathedral schools established after Norway became an archdiocese in 1153. The current Norwegian school system is divided into three main parts: elementary school (barneskole, ages 6–12), lower secondary school (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16), and upper secondary school (videregående skole, ages 16–19). The first two levels, often collectively called grunnskole, are compulsory. Education reforms over time, such as the introduction of folkeskole and later grunnskole, expanded mandatory schooling. Private schools under the Independent Schools Act of 2003 are rare and must offer internationally recognized curricula or alternative religious/pedagogical approaches.

In elementary school, children focus initially on play and basic skills such as social structure, language, and arithmetic, progressing to formal subjects including arts, science, and languages, with no official grades given. Lower secondary school introduces formal grading, electives in languages or practical subjects, and affects admission to upper secondary schools. Upper secondary education offers general and vocational paths, including a 2+2 apprenticeship model for vocational studies. Public schools predominate, with few private upper secondary schools. Graduation rates for videregående skole stand at around 73% as of 2017. Teachers’ qualifications vary by level, including preschool teachers, adjunct teachers (bachelor's degree plus pedagogy), and lecturers (master’s degree plus pedagogy). Higher education consists of universities and university colleges offering bachelor through doctoral programs, supervised by the Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (NOKUT).

Special education emphasizes inclusive, local schooling, with special education provided mainly within ordinary schools, supported by specialized services such as educational psychologists. Norway employs multiple grading systems: universities use a letter scale compatible with the Bologna system, while lower education uses numeric grades from 1 to 6, with 2 as the lowest passing grade. Final examinations in upper secondary school combine teacher-assigned grades with examination grades in randomly selected subjects. The academic year is divided into two semesters from August to December and January to June, with several national holidays and extended summer vacation.
