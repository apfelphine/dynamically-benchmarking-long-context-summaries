**Education in Norway**

Norway's education system is structured into three main levels: elementary (barneskole, ages 6–12), lower secondary (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16), and upper secondary (videregående skole, ages 16–19). Compulsory education spans from age 6 to 16, with a strong emphasis on inclusivity and student well-being.

- **Primary School (barneskole)**: Begins at age 6 and lasts until age 12. Students engage in play-based learning in the early years, gradually transitioning to structured subjects like mathematics, English, science, religion, and arts. No formal grades are given, though teachers provide comments and informal assessments.
- **Lower Secondary School (ungdomsskole)**: Begins at age 13 and lasts until age 16. Students are graded, and their grades—along with their location—determine eligibility for upper secondary school. Electives are offered in languages (e.g., German, French, Spanish) and practical subjects (e.g., career studies).
- **Upper Secondary School (videregående skole)**: Lasts from ages 16 to 19. Students choose between general or vocational tracks. The vocational path follows a "2+2 model": two years of classroom training and two years of apprenticeship. General studies emphasize theory and broader academic development. Students must pass all subjects to graduate.

The education system is highly centralized, with the Ministry of Education overseeing policy, curriculum, and teacher training. Teachers are categorized by level of qualification:
- **Preschool teachers** (førskolelærer): Bachelor’s degree holders.
- **Adjunct teachers** (adjunkt): Bachelor’s degree holders in a subject, with pedagogy training.
- **Lecturers** (lektor): Master’s degree holders with pedagogy training.

Higher education in Norway is accessible through public universities and university colleges, with a strong focus on research and quality. The Bologna Process has standardized degrees into three cycles (bachelor’s, master’s, doctorate), and the grading system uses a scale from 1 to 6, with 4 being the minimum passing grade.

Norway emphasizes equity and inclusion, with special education provided in mainstream schools. The government supports inclusive practices and ensures access for students with disabilities through specialized services. The system also promotes student well-being, with a focus on mental health and social development.

Key reforms, such as the "Kunnskapsløftet" (Knowledge Promise) introduced in 2006, have emphasized broader, more balanced curricula and reduced flexibility in elective choices. The country maintains high international performance in reading and mathematics, though some areas, like mathematics, lag behind top-performing nations.
