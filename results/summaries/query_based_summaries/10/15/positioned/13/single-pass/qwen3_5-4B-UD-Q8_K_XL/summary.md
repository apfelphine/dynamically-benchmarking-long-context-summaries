Based strictly on the provided text, education in Norway dates back to cathedral schools established in 1153, which were later converted to Latin schools after the 1537 Reformation. The system is now organized into three main levels: elementary school (*barneskole*, ages 6–12), lower secondary school (*ungdomsskole*, ages 13–16), and upper secondary school (*videregående skole*, ages 16–19). Primary and lower secondary education are compulsory, while upper secondary education is elective but has a 99% enrollment rate. The *videregående skole* was reformed in 1994 to merge general, mercantile, and vocational branches into a single system, and further reforms in 2006 (Kunnskapsløftet) allow students to choose between general studies (*studieforberedelse*) and vocational studies (*yrkesfag*). Vocational tracks often follow a "2+2 model," combining school training with apprenticeships.

Higher education in Norway is largely state-run and divided into universities (focusing on theoretical subjects) and university colleges (*høgskole*), which offer professional degrees. Admission generally requires a general university admissions certificate, and applicants for competitive fields like medicine or engineering may need to resit upper secondary exams to improve their grades. The Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (NOKUT) oversees quality assurance. Grading systems vary; compulsory and upper secondary levels use a scale from 1 to 6, while university levels historically used a 1.0 to 6.0 scale, though the Bologna process has introduced European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS) credits. Private schooling exists under the Independent Schools Act of 2003 but remains limited compared to public schools.
