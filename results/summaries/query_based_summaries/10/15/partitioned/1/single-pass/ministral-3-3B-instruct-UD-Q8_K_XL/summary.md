Education in Norway is structured into three main stages: **primary school (barneskole, grades 1–7, ages 6–12)**, **lower secondary school (ungdomsskole, grades 8–10, ages 13–16)**, and **upper secondary school (videregående skole, grades VG1–VG3, ages 16–19)**.

**Primary school** focuses on foundational skills in reading, writing, mathematics, and basic English, with no formal grading until the start of lower secondary school. The curriculum emphasizes social development and basic academic preparation.

**Lower secondary school** introduces grading and elective subjects, including foreign languages (German, French, or Spanish) and career-oriented electives. Students can take the grade 10 exam early if exempted from certain subjects. Norway’s fifteen-year-olds performed well in OECD’s PISA assessments in 2009, though disparities exist, particularly in vocational and theoretical tracks.

**Upper secondary school** is the final stage, offering a mix of general and vocational studies. The system, reformed in 1994, unifies previously separate tracks into a single curriculum, allowing flexibility in switching paths. Students can choose between general studies (studieforberedelse) or vocational studies (yrkesfag), with vocational programs often following a "2+2" model (two years of school training and two years of apprenticeship). Graduates are called *russ* and often celebrate with parties before final exams. Private schools are rare but exist, focusing on popular subjects like business management or fine arts.

**Higher education** is accessible beyond upper secondary school, requiring a general university admissions certificate (generell studiekompetanse). Institutions include universities (theoretical studies) and university colleges (vocational and professional degrees). Private schools account for 10% of higher education students. Admission is competitive, with many students re-sitting exams to improve grades for prestigious fields like medicine, law, and engineering. Norway’s higher education system is state-funded, ensuring quality and accessibility, though private schools cater to niche or high-demand fields.

**Special education** and **special needs** are addressed through inclusive policies, with specialized support available for students with disabilities. Grading systems vary, with upper secondary grades ranging from 1 (lowest) to 6 (highest), and universities adopting the Bologna system for international comparability. Norway emphasizes lifelong learning and vocational training, integrating technology and practical skills into curricula.
