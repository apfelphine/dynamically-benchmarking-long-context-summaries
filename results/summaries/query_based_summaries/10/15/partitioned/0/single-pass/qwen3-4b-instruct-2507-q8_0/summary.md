**Education in Norway**

Norway's education system is structured into three main stages: elementary (barneskole, ages 6–12), lower secondary (ungdomsskole, ages 13–16), and upper secondary (videregående skole, ages 16–19). Elementary and lower secondary education are compulsory, collectively referred to as *grunnskole*. 

The history of education in Norway dates back to 2000 B.C., with early cathedral schools established after the formation of the archdiocese in 1153. Following the Reformation in 1537, these schools transitioned to Latin schools. In 1736, reading instruction became mandatory, and by 1827, the *folkeskole* (people's school) was introduced, becoming mandatory for seven years by 1889 and nine years by 1969. The *grunnskole* system replaced the earlier *folkeskole* in the 1970s and 1980s.

In upper secondary education, the 1994 reform (*Reform 94*) merged the previous general, mercantile, and vocational tracks into a single system, ensuring students gain a balanced foundation in general studies and allowing for flexibility in switching between paths. A "2+2 model" is common in vocational studies: two years of school-based training followed by a two-year apprenticeship. Since 2006, the *Kunnskapsløftet* ("Knowledge Promise") has mandated IT integration and reduced flexibility in elective choices.

Private education is limited. Prior to 2005, private schools were only allowed if they offered religious or pedagogical alternatives. The first standard private upper secondary schools opened in 2005. 

Grading in Norway uses a scale from 1 to 6, with 4 or above considered passing. In upper secondary school, students earn a diploma upon passing all subjects, with optional exams (*trekkfag*) for selected subjects. 

Higher education is accessible through universities and university colleges. Universities focus on theoretical subjects and offer bachelor’s, master’s, and PhD degrees. University colleges provide a broader range of programs, including professional degrees. Admission requires a *general university admissions certificate* (generell studiekompetanse), typically achieved through upper secondary school or via a 23+ years-old law. 

The Norwegian Agency for Quality Assurance in Education (NOKUT) oversees quality standards. Higher education is broadly divided into universities, university colleges, and private institutions (10% of higher education students). There is no formal distinction between vocational and non-vocational programs. 

Special education is integrated into mainstream schools, with support from the Educational/Psychological Service. Students with special needs receive tailored instruction, and schools are required to create inclusive learning environments. 

The academic year runs from mid-August to mid-June, with breaks in October, December, and February. National holidays include Easter, May 1 (Workers’ Day), and Constitution Day (May 17). 

Overall, Norway emphasizes equity, inclusion, and high-quality education, with strong public funding and a focus on student well-being and comprehensive learning.
