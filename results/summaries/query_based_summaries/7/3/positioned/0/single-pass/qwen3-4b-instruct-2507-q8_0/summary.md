**Education in Germany**  

Germany's education system is decentralized, with each of the 16 federal states (Länder) responsible for its own policies, including curriculum and school organization. The system is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a strong emphasis on school choice and academic tracking.  

At the secondary level, students are streamed into five types of schools:  
- **Gymnasium** (academic, leading to the *Abitur* for university entry)  
- **Realschule** (intermediate, leading to *Mittlere Reife*)  
- **Hauptschule** (vocational, leading to *Hauptschulabschluss*)  
- **Gesamtschule** (comprehensive, offering multiple pathways)  
- **Förderschule** (special education for students with learning or developmental needs)  

A key feature is the **dual education system** (*Duale Ausbildung*), which combines vocational training in a company with classroom instruction at a state school, widely used for skilled trades. Students in vocational schools earn a *Berufsschulabschluss* and may later pursue a university-level qualification.  

Primary education is compulsory until age 16, with a wide range of school types including state, Waldorf, Montessori, and religious schools. Students typically begin school at age 6, with a transition phase (Orientierungsstufe) at age 10–11 to determine future school paths.  

The system emphasizes foreign language education, with English typically taught from primary school onward. The *Abitur* is the primary qualification for university admission, and students are assessed through a combination of exams and grades.  

Tertiary education includes public universities and *Fachhochschulen* (applied sciences), with a strong focus on research and practical training. The *Abitur* is required for university admission, and students may also pursue *Staatsexamen* (state exams) for professional careers like law or medicine.  

Performance in international assessments like PISA has shown mixed results, with German students scoring above the OECD average in reading, mathematics, and science, though performance has declined in recent years. Social class significantly influences educational outcomes, with children from lower-income backgrounds facing greater challenges.  

The system includes strong student participation through student councils (*Schülerrat*), school uniforms are not required, and extracurricular activities are common. The government supports education through funding and policy, including initiatives to promote inclusion and digital access.  

Despite its strengths, the system faces criticism for rigid tracking, socioeconomic disparities, and high student stress, particularly in the transition to secondary school.
