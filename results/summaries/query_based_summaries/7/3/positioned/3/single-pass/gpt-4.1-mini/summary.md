## Education in Germany

Germany’s secondary education is divided into lower and upper levels, featuring several school types: Gymnasium (prepares for university, culminating in the Abitur exam after grade 12 or 13), Realschule (intermediate pupils, ends with Mittlere Reife after grade 10), Hauptschule (prepares for vocational education, ends after grade 9), Gesamtschule (comprehensive schools), and special schools for students with special needs (Förderschule). Recent reforms shifted some Gymnasiums back to a 9-year Abitur track due to challenges of the earlier 8-year G8 model. After secondary education, students often enter apprenticeships combining practical training and vocational schooling in the "dual education system."

Germany’s education system is state-administered, with each of its 16 states (Länder) managing curricula, school organization, and teacher employment, resulting in variations in starting ages and foreign language instruction. Preschool (Kindergarten or Kita) is voluntary and not part of the school system. Compulsory education begins with primary school (Grundschule) and continues to secondary schooling. Private schools exist but must meet public standards and most receive public subsidies; about 9% of students attend private schools. Homeschooling is illegal due to children's rights considerations.  

Tertiary education includes universities (Universitäten), which confer doctorates, and universities of applied sciences (Fachhochschulen) focusing on practical application. Admission typically requires the Abitur or similar qualifications, with alternative pathways available. Public universities charge minimal or no tuition fees, though students pay semester contributions. Germany emphasizes academic freedom and mobility across institutions. The dual education system remains a respected pathway alongside university education.

Academic achievement in Germany is strongly influenced by social class and immigrant background, with children from wealthier and academic families more likely to attend Gymnasium. Gender differences exist, with males having higher dropout rates and emotional disturbance classifications. Efforts exist to support at-risk children through early education and family support programs.

Germany consistently scores near or above OECD averages in PISA assessments, though results show socio-economic disparities in educational outcomes. Reform efforts focus on balancing streaming by ability with educational equity, improving infrastructure, and integrating digital technologies. International students comprise roughly 15% of the student population.
