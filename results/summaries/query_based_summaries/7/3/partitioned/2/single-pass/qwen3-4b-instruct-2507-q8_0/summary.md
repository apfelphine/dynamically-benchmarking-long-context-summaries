**Education in Germany**  

Germany's education system is decentralized, with each state (Länder) responsible for its own schools and curriculum, reflecting constitutional autonomy. Secondary education is structured into five types:  
- **Gymnasium**: Prepares students for university, culminating in the *Abitur* after grades 10–13 (G9 model since 2019).  
- **Realschule**: Focuses on practical skills, ending with the *Mittlere Reife* after grade 10.  
- **Hauptschule**: Prepares students for vocational training, ending with the *Hauptschulabschluss* after grade 9.  
- **Gesamtschule**: A comprehensive school offering a blended path, with options for *Hauptschulabschluss* or *Abitur* after 5–8 years.  
- **Sonderschule**: For students with special educational needs, with some pathways leading to standard qualifications.  

A key feature is the **dual education system** (*Duale Ausbildung*), combining classroom instruction with paid apprenticeships in businesses, valued across trades. This system is a model for vocational training globally.  

Primary education begins at age 6–7, with a range of school types including state schools, Waldorf, Montessori, and religious schools. Preschool (*Kindergarten* or *Kita*) is optional and not mandatory, with no tuition fees for state schools.  

Education is highly competitive, with strong performance in PISA 2018 (reading, math, science), though recent results show a decline in math and science. Social class significantly impacts educational outcomes, with students from affluent families more likely to attend Gymnasium.  

The system emphasizes academic freedom, teacher autonomy, and research, rooted in Wilhelm von Humboldt’s model. Higher education includes bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, with a strong focus on research.  

Tuition fees are generally absent at public universities, with students paying modest semester fees for services like transportation and student activities. International students face challenges in securing funding, with limited scholarships.  

The system faces criticism for social inequality, regional disparities, and rigid tracking by ability, though reforms like inclusion and reduced class sizes aim to address these issues. Overall, Germany's system balances academic rigor with practical vocational training, fostering both research excellence and workforce readiness.
