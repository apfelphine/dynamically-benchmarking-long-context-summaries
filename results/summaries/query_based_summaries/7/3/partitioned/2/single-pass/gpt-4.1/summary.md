## Education in Germany

Education in Germany is largely the responsibility of the federal states, resulting in varied systems across the country while maintaining some nationwide standards. The school system is split into primary education (Grundschule) and several forms of secondary education: Hauptschule, Realschule, Gymnasium, Gesamtschule, and Förderschule (special school). After primary school, students are streamed based on ability, with Gymnasium leading to the Abitur (university entrance qualification), Realschule to Mittlere Reife (intermediate leaving certificate), and Hauptschule to the Hauptschulabschluss (basic secondary school certificate). The dual education system (Duale Ausbildung) is notable for combining vocational training in companies and part-time vocational school, preparing students for a wide range of professions.

In secondary schools, foreign language learning is compulsory, most often English, but options also include French, Latin, Russian, Spanish, and others. The school day generally starts early and may end before lunch, though older students have afternoon lessons. Private schools make up 9.2% of student enrollment and are government regulated. Special schools provide tailored education for children with disabilities, while international schools and the International Baccalaureate are available, particularly for expatriate families. Performance on international benchmarks such as PISA has driven reforms, although social class and immigrant status still heavily influence academic outcomes. Higher education is dominated by public universities, which typically charge low or no tuition, and distinguished research institutions (e.g., Max Planck, Fraunhofer). Admission to universities requires the Abitur or an equivalent, and degrees are in line with the Bologna process (Bachelor, Master, doctorate), with specific professional tracks like Staatsexamen for medicine and law. Academic freedom is valued, but debates continue regarding equity, digitalization, and streaming by ability.
