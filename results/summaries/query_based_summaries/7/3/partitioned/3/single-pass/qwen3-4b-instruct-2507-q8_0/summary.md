**Education in Germany**

Germany's education system is decentralized, with each of the 16 federal states managing its own school system. Secondary education is divided into five types: Gymnasium (preparing for university), Realschule, Hauptschule, Gesamtschule (comprehensive), and Sonderschule (special needs). Students typically attend Gymnasium from ages 10–18, culminating in the Abitur, which qualifies them for university. Realschule and Hauptschule lead to the Mittlere Reife or Hauptschulabschluss, often preparing students for vocational training.

The system emphasizes academic freedom and research, rooted in Wilhelm von Humboldt’s model, with universities combining teaching and research. Students choose subjects freely and earn credits in core fields, with no fixed class sizes. The Bologna Process introduced bachelor’s and master’s degrees (3 years and 2 years respectively), replacing older Diplom and Magister degrees. A student may also pursue the Staatsexamen (required for professions like law, medicine, or teaching), which is equivalent to a master’s degree.

Tertiary education is strong, with Germany ranking among the world's top universities. Public universities do not charge tuition, though students pay a semester fee (€150–€350) for administration, transport, and student services. International students can access scholarships, though competition is high. The dual education system (Duale Ausbildung) combines classroom learning with workplace training, making vocational training accessible and highly valued.

The system faces challenges, including a strong socio-economic gap in student performance, with PISA results showing significant disparities. While students in Gymnasium outperform those in other schools, those in Hauptschule and Realschule perform more poorly. The government has introduced reforms to reduce academic pressure and promote equity, including efforts to improve school facilities and reduce teacher absenteeism. Despite its strengths, Germany's education system continues to be scrutinized for its rigid tracking and socio-economic inequalities.
