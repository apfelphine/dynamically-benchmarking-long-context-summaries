**Education in Germany** is decentralized, with each of the 16 federal states (*Länder*) responsible for its own school system, leading to variations in curriculum and administration. The secondary education system is primarily divided into three tracks: the *Gymnasium* (academic high school leading to the *Abitur* and university entrance), the *Realschule* (intermediate track leading to *Mittlere Reife*), and the *Hauptschule* (vocational track leading to *Hauptschulabschluss*). Additionally, *Gesamtschulen* (comprehensive schools) combine these pathways, while special schools (*Förderschulen*) serve students with disabilities. A unique feature of the German system is the strong emphasis on the *duale Ausbildung* (dual vocational training), where apprentices alternate between theoretical instruction at a *Berufsschule* and practical training in a company, making vocational qualifications highly valued and often preferred over academic degrees for certain professions.

Tertiary education in Germany is characterized by its high quality and relatively low cost; public universities and colleges (*Fachhochschulen*) generally do not charge tuition fees for domestic students, though semester contributions (*Semesterbeitrag*) cover administrative costs and student services. The system distinguishes between *Universitäten* (which confer doctorates) and *Fachhochschulen* (Universities of Applied Sciences, focused on practical skills and employability). While the system is internationally renowned, international comparisons like PISA have historically highlighted disparities in performance based on socio-economic background and school type, prompting ongoing reforms. Admission to universities typically requires the *Abitur*, though alternative pathways such as *Studienkollegs* exist for those with vocational qualifications.
