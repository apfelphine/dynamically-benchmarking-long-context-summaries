**Education in Germany**

Germany's secondary education system is divided into lower and upper levels, with the lower level providing basic general education and preparing students for upper secondary education. The system includes five types of schools: Gymnasium (leading to the Abitur for university entrance), Realschule (leading to Mittlere Reife), Hauptschule (leading to Hauptschulabschluss), Gesamtschule (a comprehensive school), and Förderschulen (special schools for students with special needs). 

The Gymnasium typically lasts 8–13 years, culminating in the Abitur, while Realschule and Hauptschule last 10–12 years, ending with Mittlere Reife or Hauptschulabschluss. Students may transition between schools, with an orientation phase in grades 5–6 to determine future paths. A unique feature is the dual education system (Duale Ausbildung), where vocational training combines classroom learning with practical work in companies.

Primary education includes state schools, Waldorf, Montessori, and religious schools, with children typically entering at age 6. Preschool (Kindergarten) is optional and not part of the formal system. 

The education system is decentralized, with each of Germany’s 16 states managing its own curriculum and policies. Schools follow a structured timetable with 45-minute lessons, and students study core subjects like foreign languages, mathematics, and sciences. Exams are essay-based, and students in Gymnasium study a broader range of languages and subjects.

Germany's education system has been evaluated internationally, with PISA 2018 showing above-average performance in reading, mathematics, and science, though results declined in science compared to earlier years. The system emphasizes practical skills and vocational training, with strong support for apprenticeships and dual education. 

Tertiary education includes public universities and Fachhochschulen (Universities of Applied Sciences), with no tuition fees at public institutions. Admission typically requires the Abitur or equivalent, with some exceptions for students with vocational training or aptitude tests. The German model of combining academic and practical training is globally recognized and widely respected.
