The education systems in Greece, India, and China are distinct in their governance, structure, and historical development, each reflecting unique national priorities and challenges. In **Greece**, education is centralized under the Ministry of Education, featuring a three-tier system: compulsory six-year primary school (*Dimotiko*) followed by three-year lower secondary (*Gymnasio*) and three-year upper secondary (*Lykeio*). The system emphasizes a rigorous academic track leading to the *Apo lytirio Lykeiou*, which serves as a prerequisite for university admission, alongside a growing vocational sector (*EPALE*) and diverse special schools for specific talents or needs. Public universities are largely tuition-free, though recent reforms have introduced fees for certain programs and long-time students, while corruption and bullying remain significant societal concerns affecting educational quality.

**India** operates under a concurrent list framework where both the central and state governments share responsibility for policy and implementation, leading to vast variations across the country. The system is characterized by a strong emphasis on public schools, though private sector growth is accelerating, particularly in urban areas and English-medium education. Despite high enrollment rates driven by acts like the Right to Education (RTE) 2009, the system faces severe challenges regarding quality, teacher absenteeism, and infrastructure. Higher education is dominated by public institutions like the IITs, which are globally renowned, but the sector also suffers from a proliferation of unaccredited private colleges and a significant skills gap between engineering graduates and industry demands.

In **China**, the education system is heavily centralized and politically guided by the Communist Party, with a focus on national modernization and economic development. A nine-year compulsory education system is universal, but access to higher education remains highly competitive, driven by the *Gaokao* (college entrance exam). The system has achieved near-universal primary and secondary enrollment and ranks highly in international assessments like PISA, particularly in science and mathematics. However, it is increasingly criticized for its intense pressure on students, rote learning methods, and a widening gap between urban and rural educational opportunities. Recent policies aim to reduce the burden on students through the "Double Reduction" policy and to expand vocational training, yet the dominance of the examination system continues to shape educational outcomes and social mobility.
