China’s education system is structured around **nine years of compulsory education**, starting from primary school (grades 1–6) and extending to junior high school (grades 7–9), followed by optional senior high school (grades 10–12). The system emphasizes **universal access** to basic education, with significant government investment to ensure all children receive at least six years of primary schooling. Compulsory education is governed by the **Law on Nine-Year Compulsory Education**, introduced in 1986, mandating free education and subsidies for students from low-income families.

**Primary Education (Grades 1–6)** focuses on foundational skills in Chinese, mathematics, science, and social studies, supplemented by physical education, music, and art. English is introduced in the third grade. The curriculum integrates ideological and political studies, emphasizing communist values and national identity. **Secondary Education** includes junior high school (grades 7–9) and senior high school (grades 10–12), with the latter offering specialized tracks for university preparation. Students take the **Gaokao** (college entrance examination) after grade 12, a highly competitive test determining admission to universities or vocational schools.

China’s education system has undergone reforms to address regional disparities, improve teacher quality, and reduce reliance on rote memorization. The government has implemented policies such as the **"Two Exemptions and One Subsidy"** plan to support rural students and the **"Double First-Class Construction"** initiative to elevate elite universities to global standards. Despite advancements, challenges persist, including **regional inequality**, **intense academic pressure**, and **discrimination against marginalized groups**, such as rural students and children of migrant workers. The system also faces criticism for its emphasis on standardized testing and limited exposure to English outside of school.
