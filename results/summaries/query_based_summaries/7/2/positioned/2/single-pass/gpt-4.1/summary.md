## Education in China – Summary

Education in China is characterized by a strong government role, widespread compulsory schooling, and an emphasis on academic rigor. Since the 1950s, China has prioritized literacy and developed a nine-year compulsory education system covering primary (6 years) and junior secondary (3 years) schooling, with the Law on Nine-Year Compulsory Education coming into effect in 1986. While urban and developed regions typically achieve full coverage, some rural areas continue to struggle with access, funding, and qualified teachers, and regional inequalities persist. The curriculum places great importance on core subjects such as Chinese, mathematics, and politics, with political education and patriotism being central to all levels. Moral, ideological, and Marxist teachings are required throughout primary, secondary, and tertiary education. Vocational and technical schools operate alongside academic senior secondary schools, but a perceived hierarchy favors academic pathways leading to university.

China's higher education sector boasts the world’s largest enrollment, surpassed only by the United States in global reputation. The competitive Gaokao (college entrance exam) determines access to universities, which have expanded and diversified since the late 20th century, partly through initiatives like the Double First-Class plan to build elite universities. Investment in education has increased, with additional measures for scholarships and student loans. Private and international schools exist but are subject to strict regulation and recent centralization policies. Continuing education and adult learning are also strongly promoted, and recent years have seen a surge in online education and ICT integration. Despite high literacy rates and outstanding performance in international assessments from select regions, challenges remain: intense student stress, emphasis on rote memorization over creativity, significant regional socioeconomic and gender disparities, academic censorship, and remaining barriers for rural and disabled students. English education is widespread, but often criticized for its focus on testing rather than communication.
