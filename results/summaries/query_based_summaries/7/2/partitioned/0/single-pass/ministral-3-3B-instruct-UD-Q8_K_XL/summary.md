China’s education system has undergone significant evolution since the founding of the People’s Republic in 1949, with a primary focus on improving literacy and expanding access to education. Early efforts emphasized universal literacy and expanded enrollment in primary, secondary, and higher education, with literacy rates rising from 20–40% in 1949 to near-universal access by the late 20th century. The **Law on Nine-Year Compulsory Education (1986)** established a structured nine-year compulsory education system, integrating primary and junior high school into a unified "Nine Years – One Policy" framework, ensuring all children aged 6–15 receive education.

Post-Cultural Revolution reforms (1976 onward) prioritized economic modernization, shifting education toward vocational and technical skills to align with China’s modernization goals. The **State Education Commission (1985)** decentralized educational management, allowing local governments to allocate resources more effectively. By 1999, primary education had become nearly universal, with 90% of children enrolled, though rural areas faced challenges due to limited resources. The system includes **pre-school (3–5 years)**, **nine-year compulsory education (6–15 years)**, and **standard senior high school education**, with specialized vocational training integrated into secondary education. Higher education expanded dramatically, with over **3,000 colleges and universities** and **44.3 million students** enrolled by 2021, achieving a **gross enrollment rate of 58.42%** in 2020.

Key reforms include the **Double First-Class Construction Initiative (2015)**, aiming to develop elite universities into global leaders by 2050, and the **Double Reduction Policy (2021)**, which reduced excessive off-campus tutoring and homework burdens to promote balanced development. The government has also invested in **digital education**, leveraging technology to enhance learning, and promoted **international cooperation**, such as partnerships with foreign universities. However, challenges persist, including **regional inequality**, **intense academic pressure**, and **vocational training gaps**, with debates ongoing about the system’s emphasis on memorization, standardized testing, and innovation.
