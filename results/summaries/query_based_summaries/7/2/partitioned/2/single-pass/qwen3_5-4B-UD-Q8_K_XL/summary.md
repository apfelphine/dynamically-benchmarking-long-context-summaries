**Education in China**

China's education system is centrally directed by the Communist Party and the government to support national modernization and economic development. Historically, the system prioritized political ideology, but since the late 1970s, reforms have shifted focus toward scientific and technical training while maintaining the dominance of rote memorization and standardized testing. The **Gaokao** (college entrance examination) remains the cornerstone of higher education access, featuring intense competition and strict national quotas that favor urban students over rural ones, often resulting in significant regional inequality. While the government has expanded access through the **Nine-Year Compulsory Education Law**, challenges persist regarding the quality of rural education, teacher shortages in impoverished areas, and the "brain drain" of elite students studying abroad.

The system is characterized by a rigid tracking mechanism and a heavy emphasis on academic achievement over creativity. Vocational and technical education has historically been undervalued, though recent reforms aim to increase its relevance for employment. Private tutoring has long been a widespread phenomenon, but the government has increasingly cracked down on off-campus training and private schools to promote "common prosperity" and reduce the educational gap between wealthy and poor families. Additionally, the curriculum is strictly controlled to align with state ideology, including mandatory courses in Marxism and the Chinese language, while recent policies under President Xi Jinping have also introduced initiatives to reduce homework burdens and promote entrepreneurship among students.
