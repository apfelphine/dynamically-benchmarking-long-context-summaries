## Education in China: Key Features and Developments

Education in China is characterized by a compulsory nine-year system—six years of primary school followed by three years of junior secondary—established by the 1986 Law on Nine-Year Compulsory Education. Enrollment rates for primary and junior high school are very high at 98.9% and 94.1%, respectively. While cities and developed regions have largely achieved universal nine-year education, rural and disadvantaged areas face persistent challenges in resources, teacher quality, and completion rates. Special education for students with disabilities has expanded, though it remains a low priority compared to mainstream schooling. The curriculum emphasizes Chinese, mathematics, and political/moral education, with increasing integration of Communist Party history from kindergarten onward. English is introduced as a foreign language in primary schools.

Secondary education comprises both academic and vocational tracks, with strong competition for university entrance via the "gaokao" exam. Vocational education is promoted as a means to meet workforce needs, yet it is often less popular due to perceptions of limited upward mobility. Higher education in China has expanded rapidly, with over 3,000 colleges and universities and increasing international prestige, particularly among elite "Double First-Class" institutions. Admission to higher education relies on rigorous entrance exams, with policies that offer partial support to disadvantaged groups and growing opportunities for distance and online learning.

## Reform, Inequality, and Current Challenges

Since the 1980s, educational governance has shifted towards greater local autonomy, with investment rising steadily. Major reforms have aimed to modernize education, strengthen science and technology, improve teacher qualifications, and diversify enrollment pathways. Despite China’s strong performance in international assessments, critics highlight issues such as rote memorization, pressure stemming from standardized tests, regional and gender inequalities, and the urban-rural education gap. Supplemental tutoring is widespread yet recently regulated to reduce stress and inequality. Private, international, and online education sectors have grown, expanding options for families and foreign students. The system faces ongoing scrutiny regarding academic censorship, quality disparities, food safety in schools, and equitable access for migrant, disabled, and ethnic minority children. Integration of digital and distance education is a priority in national policy, with significant investments in ICT to support lifelong learning.
