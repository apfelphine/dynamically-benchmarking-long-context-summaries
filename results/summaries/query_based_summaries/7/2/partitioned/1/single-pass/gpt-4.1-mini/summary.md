# Summary of Education in China

China's education system has evolved significantly since 1949, focusing initially on improving literacy and expanding access at all levels. The Law on Nine-Year Compulsory Education (enacted in 1986) mandates at least nine years of education—six years of primary and three years of junior secondary education—tailored to local conditions, aiming to equalize urban and rural educational opportunities. Primary education is tuition-free and emphasizes subjects including Chinese, mathematics, morality, and, increasingly, political studies aligned with Communist Party values. Though urban schools operate on standard schedules, rural schools adapt to agricultural cycles, often with limited curricula and flexible attendance. Senior secondary education (high school) is not compulsory; students may attend academic or vocational schools, with admission often influenced by entrance exams like Zhongkao. Vocational and technical education expanded rapidly since the 1980s to meet economic demands, though quality and public perception remain challenges. Private education has grown, including international cooperations, but is now subject to stricter government control. Supplemental tutoring was widespread but faced heavy regulation starting in 2021 to reduce inequity and academic pressure.

Higher education in China has undergone reforms since the 1980s to increase autonomy, diversify enrolment methods, and improve quality, with considerable government investment and initiatives like the Double First-Class Project aiming to build world-class universities. Competitive national entrance examinations (Gaokao) remain central, though some universities exercise discretionary admissions. China hosts over 3,000 universities and is highly ranked globally in various university league tables. Adult education, including distance learning via television and radio, aims to extend educational access to workers and peasants. Information and communications technology (ICT) is a key focus for modernizing education infrastructure and pedagogy. The government emphasizes ideological education alongside academic content, with increased integration of Marxism in curricula. Persistent issues include intense competition, regional inequality (notably rural-urban disparities), gender gaps, food safety concerns, and strict state control over curricula and academic freedom through textbook censorship and ideological oversight. English education starts typically at age 10, but faces criticism for rote teaching methods and limited practical usage. Children of migrant workers often face barriers to access public urban schools, resulting in segregated private schooling with variable quality.
