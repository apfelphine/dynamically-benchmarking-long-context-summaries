Garry Kasparov, born **Garik Kimovich Weinstein**, was a Soviet and Russian chess prodigy who rose to become one of the greatest chess players in history. Born in Baku, Azerbaijan, in 1963, he adopted the surname **Kasparov** at age 12 after his mother’s request. His early training under Mikhail Botvinnik and Vladimir Makogonov led to rapid success, including wins in the Soviet Junior Championship at ages 13 and 14.

Kasparov’s chess career was defined by his dominance in the 1980s and early 1990s, marked by five world championship matches against Anatoly Karpov. His 1985 victory over Karpov at age 22 made him the youngest world champion in history, a record he held for over two decades. Kasparov’s playing style was dynamic, aggressive, and characterized by deep strategic understanding and tactical brilliance, often compared to the attacking style of Alekhine. His contributions to opening theory, including innovations like the **Kasparov Gambit** in the Sicilian Defense, further cemented his legacy.

Beyond chess, Kasparov became a vocal political figure, particularly in Russia. Initially a member of the Communist Party, he later became a prominent critic of Vladimir Putin’s regime, advocating for democratic reforms and opposing authoritarianism. His activism led to persecution, including arrests, beatings, and accusations of extremism. In 2013, he fled Russia, settling in the U.S., where he continued his political and chess-related advocacy, including founding the Kasparov Chess Foundation to promote chess education globally.

Kasparov’s intellectual influence extends beyond chess and politics, with his writings on decision-making, economics, and philosophy reflecting his lifelong pursuit of knowledge and his belief in the power of critical thinking. Though he retired from competitive chess in 2005, his impact on the game and his unwavering commitment to freedom and democracy ensure his enduring legacy.
