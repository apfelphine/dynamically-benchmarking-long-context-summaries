Garry Kasparov, born **Garik Kimovich Weinstein** in Baku, Azerbaijan, was a prodigious chess prodigy who adopted his mother’s surname, **Kasparov**, at age 12. His early training under Mikhail Botvinnik and Vladimir Makogonov laid the foundation for his rapid ascent in chess. By age 13, he won the Soviet Junior Championship, and at 15, he became the youngest-ever participant in the USSR Chess Championship.

Kasparov’s chess career was defined by his rivalry with Anatoly Karpov, culminating in five world championship matches (1984–1990). His 1985 victory at age 22 marked the youngest world championship in history, though his 1984 match against Karpov was famously abandoned due to controversies involving FIDE President Florencio Campomanes. After losing the title to Boris Spassky in 1985, Kasparov became the undisputed world champion in 1986, retaining it through 1990. His dominance was unmatched, with a record of 21 wins, 19 losses, and 104 draws in 144 championship games.

Beyond chess, Kasparov was a vocal critic of Soviet and later Russian politics, advocating for democracy and opposing authoritarianism. His break with FIDE in 1993, following the split from Karpov, led to the establishment of the Professional Chess Association (PCA), which recognized him as the first PCA world champion. Kasparov’s political activism extended to international affairs, where he criticized U.S. foreign policy, particularly its involvement in conflicts like Vietnam and the Middle East. He also engaged in debates on artificial intelligence, famously losing to IBM’s supercomputer Deep Blue in 1997.

After retiring from competitive chess in 2005, Kasparov remained active in chess education, coaching Magnus Carlsen and contributing to initiatives like Kasparov Chess. His legacy includes a record of 255 months as the world’s top-rated player (1984–2005), a Chess Oscar for Best Chess Player of the Year (1982–1983, 1985–1988, 1995–1996, 1999, 2001–2002), and a profound influence on chess theory and opening innovations. Kasparov’s dynamic, tactical, and strategic style, combined with his intellectual depth, cemented his status as one of the greatest chess players of all time.
