## Garry Kasparov: Summary

Garry Kasparov, born Garik Kimovich Weinstein in Baku in 1963, emerged as one of the most influential figures in chess history. He began studying chess seriously as a child, quickly rising through the ranks with the help of esteemed coaches like Mikhail Botvinnik and Vladimir Makogonov. Kasparov became the youngest-ever world No. 1 at age 21, and at 22, he defeated Anatoly Karpov to become the youngest world chess champion, holding the title from 1985 to 2000 across several intense matches, mostly against Karpov. Kasparov famously broke with FIDE in 1993, founding the Professional Chess Association and defending the "classical" world championship until his loss to Vladimir Kramnik in 2000. He retired from competitive chess in 2005 after a record-breaking career that included holding the No. 1 world ranking for over 20 years and winning or tying for first in every tournament he entered from 1981 to 1991. Kasparov was also a key contributor to chess opening theory and integrated computer preparation into his training, most notably in matches against chess engines such as Deep Blue.

Post-retirement, Kasparov remained active in the chess world through training rising stars like Magnus Carlsen, engaging in exhibition matches, and founding online chess platforms. He played in Chess Olympiads for both the Soviet Union and Russia, amassing numerous medals and contributing organizational expertise. He is widely recognized for his dynamic, attacking playing style and thorough opening preparation, earning eleven Chess Oscars and other distinctions. Kasparov authored numerous influential books, including the "My Great Predecessors" series, and became a prominent public intellectual and activist, notably in politics and human rights. His legacy as a chess player, theorist, computer competitor, coach, and ambassador for the game is considered unparalleled.
