## Summary: Noam Chomsky

Noam Chomsky, born in 1928 in Philadelphia to Ashkenazi Jewish immigrants, is a highly influential linguist, philosopher, cognitive scientist, and political activist. Inspired by his family's intellectual and social values, as well as encounters with left-wing political thought, Chomsky studied linguistics at the University of Pennsylvania under Zellig Harris and earned his PhD in 1955. At MIT, where he spent much of his career, Chomsky revolutionized the field of linguistics by founding generative grammar and proposing the concept of universal grammar—the idea that the ability to acquire language is biologically hardwired in humans. He challenged behaviorist theories, arguing for a nativist, rationalist approach to language, and his work catalyzed the "cognitive revolution" in psychology and philosophy, establishing linguistics as a formal natural science.

Chomsky is also renowned for his extensive political activism and criticism, focusing on issues such as U.S. foreign policy, media propaganda, economic inequality, capitalism, and the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. He identifies as an anarcho-syndicalist or libertarian socialist and is noted for his advocacy of intellectual self-defense against elite-driven misinformation. Chomsky is one of the most cited scholars in the world, credited as a foundational figure in modern linguistics, cognitive science, computer science, and philosophy. His dual legacy includes numerous influential publications in both linguistics and politics; he has received widespread academic recognition, honorary doctorates, and peace awards. Chomsky retired from MIT in 2002 but remains active as a writer and thinker, despite recent health setbacks.
