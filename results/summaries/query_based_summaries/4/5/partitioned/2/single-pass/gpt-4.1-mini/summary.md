# Summary on Noam Chomsky

Noam Chomsky, born in 1928 to Ashkenazi Jewish immigrants in Philadelphia, is a preeminent linguist and political dissident. He pioneered the "cognitive revolution" in linguistics, establishing the field as a formal, natural science and is often called the father of modern linguistics. His work includes the development of generative grammar and the Chomsky hierarchy, influencing cognitive science, computer science, philosophy, and psychology. Politically, Chomsky identifies largely as an anarcho-syndicalist or libertarian socialist and has been a vocal critic of U.S. foreign policy, media propaganda, and capitalist structures. His activism in anti-war movements, especially against the Vietnam War, and support for East Timorese independence have marked his public dissent. Despite facing controversies—such as the defense of free speech for Holocaust denial figures and criticism of Israeli policies—Chomsky remains influential in both academic and political spheres.

Throughout his career, Chomsky maintained a sharp critique of power elites, arguing that mainstream media serve corporate and state interests by filtering news and marginalizing dissenting views. He advocates intellectual self-defense through critical thinking and grassroots democratic control of media. His political writings have established him as a defining intellectual figure and “enemy number one” for establishment discourse. Chomsky retired from MIT in 2002 but continued activism and research, dividing his time between the United States and Brazil in later years. In 2023 he suffered a massive stroke, leading to a decline in public activity, although he remains engaged with current events.
