John von Neumann was a Hungarian-American mathematician, physicist, computer scientist, and polymath who made foundational contributions across multiple disciplines. Born in Budapest in 1903, he was a child prodigy and entered the University of Berlin at age 15. He earned a Ph.D. in mathematics from the University of Budapest and later studied under David Hilbert at the University of Göttingen. 

Von Neumann's mathematical achievements include pioneering work in set theory, where he introduced the axiom of foundation and the notion of class, and developed the theory of von Neumann algebras. He made significant contributions to ergodic theory, measure theory, and functional analysis, and laid the groundwork for the modern theory of Hilbert spaces and operator algebras. He also developed the von Neumann entropy, a cornerstone in quantum information theory, and introduced the von Neumann measurement scheme.

In physics, he established the mathematical framework of quantum mechanics in his 1932 work *Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics*, formulating the Dirac–von Neumann axioms. He also contributed to the development of the theory of nuclear weapons during the Manhattan Project, where he designed the explosive lenses for the plutonium core of the "Fat Man" bomb. He later became a key advisor in the U.S. defense sector, influencing the development of the hydrogen bomb and intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs).

In computer science, von Neumann is credited with proposing the architecture of modern computers—the von Neumann architecture—where both data and instructions are stored in the same memory. He developed algorithms such as merge sort and contributed to the field of stochastic computing and cellular automata. He also played a role in the early development of numerical weather prediction and climate modeling.

Von Neumann was a prolific and influential thinker, with a broad impact on science, mathematics, and technology. He died in 1957 at age 53 from cancer. His legacy endures in numerous fields, including quantum mechanics, computer science, game theory, and economics. He is widely regarded as one of the most influential and brilliant minds of the 20th century.
