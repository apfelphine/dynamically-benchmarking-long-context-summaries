**John von Neumann** (1903–1957) was a Hungarian-born polymath renowned for his profound contributions across mathematics, physics, economics, computer science, and military science. Born into a wealthy Jewish family in Budapest, he was a child prodigy fluent in several languages and exhibiting exceptional mathematical talent from an early age. Von Neumann studied chemical engineering and mathematics, earning a doctorate in mathematics by age 23. He held academic positions in Berlin and Hamburg before moving to Princeton and the Institute for Advanced Study in the United States, where he naturalized and anglicized his name.

Von Neumann's major achievements include foundational work in set theory, introducing the axiom of foundation and class concepts; the formal mathematical framework for quantum mechanics; pioneering ergodic theory, measure theory, and operator algebras (von Neumann algebras); and founding game theory with the minimax theorem and its extension. He was integral to the development of linear programming and made seminal contributions to computing, including the design of the stored-program computer architecture (known as von Neumann architecture), the merge sort algorithm, and early cellular automata theory. During World War II, he significantly impacted the Manhattan Project, especially in designing the implosion mechanism for the atomic bomb. Post-war, he advised U.S. defense and nuclear agencies, advocating for hydrogen bomb development and ICBMs. Widely regarded as one of the 20th century’s most influential mathematicians and scientists, von Neumann combined broad theoretical insight with practical applications, and his legacy spans numerous scientific disciplines.
