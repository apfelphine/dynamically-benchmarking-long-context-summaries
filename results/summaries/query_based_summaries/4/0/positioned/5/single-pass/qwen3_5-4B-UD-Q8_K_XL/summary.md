John von Neumann (1903–1957) was a child prodigy and polymath whose intellect spanned mathematics, physics, computer science, economics, and engineering. In mathematics, he provided the first rigorous axiomatization of set theory, introduced the concept of Hilbert spaces, and developed operator algebras, while making foundational contributions to ergodic theory and measure theory. In economics, he co-founded game theory with Oskar Morgenstern, proving the minimax theorem and establishing the mathematical basis for general equilibrium and linear programming.

Von Neumann was a pivotal figure in the development of modern computing and the atomic age. He designed the theoretical architecture of stored-program computers (the von Neumann architecture) and contributed significantly to the Manhattan Project by designing the implosion lenses for the plutonium core of the atomic bomb. Post-war, he led efforts in scientific computing, numerical weather prediction, and the development of the hydrogen bomb. His work on cellular automata and universal constructors anticipated concepts in artificial life and robotics, cementing his legacy as one of the most influential scientists of the 20th century.
