# Summary of Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman, born in 1953 in New York City to a Jewish family, developed an early passion for computing and was active in the hacker community at MIT’s Artificial Intelligence Laboratory starting in 1971. He earned a physics degree from Harvard in 1974 and briefly pursued graduate studies at MIT. Stallman contributed foundational software like GNU Emacs and other GNU tools and strongly opposed proprietary software and restrictive licensing.

In 1983, Stallman announced the GNU Project to create a free Unix-compatible operating system, formalized in the GNU Manifesto of 1985. He founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF) to support free software development and popularized the concept of copyleft, implemented in the GNU General Public License (GPL). Although the GNU system was largely developed by Stallman and others, it lacked a functioning kernel until Linux emerged in 1991. Stallman advocates calling the complete OS “GNU/Linux” to credit the GNU Project’s philosophical and technical foundation.

Stallman’s activism extends beyond software to digital rights, opposing digital rights management (DRM) and mass surveillance, and emphasizing precise terminology—he favors “free software” over “open source” and insists on “GNU/Linux” over “Linux” alone. He campaigns for reduced copyright terms, supports civil disobedience against restrictive laws, and rejects the term “intellectual property” as misleading. His political stance is grounded in user freedom rather than anarchism.

In 2019, Stallman faced controversy due to comments related to the Jeffrey Epstein scandal and past writings on sensitive topics; this led to his resignation from MIT and FSF. He later rejoined the FSF board in 2021 amid debate and organizational backlash. Despite turbulent events, Stallman remains head of the GNU Project and a prominent figure advocating software freedom.
