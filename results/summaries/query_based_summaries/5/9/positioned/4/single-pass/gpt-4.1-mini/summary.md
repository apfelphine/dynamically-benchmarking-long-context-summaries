## Summary on Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman (b. March 16, 1953, New York City) is a seminal figure in computing, known for initiating the free software movement and founding the GNU Project. Early exposed to computers, he became deeply involved in the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory during the 1970s. He contributed significant technical work such as the development of the Emacs text editor, the GNU Compiler Collection (GCC), and the GNU Debugger, along with pioneering contributions to remote-controlled software development. Stallman’s activism began as a response to the rise of proprietary software in the late 1970s and early 1980s, particularly after encountering restrictions that hindered software freedom. This motivated him to launch the GNU operating system in 1983, advocating the philosophy that software users must have freedom to use, study, modify, and redistribute software.

Stallman is also known for popularizing the concept of copyleft licensing, embodied in the GNU General Public License (GPL), to legally protect software freedoms. He has remained president of the Free Software Foundation (FSF), a nonprofit established to support free software development. His advocacy extends to opposing software patents, digital rights management, and surveillance, and promoting free software terminology such as “free software” (not open source). Stallman’s career includes controversies, notably his 2019 resignation from MIT and FSF following backlash over statements regarding the Jeffrey Epstein scandal and related topics; he reinstated to the FSF board in 2021 amid significant community debate. Throughout, Stallman’s work reflects a steadfast commitment to the ethical and social dimensions of software freedom, blended with technical expertise that fundamentally shaped modern software culture and licensing.
