Richard Stallman was born on March 16, 1953, in New York City. He developed an early interest in computers, first gaining experience writing a numerical analysis program in Fortran at the IBM New York Scientific Center in 1970. Later, while a student at Harvard University in 1970, he became a programmer at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, where he became known by his initials, RMS.

The shift toward proprietary software in the late 1970s and early 1980s prompted Stallman to protest restrictive software licenses, declaring that blocking user freedom was a "crime against humanity." In 1984, he quit MIT to work full-time on the GNU project, announcing the plan for a free operating system compatible with Unix. In 1985, he published the GNU Manifesto, outlining his motivation for creating GNU, and founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF). Stallman popularized the concept of copyleft, implementing it first in the GNU Emacs General Public License (GPL), and contributed key tools to the project, such as GNU Emacs and GNU Make. He advocates for free software as a moral value, not merely a pragmatic one, and continues to campaign against software patents and digital rights management.
