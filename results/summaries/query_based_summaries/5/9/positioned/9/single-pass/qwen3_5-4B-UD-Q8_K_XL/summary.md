Richard Stallman, born in 1953 in New York City, is a computer scientist and the driving force behind the free software movement. After earning a bachelor's degree in physics from Harvard in 1974 and pursuing a doctorate at MIT, he became a research assistant at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. His work there included developing key software tools like Emacs, GCC, and GNU Make, as well as pioneering constraint satisfaction techniques. In 1983, disillusioned by proprietary software restrictions, Stallman quit his job to launch the GNU Project, aiming to create a complete, compatible free operating system. He established the Free Software Foundation (FSF) in 1985 and popularized the concept of "copyleft" through the GNU General Public License (GPL) to legally protect software freedom.

Stallman is a prominent activist who advocates for user freedom, opposing digital rights management, software patents, and surveillance. He insists on the terminology "free software" and "GNU/Linux" to distinguish the movement from the open-source model. His influence extends beyond coding to political activism, including persuading the Indian government to adopt free software in schools and advising leaders globally. In 2019, he resigned from MIT and the FSF due to controversies regarding comments on the Jeffrey Epstein case, but returned to the FSF board in 2021. Stallman remains the head of the GNU project, continues to write extensively on software ethics, and has received numerous honors, including a MacArthur Fellowship and multiple honorary doctorates.
