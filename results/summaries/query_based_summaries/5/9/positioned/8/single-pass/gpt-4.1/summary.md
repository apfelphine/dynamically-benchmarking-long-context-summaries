## Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman, born in New York City in 1953, is known as the founder of the Free Software Movement and the GNU operating system. He studied physics at Harvard University and then worked at MIT's AI Laboratory, where he became influential in hacker culture through contributions such as TECO, Emacs, and advocacy for open access to computers. In response to the growing prevalence of proprietary software and experiences at MIT that limited user freedoms, Stallman initiated the GNU Project in 1983, announcing his plan for a free, Unix-compatible operating system. He established the Free Software Foundation (FSF) and popularized the notion of "copyleft," leading to licenses like the GNU General Public License (GPL). Stallman developed significant GNU tools such as Emacs, GCC, and GDB but the GNU kernel (Hurd) was not completed; the combination of GNU tools with the Linux kernel created what he terms "GNU/Linux." He has advocated fiercely for software freedom, preferring the terms "free software" and "GNU/Linux" over "open source" and "Linux," and strongly opposes proprietary software, software patents, and digital rights management (DRM).

Stallman is also noted for political activism related to software freedom, copyright reduction, surveillance resistance, and free access to information and culture. Throughout his career, he has published influential essays, manuals, and spoken globally on these topics. Stallman’s uncompromising philosophy has shaped both software development and digital ethics. He served as president of the FSF until resigning in 2019 after controversy, later returning to the FSF board; he remains a central figure in the GNU Project. His numerous awards and honorary doctorates are in recognition of his pioneering role in free software, including the invention of tools foundational to GNU and the broader open-source ecosystem. Stallman continues his advocacy and programming despite recent health challenges.
