Richard Stallman is a computer scientist and software freedom advocate born in 1953 in New York City. He developed a strong interest in computers at a young age and studied at Harvard and MIT, where he worked on projects like Emacs and the Lisp machine. In 1983, he announced the GNU project, a free operating system compatible with Unix, to promote software freedom and prevent proprietary software from restricting users' rights to modify, study, and share software. He founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF) to support the movement and introduced the concept of copyleft, a legal mechanism to protect software freedom. Stallman authored key tools like GNU Emacs, GCC, and GNU make, and advocated for free software principles, including opposition to software patents and digital rights management (DRM). He promoted a "free software" philosophy, emphasizing user freedom over commercial interests, and criticized the term "open source" for not highlighting freedom. Stallman played a major role in the free software movement, including advocating for free education and open access to knowledge. In 2019, he resigned from MIT and the FSF following controversy over comments regarding Jeffrey Epstein and his past writings on child pornography, but returned to the FSF board in 2021. He remains a prominent figure in the movement, known for his advocacy of software freedom, ethical use of technology, and resistance to surveillance. Stallman is a recipient of numerous honors, including the MacArthur "Genius" Fellowship and the ACM Software System Award, and is recognized for his contributions to computer science and open-source software.
