## Summary of Information on Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman, born March 16, 1953, in New York City to a Jewish family, showed early interest in computers, programming during high school summers and attending Columbia University Saturday programs. He studied physics at Harvard University, graduating magna cum laude in 1974, then pursued graduate studies briefly at MIT before dedicating himself to programming at MIT's AI Lab. There, he contributed to software projects like TECO, Emacs, and the Lisp machine operating systems, and became known for opposing restricted computer access, notably disabling a password system at MIT in favor of open access.

In response to growing proprietary software restrictions in the late 1970s and 1980s, Stallman launched the GNU Project in 1983 to create a free operating system compatible with Unix. He also founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF) to support free software development and legal advocacy. He introduced the concept of copyleft and authored major GNU components including GNU Emacs, GCC, and GNU Debugger, though the GNU kernel (Hurd) remained incomplete. Stallman popularized calling the combined system GNU/Linux, a naming point of contention with Linux creator Linus Torvalds.

Beyond software development, Stallman is a vocal activist for software freedom, opposing proprietary software, software patents, and digital rights management. He advocates precise terminology favoring "free software" over "open source" and actively campaigns against surveillance and privacy invasions, refusing to use mobile phones and favoring anonymous internet use. His outspoken style has sparked controversies, notably his 2019 comments related to the Jeffrey Epstein scandal, which led to his resignation from MIT and FSF, though he later returned to FSF leadership amid divided community responses.

Stallman resides in Boston, identifies as an atheist of Jewish ancestry, and has spoken on topics ranging from copyright reform to software freedom ethics. His honors include a MacArthur Fellowship and multiple honorary doctorates, reflecting his profound influence on the free software movement and hacker culture.
