## Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman, born in 1953 in New York City, became involved in computing at an early age, later excelling at Harvard and joining the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory as a programmer. Stallman was a prominent figure in the hacker community, contributing to foundational software projects such as TECO and EMACS, and championing open access in computing environments. During the late 1970s and early 1980s, restrictive software practices prompted Stallman to advocate for users' rights to freely share, study, and modify software. In 1984, he left MIT to launch the GNU project—a free Unix-compatible operating system—eventually founding the Free Software Foundation to support the movement.

Stallman authored the GNU Manifesto and pioneered the concept of copyleft, leading to the creation of the GNU General Public License (GPL), which became central to the free software movement. He was responsible for developing key GNU tools, such as Emacs, GCC, and GNU Debugger; he also insisted on recognition of GNU’s role in the widely used GNU/Linux operating system. Throughout his career, Stallman maintained a strong philosophical stance on software freedom, advocating for terminology that emphasized users’ rights and rejecting "open source" as insufficiently principled. His activism expanded to protests against proprietary software, DRM, software patents, and digital surveillance, and he influenced international policies and educational initiatives supporting free software. Despite controversies and resignations in 2019, Stallman returned to the FSF board in 2021. He has received numerous awards for his contributions to computing and software freedom, and continues his advocacy through writings, lectures, and leadership in the GNU project.
