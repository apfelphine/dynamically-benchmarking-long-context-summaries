## Summary: Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman, born in New York City in 1953 to a Jewish family, developed an early interest in computers and programming, working at the IBM New York Scientific Center during high school. He excelled academically at Harvard and later at MIT's AI Laboratory, where he contributed to early software projects like TECO and Emacs. Stallman became a prominent figure in the hacker community but grew increasingly critical of proprietary software as companies began restricting source code access in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Motivated by an incident involving the Xerox 9700 printer software, he launched the GNU Project in 1983 to develop a free Unix-compatible operating system and founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF), advocating software freedom as a moral imperative.

Stallman popularized the concept of "copyleft" through the GNU General Public License, ensuring software remained free to modify and redistribute. He contributed key GNU utilities such as Emacs, GCC, and GDB, but did not develop a kernel; the combination of GNU tools and Linux kernel forms what he calls GNU/Linux. He is known for his strong stance on terminology, emphasizing "free software" over "open source," and criticizing proprietary software. Stallman is also an activist against software patents and digital restrictions, advocating against surveillance and proprietary computing platforms. Controversies surrounding his statements on sensitive social issues led to his resignation from MIT and the FSF in 2019, though he later returned to the FSF board in 2021, sparking further debate. Stallman remains a seminal but polarizing figure in the free software movement.
