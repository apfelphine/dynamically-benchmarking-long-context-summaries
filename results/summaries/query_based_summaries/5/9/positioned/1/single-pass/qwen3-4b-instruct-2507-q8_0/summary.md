Richard Stallman is a computer scientist and software activist born in 1953 in New York City. He developed a strong interest in computers during his youth, working with programming languages like Fortran and APL at a young age. He studied at Harvard University and later worked at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, where he became a prominent figure in the hacker community. 

Stallman is best known for founding the GNU Project in 1983, a free and open-source operating system designed to be compatible with Unix. He championed the concept of free software, emphasizing user freedom to study, modify, and redistribute software. His work led to the creation of essential tools like GNU Emacs, GCC, and the GNU Debugger. He also introduced the concept of "copyleft," a legal mechanism to protect software freedom.

Stallman has been a vocal advocate for free software and against proprietary software, often criticizing software patents and digital rights management. He has participated in protests and campaigns, including efforts to promote free software in schools and to oppose restrictive software licensing. He is known for his strong moral stance, arguing that software freedom is a fundamental human right, not just a technical or pragmatic one.

He has also contributed to open-source communities and promoted free software through education and public speaking. Stallman is a key figure in the free software movement, and his work has influenced major developments in software development, including the Linux operating system, which combines GNU tools with the Linux kernel. He continues to be a leading voice in advocating for software freedom and ethical computing practices.
