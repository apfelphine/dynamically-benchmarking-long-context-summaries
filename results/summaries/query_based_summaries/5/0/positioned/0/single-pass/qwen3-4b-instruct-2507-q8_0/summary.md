Alfred North Whitehead was born in 1861 in Ramsgate, England. He was educated at Sherborne School and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he studied mathematics and earned a B.A. as fourth wrangler. He later became a Fellow of Trinity and made significant contributions to mathematics and philosophy, including authoring *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* and co-authoring *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a foundational work in mathematical logic. He also wrote *An Introduction to Mathematics*, which aimed to explain the unity and structure of mathematics. In the 1920s, Whitehead shifted his focus to philosophy, publishing *The Concept of Nature* and becoming president of the Aristotelian Society. In 1924, he moved to Harvard University as a professor of philosophy, where he produced influential works such as *Science and the Modern World* and *Process and Reality*, a complex metaphysical text compared to Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*. Whitehead's philosophy, known as process philosophy, rejected Cartesian dualism and proposed a dynamic, event-based universe where reality is composed of interconnected processes and experiences. He introduced the concept of "prehension," a form of perception applicable to both living and non-living entities, and argued that all things possess a degree of creativity and freedom. His views on evolution emphasized that life is directed toward increasing satisfaction, and he developed a theistic framework where God is not a static being but a dynamic force that evolves through interaction with the world, embodying love and purpose. Whitehead's work had a lasting influence in theology, ecology, and education, with his ideas being adopted in American progressive theology and educational reform. Despite the difficulty of his writing and initial resistance, his philosophy has been recognized as a significant contribution to 20th-century thought, with ongoing scholarly work and critical editions being developed.
