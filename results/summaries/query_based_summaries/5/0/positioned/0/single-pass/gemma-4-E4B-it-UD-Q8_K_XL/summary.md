Alfred North Whitehead was born in Ramsgate, Kent, England, in 1861. He received his undergraduate education at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he studied mathematics, earning his B.A. in 1884. He taught and wrote on mathematics and physics at Trinity until 1910, during which time he published *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* (1898) and collaborated with Bertrand Russell on *Principia Mathematica* (1910–13).

After resigning from his senior lectureship, Whitehead moved to London and later accepted a professorship of applied mathematics at Imperial College London in 1914. In 1918, he took on significant administrative roles within the University of London system. Toward the end of his career in England, he shifted focus to philosophy, publishing *The Concept of Nature* in 1920. In 1924, he moved to Harvard University, where he produced major philosophical works, including *Science and the Modern World* (1925) and *Process and Reality* (1929). Whitehead's philosophy is known as "process philosophy," rejecting the idea of irreducible brute matter in favor of an event-based ontology, and his views on education stressed the importance of imagination and values.
