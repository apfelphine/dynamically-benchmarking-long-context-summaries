Alfred North Whitehead was a distinguished philosopher and mathematician born in Ramsgate, Kent, England, in 1861. He educated himself at Sherborne and began his studies in mathematics at Trinity College, Cambridge, earning his B.A. in 1884. He spent time collaborating with Bertrand Russell on *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), a work considered one of the most important in 20th-century mathematical logic, which sought to prove all mathematical truths from axioms and inference rules.

Whitehead's philosophical contributions centered on his rejection of "scientific materialism," favoring an event-based or "process" ontology where events are primary and fundamentally interrelated. He developed the concept of "process philosophy," arguing that all entities are experiential and that relations are primary to the entities themselves. His system, known as the "philosophy of organism," posited that creativity is the absolute principle of existence, and he conceptualized God not as a ruling monarch, but as a "dipolar" entity that both receives the world's activity and provides it with ultimate order.
