**Alfred North Whitehead** (1861–1947) was an English mathematician and philosopher renowned for his extensive contributions to mathematics, logic, metaphysics, and education. Educated at Sherborne and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he excelled in mathematics, Whitehead collaborated with Bertrand Russell on *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), a foundational work in mathematical logic. After teaching mathematics and holding several academic positions in England, he moved to Harvard University in 1924, focusing on philosophy and producing significant works such as *Science and the Modern World* (1925) and *Process and Reality* (1929). His philosophical legacy includes the development of "process philosophy," emphasizing reality as a series of interrelated events rather than static matter, and introducing a metaphysical system that integrates creativity, experience, and relationality.

Whitehead’s work spans major mathematical texts (*A Treatise on Universal Algebra*, 1898; *An Introduction to Mathematics*, 1911) and pioneering efforts in logic, ontology, and education theory. He was critical of mechanistic and materialistic views of reality and argued for the importance of imagination and the integration of values in education. His metaphysical system notably reconceived God in a “dipolar” way, emphasizing relationality and co-creation with the world, inspiring modern process theology. Despite the intellectual complexity and initial limited readership of his philosophical works, Whitehead’s influence endures, particularly in American progressive theology, education reform, ecological thought, and some scientific fields. His writings and ideas continue to be studied and expanded upon worldwide, with notable contemporary centers in the United States and China.
