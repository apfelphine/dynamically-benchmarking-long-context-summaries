Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician and philosopher best known for his co-authorship of *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a foundational work in mathematical logic published between 1910 and 1913. Beyond logic, he authored significant treatises on mathematics, including *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* and *An Introduction to Mathematics*, and developed the "philosophy of organism," often termed process philosophy. This metaphysical system posits that reality consists of interrelated events or "occasions of experience" rather than static matter, emphasizing creativity, relationality, and the importance of value and purpose in existence. Whitehead's ideas profoundly influenced theology, particularly the development of process theology, and made contributions to education theory, advocating for a curriculum that connects knowledge through imagination rather than rote learning. He served in academic leadership roles at Trinity College, Cambridge, and Imperial College London before moving to Harvard University in 1924, where he remained until his death. His work remains highly regarded in philosophy, theology, and ecology, offering a holistic view of the universe that anticipates modern concerns about interconnectedness and sustainability.
