Alfred North Whitehead was born in 1861 in Ramsgate, Kent, England. He was educated at Sherborne School and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he earned a B.A. in 1884. He later became a professor of mathematics and physics at Cambridge and, in 1910, resigned his position to join the University of London, where he served as dean of the Faculty of Science. In 1924, he moved to Harvard University, where he became a professor of philosophy and produced major philosophical works, including *The Concept of Nature* (1920), *Science and the Modern World* (1925), and *Process and Reality* (1929), which is considered a foundational text in 20th-century metaphysics. Whitehead's philosophy emphasized a dynamic, process-based view of reality, rejecting static materialism in favor of an ontology of events and relationships. He introduced the concept of "prehension," a form of perception that allows entities to incorporate aspects of the perceived world, and developed a theory of value and evolution in which life seeks to improve its own satisfaction. He also proposed a unique conception of God as a dynamic, relational being that both creates and is transformed by the world. Whitehead's work influenced fields such as theology, education, and science, with lasting impacts in process theology, ecology, and philosophy of science. He is remembered for his profound contributions to metaphysics and his advocacy for a holistic, value-based approach to knowledge and existence.
