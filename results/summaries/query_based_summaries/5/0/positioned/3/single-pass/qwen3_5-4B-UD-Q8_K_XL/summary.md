Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was an English mathematician and philosopher who moved to Harvard University in 1924, where he made his most significant contributions to logic and metaphysics. He is best known for co-authoring *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913) with Bertrand Russell, a foundational work in mathematical logic that, despite being proven incomplete by Gödel's theorem, popularized modern symbolic logic. Beyond logic, Whitehead developed "process philosophy," also known as the philosophy of organism, which rejected the Cartesian idea of static matter in favor of a dynamic universe composed of interrelated "occasions of experience." He argued that reality is defined by processes of change and creativity rather than fixed essences, positing that all entities possess a degree of experience and that God is a "dipolar" being who both provides initial possibilities and receives the world's actualities. His educational philosophy, notably in *The Aims of Education* (1929), criticized "inert ideas" and advocated for a curriculum that emphasizes imagination, values, and the organic connection of knowledge.

Whitehead's influence extended beyond academia into theology, biology, and computer science. His metaphysical framework became the basis for "process theology," influencing figures like Charles Hartshorne and John B. Cobb, and found application in ecological civilization theories that emphasize interdependence. In the realm of computing, his theory of "extensive abstraction" laid the groundwork for "mereotopology," a field describing spatial relations among parts and wholes. His legacy is preserved through the University of Clermont-Ferrand, which bears his name, and the ongoing Whitehead Research Project, which is compiling a critical edition of his works to ensure their continued study and accessibility.
