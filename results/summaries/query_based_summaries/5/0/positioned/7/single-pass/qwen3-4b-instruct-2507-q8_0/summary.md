Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was an English mathematician, logician, and philosopher who made foundational contributions to mathematics and metaphysics. Born in Ramsgate, Kent, he studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he earned a fourth wrangler distinction and wrote a dissertation on James Clerk Maxwell. He later taught at Cambridge and collaborated with Bertrand Russell on *Principia Mathematica*, a landmark work in mathematical logic. 

Whitehead's philosophical work, particularly *Process and Reality* (1929), established him as a leading figure in 20th-century metaphysics. He developed a "process philosophy" that rejected Cartesian materialism, proposing instead that reality consists of interconnected, dynamic events ("occasions of experience") rather than static matter. He emphasized the role of relations, creativity, and change as fundamental to existence, and introduced the concept of "prehension" to describe how entities perceive and interact with one another.

He also made significant contributions to education, advocating for a student-centered, imaginative, and transdisciplinary approach to learning, as detailed in *The Aims of Education and Other Essays*. Whitehead believed knowledge must be linked to real-world application and that education should foster personal growth and values.

His ideas influenced diverse fields, including theology (process theology), ecology, economics, and computer science. He is recognized for his work in ontology, particularly in mereotopology, and for his early insights into the nature of computation and intelligence. Whitehead's legacy endures through his influence on modern thought, especially in areas emphasizing interdependence, creativity, and the value of experience.
