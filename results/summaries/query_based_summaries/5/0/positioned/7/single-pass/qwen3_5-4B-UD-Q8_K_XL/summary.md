Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a prominent English mathematician and philosopher whose work fundamentally shaped 20th-century thought. He is best known for co-authoring *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913) with Bertrand Russell, a monumental three-volume work in mathematical logic that aimed to derive all mathematical truths from a small set of axioms. Despite its initial financial loss and the eventual discovery of Gödel's incompleteness theorem, which demonstrated that such a complete system is impossible, the book remains a cornerstone of modern logic and popularized the field. Whitehead also authored *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* and *An Introduction to Mathematics*, the latter intended for a broader audience.

Whitehead's later career was dominated by his development of "process philosophy" (also known as process metaphysics), a system he termed the "philosophy of organism." Rejecting the materialist view that reality consists of static, independent objects, he argued that the fundamental constituents of the universe are dynamic, interrelated "occasions of experience" or events. This perspective, detailed in his seminal work *Process and Reality* (1929), posits that all entities are defined by their relations to one another and possess a degree of creativity and novelty. His philosophy had a profound influence on theology, particularly the development of process theology, as well as on ecology, education, and physics, emphasizing a holistic, relational view of existence over mechanistic reductionism.
