## Alfred North Whitehead: Summary

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician and philosopher renowned for his contributions to mathematics, logic, philosophy, and education. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, he taught and published influential works on mathematics and physics, including the foundational *Treatise on Universal Algebra* (1898). Collaborating with Bertrand Russell, Whitehead co-authored the landmark *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), a seminal work in mathematical logic. Later, at Harvard University, he became a leading philosopher, developing process philosophy—a metaphysical system emphasizing the primacy of events, change, and relations over static matter.

Whitehead's philosophical writings, such as *Science and the Modern World* (1925) and *Process and Reality* (1929), challenged scientific materialism and advocated for a universe constituted by interdependent processes. He introduced concepts including "prehension" (a general theory of perception), panexperientialism, and a dipolar conception of God that influenced process theology. Whitehead also made significant contributions to educational theory, opposing rote learning and emphasizing imaginative, value-laden, and transdisciplinary approaches. Despite being considered dense and challenging, his work impacted fields ranging from theology and ecology to business and physics. His legacy is especially visible in process philosophy and theology, and his biography has been extensively documented by Victor Lowe.
