Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician and philosopher whose work fundamentally reshaped the understanding of reality through his development of process philosophy. Rejecting the notion of matter as static, independent entities, Whitehead argued that the fundamental building blocks of the universe are dynamic "occasions of experience" or events characterized by "becoming" rather than "being." His metaphysical system, often termed the "philosophy of organism," posits that all things are interrelated through relations and creativity, where every entity possesses a degree of freedom and novelty. This panexperientialist view suggests that experience is universal, applying to humans, animals, and even inanimate processes like electron collisions.

Whitehead's intellectual contributions extended beyond metaphysics into significant achievements in mathematics, most notably his co-authorship of *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a foundational text in mathematical logic that attempted to derive all mathematical truths from symbolic axioms. Despite its initial commercial failure and later partial refutation by Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems, the work remains a cornerstone of 20th-century logic and popularized modern mathematical logic. In his later career, Whitehead moved to Harvard University in 1924, where he wrote seminal works such as *Science and the Modern World* and *Process and Reality*, which challenged Cartesian dualism and influenced diverse fields including theology (inspiring process theology), ecology, and education. His educational philosophy emphasized transdisciplinary learning, imagination, and the rejection of "inert ideas," advocating for an approach where knowledge is organically linked to the student's life and purpose.
