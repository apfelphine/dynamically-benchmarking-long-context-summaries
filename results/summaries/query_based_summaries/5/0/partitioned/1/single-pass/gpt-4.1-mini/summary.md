## Summary: Alfred North Whitehead

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was an English mathematician, logician, and philosopher whose career evolved from mathematics and physics to pioneering work in philosophy and metaphysics. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, he co-authored *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell, a foundational text in mathematical logic. Later, Whitehead developed a process-oriented metaphysical system rejecting the classical notion of reality as composed of independent, unchanging matter. Instead, he proposed that reality consists of interrelated, experiential events, emphasizing creativity, change, and relations as fundamental. His metaphysics introduced the concept of "prehension" to describe perception as the incorporation of other entities into the experience of a given entity, applicable even to inanimate processes. Whitehead’s philosophy of education criticized traditional teaching of disconnected facts ("inert ideas") and advocated for imaginative, transdisciplinary learning rooted in values.

Whitehead moved to Harvard in 1924, where he produced major philosophical works including *Science and the Modern World* and *Process and Reality*. Although his writings are dense and challenging, he influenced various fields such as theology (particularly process theology), ecology, education, and even physics. His process philosophy, highlighting the mutual interdependence of entities and God’s dipolar nature as both primordial potential and consequent actuality, remains influential in progressive theology and interdisciplinary studies. Whitehead emphasized the integration of individual experience with the universal, regarding religion as the individual's solitariness expanding into world loyalty. Despite limited broad acceptance in mainstream philosophy, his ideas have spurred ongoing scholarly and practical applications globally.
