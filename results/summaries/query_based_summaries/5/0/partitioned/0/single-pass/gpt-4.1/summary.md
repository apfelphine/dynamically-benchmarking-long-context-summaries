## Alfred North Whitehead: Key Information

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) was a British mathematician and philosopher whose work spanned mathematics, logic, philosophy, metaphysics, and the philosophy of education. He was educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he later became a fellow and taught mathematics and physics, notably collaborating with Bertrand Russell on *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), a foundational text in mathematical logic. Whitehead also authored *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* (1898) and *An Introduction to Mathematics* (1911), and his theory of "extensive abstraction" influenced ontology and computer science.

Later in life, Whitehead turned to philosophy, publishing *The Concept of Nature* (1920) and eventually joining the faculty at Harvard University as a philosophy professor. There, he produced significant works such as *Science and the Modern World* (1925), *Process and Reality* (1929), and *The Aims of Education and Other Essays* (1929). Whitehead's philosophy, known as process philosophy or philosophy of organism, challenged static materialism by emphasizing interconnected processes ("events") as primary, and introduced influential concepts such as prehension and panexperientialism. His ideas have impacted fields including process theology, education theory, ecology, and science, and led to the development of process thought and ecological civilization models. Whitehead’s legacy endures through ongoing scholarship, including critical editions of his works, though he left no personal archive due to his strong belief in privacy.
