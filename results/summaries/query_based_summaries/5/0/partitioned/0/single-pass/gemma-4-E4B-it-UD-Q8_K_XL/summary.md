## Alfred North Whitehead Summary

Alfred North Whitehead was born in Ramsgate, Kent, England, in 1861. He was educated at Sherborne and began studying mathematics at Trinity College, Cambridge, earning his B.A. in 1884. He taught mathematics and physics at Trinity until 1910, during which time he wrote *Treatise on Universal Algebra* (1898) and collaborated with Bertrand Russell on *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913). After a period in London, he became a professor of applied mathematics at Imperial College London in 1914. In 1918, his responsibilities expanded within the University of London system, leading to his election as dean of the Faculty of Science. In 1920, he published *The Concept of Nature*, and in 1925, *Science and the Modern World*.

In 1924, Whitehead moved to the United States to join the faculty at Harvard University. During his Harvard tenure, he produced major philosophical contributions, including *Process and Reality* (1929). Whitehead developed a "process" ontology, rejecting the notion of static, independent matter in favor of reality composed of interrelated events. His philosophy emphasizes "panexperientialism," suggesting all things have some degree of creativity or freedom. He viewed God not as an imposing sovereign, but as a receptive entity that is changed by the world's activities. In education, he advocated for a transdisciplinary approach focused on deep, valued concepts rather than disconnected facts.
