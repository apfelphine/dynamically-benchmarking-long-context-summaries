## Summary of "Frankenstein"

"Frankenstein" is a novel by Mary Shelley, written during the summer of 1816 and published anonymously in 1818. The story follows Victor Frankenstein, a young scientist from Geneva who, after the death of his mother, becomes fascinated with the creation of life. At the University of Ingolstadt, Victor secretly assembles a large humanoid creature from body parts, bringing it to life through an undisclosed scientific process. Horrified by his experiment, Victor abandons the creature, who eventually suffers harsh rejection and violence from humanity. The creature, after experiencing isolation and mistreatment, seeks revenge on Victor by killing his loved ones, most notably his younger brother William and Victor's wife, Elizabeth. The narrative concludes with Victor pursuing the creature into the Arctic, where he dies, and the creature, expressing remorse, vows to destroy himself.

Mary Shelley's composition was inspired by conversations about science, galvanism, and the principle of life while she was at Lake Geneva with Percy Bysshe Shelley and Lord Byron. The novel integrates influences from her parents' works, contemporary science, and authors such as Milton, Coleridge, and Ovid. Critically, "Frankenstein" explores themes of creation, responsibility, the pursuit of knowledge, and alienation. It is framed as a "Modern Prometheus" and often touches on questions of humanity and morality. The book's reception evolved from mixed early reviews to widespread acclaim, and it is now considered foundational to science fiction and Gothic literature. Its lasting legacy is seen in numerous adaptations in film, literature, and other media, including comic books and postage stamps, further cementing its influence on culture and the genre.
