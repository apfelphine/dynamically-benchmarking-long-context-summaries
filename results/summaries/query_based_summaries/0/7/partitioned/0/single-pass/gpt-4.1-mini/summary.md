# Summary of "Frankenstein"

Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* was written during the summer of 1816 and published anonymously in 1818, with later editions crediting her authorship. The novel narrates Victor Frankenstein, a scientist from an aristocratic family, who discovers a method to create life and assembles a grotesque creature from cadaver parts. Upon animating it, he is horrified and rejects his creation, which then learns language, violence, and ultimately seeks vengeance against Victor, leading to tragedy.

The work is heavily influenced by Enlightenment and Romantic ideas, as well as literary sources like Milton’s *Paradise Lost* which shapes the creature’s identity as both an Adam and a fallen angel. Shelley’s personal experiences — including loss and guilt — shape themes in the novel such as the ethical ramifications of scientific hubris, isolation, and the quest for identity. Over time, *Frankenstein* has become a foundational Gothic and early science fiction text, inspiring countless adaptations and critical discourse exploring its aesthetic, moral, psychoanalytic, and feminist dimensions. Notably, the novel’s depiction of the creature diverges from popular culture’s image of a composite monster brought to life by electricity; Shelley’s original leaves the life-giving process ambiguous and emphasizes the creature’s humanity and rejection.
