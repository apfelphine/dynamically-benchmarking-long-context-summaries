Frankenstein, a novel by Mary Shelley, centers on Victor Frankenstein, a young man from a wealthy Genevese family who becomes obsessed with alchemy and modern sciences. After his mother's death, he studies at the University of Ingolstadt, where he discovers a way to create life by assembling body parts from charnel houses and graves. He brings the creature to life using an unspecified process, but is horrified by its appearance and flees. The creature, initially unnamed and referred to as a "wretch" or "demon," grows intelligent, learns to read (including Paradise Lost), and develops a desire for companionship. It eventually confronts Victor, kills his younger brother William, and frames Victor's servant Justine for the murder. Victor, devastated, vows revenge and pursues the creature, eventually following it to the Arctic. In a climactic confrontation, the creature asks Victor to create a female companion, which Victor agrees to. However, Victor later destroys the incomplete female creature, and the original creature kills Victor's fiancée Elizabeth on their wedding night. Victor's father dies of grief, and Victor, now broken, chases the creature across the Arctic ice. He nearly dies but is rescued by Captain Robert Walton, who recounts the story. The creature, mourning Victor's death, plans to burn himself on a pyre before departing.

The novel draws on themes of scientific ambition, guilt, and the consequences of defying nature. Shelley's personal experiences—loss of her mother, the death of her first child, and strained relationships—deeply influence the themes of grief and isolation. The creature is often mislabeled as "Frankenstein," a misnomer popularized by the 1931 film. The creature's identity as a "fallen angel" (echoing Lucifer from Milton's Paradise Lost) and its comparison to Adam reflect its longing for acceptance and its struggle with being created without a name. The name "Frankenstein" is linked to the German name for a castle associated with alchemical experiments, and "Victor" may stem from Milton's Paradise Lost, where God is called "the victor" in creating life. The novel's subtitle, "Modern Prometheus," draws a parallel between Victor's creation of life and Prometheus's gift of fire to humanity, highlighting the moral and existential risks of scientific progress. The story explores the dangers of unchecked ambition, the isolation of the created, and the complex relationship between creator and creation.
