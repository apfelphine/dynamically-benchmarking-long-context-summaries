## Frankenstein

*Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* is an influential novel written by Mary Shelley, conceived during the summer of 1816 at Lake Geneva amid discussions on science and life. Inspired by Enlightenment and Romantic ideas, as well as personal tragedies, Shelley began the story as part of a challenge among friends. The plot follows Victor Frankenstein, a young scientist from Geneva, who, driven by grief and obsession with science, creates life using ingredients from graves. Repulsed by his creation—a large, grotesque creature—Victor abandons him, which leads to tragic consequences. The creature evolves intellectually and emotionally, learning language and reading, but faces rejection and cruelty from humanity, including Victor. Seeking companionship, the creature requests Victor create a female counterpart, but Victor destroys her, resulting in a series of murders and the destruction of Victor’s family. Victor ultimately dies pursuing the creature, who appears aboard a ship in the Arctic, vows to end his own life, and disappears.

The novel draws on literary influences like Paradise Lost, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, Enlightenment thought, and contemporary scientific debates about life and death. Frankenstein’s monster, often mistakenly called Frankenstein, reflects themes of creation, isolation, rebellion, and the ethical dilemmas of unchecked scientific ambition. The book was first published anonymously in 1818, later credited to Mary Shelley in its 1823 edition, and revised in 1831. Though initially met with mixed reviews, Frankenstein rapidly gained popularity, especially through dramatic adaptations and has been recognized as a landmark in Gothic and science fiction literature. The story has been adapted into many films, comic books, and stage works, and continues to resonate due to its exploration of fundamental questions about humanity, responsibility, and the consequences of creation.
