**Frankenstein** by Mary Shelley emerged from the literary influences of the French Revolution, Percy Shelley’s poetry, and the scientific theories of the time, particularly those of Erasmus Darwin and Luigi Galvani. The novel centers on Victor Frankenstein, a scientist who creates a life-sized humanoid creature using stolen body parts and an unspecified scientific process. Initially repelled by his creation, Victor abandons it, which grows bitter and seeks revenge. The creature, initially isolated and fearful, learns language and society through observation, ultimately confronting Victor in Geneva, where it kills his brother and frames a servant for the crime. Victor, consumed by guilt, pursues the creature across the Arctic, where he dies, leaving the creature to mourn his loss.

Shelley’s personal tragedies, including the loss of her mother, stepsister, and first child, deeply influenced the novel’s themes of loss, guilt, and the consequences of defying nature. The story’s subtitle, *The Modern Prometheus*, reflects Shelley’s critique of Victor’s hubris, paralleling the mythological Prometheus’s theft of fire from the gods. The creature’s tragic arc and its existential questions about humanity’s purpose resonate with Shelley’s own philosophical and emotional struggles. The novel’s enduring legacy lies in its exploration of ethical dilemmas in science, the nature of creation, and the moral responsibility of humanity.
