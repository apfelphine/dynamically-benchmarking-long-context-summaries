## Frankenstein — Summary

*Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* is a novel written by Mary Shelley, first published anonymously in 1818 and later credited to her in a second edition in 1823. The story follows Victor Frankenstein, a young scientist from Geneva who, after years studying alchemy and modern sciences, discovers a method to imbue life into assembled body parts taken from graves and slaughterhouses. Victor’s creature, an unnamed "monster," is rejected by society and his creator due to his grotesque appearance. Throughout the novel, the creature seeks acceptance and companionship, learning language and literature in secret, notably referencing *Paradise Lost*. Traumatized by repeated rejection and acts of violence, he becomes vengeful, leading to a cycle of tragedy involving Victor’s loved ones. The novel’s subtitle and many themes draw inspiration from classical works, notably the Prometheus myth and Milton’s *Paradise Lost*, and it heavily draws on the scientific debates and literature of Shelley’s era, including works by her parents and figures like Humphry Davy.

Shelley’s personal experiences—loss, guilt, and isolation—profoundly influenced the themes of the novel, as did literary influences and scientific discussions of her time. The book debuted as a triple-decker in 1818 and found immediate success, thanks in part to popular stage adaptations. It has since been acclaimed as a pioneering work of science fiction and a foundational Gothic novel, celebrated for its exploration of creation, responsibility, and what it means to be human. Modern criticism recognizes its aesthetic and moral relevance, and it remains influential in literature and popular culture, regularly adapted into plays, films, and comics. Esteemed as a landmark work, *Frankenstein* is regarded as one of the greatest novels in the English language, exploring timeless philosophical questions of purpose, belonging, and creation.
