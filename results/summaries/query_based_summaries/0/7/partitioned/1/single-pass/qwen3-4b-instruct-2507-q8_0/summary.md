Frankenstein, written by Mary Shelley, explores the creation of a sentient creature by Victor Frankenstein, who discovers a new principle of life through scientific experimentation. The creature, initially rejected by its creator, grows to be intelligent and empathetic, leading to a tragic cycle of rejection and violence. The novel delves into themes of isolation, guilt, and the consequences of defying nature, influenced by Shelley's personal experiences, including the loss of her mother and the death of her first child. The creature's identity as a composite of whole body parts and the use of electricity are not consistent with Shelley's original work, which emphasizes a deeper, more ambiguous scientific process. The novel's subtitle, "Modern Prometheus," draws on the myth of Prometheus, who brought fire to humanity, and is interpreted as a critique of scientific advancement and its moral implications. The story was first published anonymously in 1818, with a dedication to Shelley's father, William Godwin, and gained immediate popularity through theatrical adaptations. Critical reception has evolved from mixed contemporary reviews to widespread acclaim, with modern scholars recognizing it as a foundational work in Gothic and Romantic literature, and one of the earliest science fiction novels due to its focus on scientific experimentation and its ethical consequences. The narrative has inspired numerous films, plays, and adaptations, including the iconic 1931 film with Boris Karloff, and continues to be a subject of psychoanalytic, feminist, and philosophical analysis.
