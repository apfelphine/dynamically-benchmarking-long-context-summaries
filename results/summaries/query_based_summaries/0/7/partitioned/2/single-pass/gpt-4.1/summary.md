## Summary of Frankenstein

Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* was written in 1816 and first published anonymously in 1818, later credited to her in subsequent editions. The novel follows Victor Frankenstein, a young scientist who discovers a method to animate dead matter, ultimately creating a large, grotesque creature assembled from body parts. Repulsed and fearful, Victor abandons his creation, who then becomes embittered after repeated cruelty from humans. The creature learns language and literature, including Milton's *Paradise Lost*, and ultimately seeks revenge by killing Victor’s brother and framing a servant, demanding Victor make him a companion. After Victor refuses and destroys the intended companion, the creature retaliates by killing Victor’s loved ones, leading to a pursuit that ends with Victor’s death in the Arctic and the creature’s vow to end his own life.

Shelley's inspiration drew from Enlightenment and Romantic-era discussions on science, philosophy, and the nature of life, as well as personal experiences of loss. Literary influences include Milton’s *Paradise Lost*, Coleridge’s *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*, and contemporary scientific debates about galvanism and the possibilities of reanimation. *Frankenstein* was the product of a challenge proposed by Lord Byron in Geneva, and its themes reflect both the optimism and anxieties of scientific progress. The novel received mixed reviews upon publication but rapidly gained popularity, inspiring numerous adaptations in theater, film, comics, and even postage stamps. Today, it is regarded as a founding work of science fiction and Gothic literature, often discussed for its exploration of creation, isolation, and the limits of human ambition.
