**Frankenstein**

Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* (1818) draws inspiration from her parents’ philosophical and political works, particularly her father William Godwin’s ideas on political justice and her mother Mary Wollstonecraft’s advocacy for women’s rights. The novel’s central themes—scientific ambition, the consequences of defying nature, and the ethical dilemmas of creation—stem from Shelley’s personal experiences, including the loss of her mother, her father’s influence, and her own struggles with grief and identity.

The story follows Victor Frankenstein, a brilliant but obsessive scientist who seeks to create life through alchemy and modern scientific methods. After assembling a grotesque humanoid creature from stolen body parts, Victor abandons it, fearing its monstrous nature. The creature, initially misunderstood and persecuted, learns to survive and eventually seeks revenge against Victor. The novel explores themes of isolation, guilt, and the moral responsibility of creation, with Victor’s pursuit of revenge culminating in his own downfall. The creature’s tragic journey, marked by loneliness and a quest for acceptance, underscores the novel’s central question: *What does it mean to be human?* Shelley’s work remains a foundational text in science fiction and Gothic literature, celebrated for its exploration of ethical dilemmas and the consequences of unchecked scientific ambition.
