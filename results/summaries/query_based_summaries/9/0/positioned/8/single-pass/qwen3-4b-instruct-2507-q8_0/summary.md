**Education in Pakistan**  

Pakistan's education system is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, inspired by the English model. Primary education spans 6 years (grades 1–6), followed by middle school (grades 6–8), and secondary education (grades 9–12). Students take national examinations administered by regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education (BISE) at the end of grades 9 (SSC-I) and 10 (SSC-II), leading to the Secondary School Certificate (SSC). At grades 11 and 12, students choose from streams such as pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities, commerce, or computer science, culminating in the Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC).  

Tertiary education includes undergraduate degrees (Bachelor’s) in fields like engineering, medicine, law, and business, and postgraduate programs (Master’s and PhD). Teacher education is a key focus, with programs available at the undergraduate and postgraduate levels. However, challenges persist, including teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and gender disparities.  

Gender disparity remains a significant issue, with fewer girls enrolling and attending school, especially in rural areas. Women face lower literacy rates (42% vs. 67% for men) and limited access to higher education and leadership roles.  

The system emphasizes rote learning and lacks modern pedagogical approaches, with poor infrastructure and outdated labs. Despite government efforts, access and quality remain uneven.  

Religious education is integrated into the national curriculum, with Islamic studies mandatory. Madrassas, while providing free education and shelter, are criticized for promoting rigid religious ideologies and rote memorization.  

Pakistan's literacy rate has improved to 60.65% (2023), with higher rates in urban areas. The government aims to increase tertiary education access, but financial constraints and socio-cultural barriers limit progress.  

Private and international schools offer alternative curricula (e.g., GCE, IGCSE, AP), providing access to global standards.  

Overall, the system is marked by progress in access but faces persistent challenges in equity, quality, and relevance to modern needs.
