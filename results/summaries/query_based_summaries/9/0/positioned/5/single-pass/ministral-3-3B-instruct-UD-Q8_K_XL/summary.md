Pakistan’s education system is structured into **primary, secondary, and tertiary education**, with significant regional and gender disparities.

**Primary Education (Grades 1–6):**
- **Attendance rates** are low, with only **67.5%** of children completing primary school, below the global average of 90%. The curriculum follows an English-based system, with subjects including Urdu, English, mathematics, science, religious studies, and Pakistan Studies. Provincial and regional languages (e.g., Punjabi, Sindhi) are taught in region-specific schools.
- **Public expenditure** on education is minimal (2.2% of GDP), with most funding directed toward lower-level institutions. Higher education receives only about **12%** of the total allocation, leaving lower-income families unable to access quality education.

**Secondary Education (Grades 7–12):**
- **Compulsory** from age 5 to 16, but **only 62%** of students complete secondary school. The system is divided into **matriculation (SSC, grades 9–10)** and **intermediate (HSSC, grades 11–12)**, with students choosing streams like pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities, or commerce.
- **Alternative qualifications** include the **General Certificate of Education (GCE)** and **International Baccalaureate (IB)**, though these are less common. Vocational education (e.g., Technical School Certificate) is also available.
- **Gender disparities** persist, with **18%** of women completing 10+ years of schooling. While progress has been made in improving female enrollment, cultural norms, high tuition costs, and poor infrastructure remain barriers.

**Tertiary Education:**
- **University enrollment** is low (6% of Pakistanis, 9% of men and 3.5% of women). Most tertiary education is offered through **public universities** (e.g., Quaid-i-Azam, NUST) and **private institutions**, with degrees in engineering, medicine, law, and the arts.
- **Teacher education** programs are criticized for being **knowledge-based rather than application-focused**, with many teachers lacking advanced qualifications. Reforms aim to improve curriculum relevance and teacher training but face challenges due to limited resources.
- **Vocational and technical education** is growing, though it remains underdeveloped compared to academic education. The system aims to prepare students for industry, but gaps in infrastructure and teacher training persist.

**Key Challenges:**
- **Low literacy rates** (60.65% in 2023), particularly in rural areas, linked to poverty and limited access to education.
- **Infrastructure issues**, including overcrowded schools and inadequate facilities, especially in underfunded regions.
- **Religious and cultural influences**, such as the growing role of **madrassas** (Islamic seminaries) in rural areas, which often prioritize rote memorization over academic learning.
- **Gender inequality**, with women facing systemic barriers to education and employment, despite legal rights to free primary education.

**Reforms and Initiatives:**
- The government has introduced policies like **free and compulsory education** and **stipends for female students**, though implementation remains inconsistent.
- **Distance learning** (e.g., Teleschool) has expanded access, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, but rural students still lack internet connectivity.
- **International recognition** of Pakistani universities has improved, with institutions like NUST and LUMS ranking among the top in Asia. However, systemic issues like teacher shortages and outdated curricula persist.
