## Education in Pakistan

Education in Pakistan follows a national system inspired largely by the English educational framework and is divided into primary, secondary, tertiary, technical, and vocational levels. Primary education (years 1–5) is followed by middle school (years 6–8), often single-sex, and secondary education (years 9–12), which culminates in national examinations awarding Secondary School Certificates (SSC) and Higher Secondary School Certificates (HSSC). Various academic streams—such as pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities, commerce, and computer science—are offered at the intermediate level. English and Urdu dominate as languages of instruction, with regional languages and foreign languages also taught. Despite efforts, Pakistan has a relatively low primary school completion rate (~67.5%) and net primary school attendance around 66%, with significant gender disparities: females lag behind males in enrollment, literacy, and academic attainment, especially in rural areas. Madrassas represent a significant part of the system, especially for poor families, focusing on Islamic education but sometimes criticized for limited curricular scope.

Tertiary education includes universities providing bachelor’s (two- to four-year programs), master’s, MPhil, and PhD degrees, with professional degrees in fields like engineering, medicine, law, and pharmacy requiring accreditation from respective councils. Technical and Vocational Education Training (TVET) plays a growing role to meet workforce needs. Teacher education faces challenges including shortages, inadequate training, and outdated curricula. Public expenditure on education is about 2% of GDP, among the lowest regionally, leading to infrastructure issues such as unsafe school buildings. The literacy rate in Pakistan is approximately 60.65%, with urban literacy higher than rural and male literacy outperforming female literacy. Distance learning tools like Teleschool and open universities aid education access, especially during crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic. The educational system continues to grapple with quality, relevance, gender inequality, and the influence of religious institutions on curriculum and pedagogy.
