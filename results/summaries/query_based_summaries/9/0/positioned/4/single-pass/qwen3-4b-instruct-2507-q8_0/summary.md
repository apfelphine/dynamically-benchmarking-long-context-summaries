**Education in Pakistan**  

Pakistan's education system is structured into three main stages: primary, secondary, and tertiary. Primary education, which begins at age 5–6, lasts for 8 years (grades 1–8), with the curriculum including subjects like arts, computer studies, general science, modern languages (Urdu and English), mathematics, religious education (Islamic Studies), social studies, and Pakistan Studies. Attendance is not mandatory, and only about 67.5% of children complete primary education.  

Secondary education begins at grade 9 and lasts for four years, culminating in the Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC). Students take standardized national exams at the end of each year (SSC-I, SSC-II, HSSC-I, HSSC-II). The curriculum includes a combination of compulsory and elective subjects, with students choosing between three streams—pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities, computer science, and commerce—starting in grade 11. Alternative qualifications like GCE, IGCSE, and AP are available through British examination boards.  

Tertiary education includes universities offering bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees, as well as technical and vocational institutions. Most university programs require a minimum of four years, with pass or honors degrees available. The government aims to increase university enrollment from 6% (2007) to 10% by 2015 and 15% by 2020.  

Significant challenges include gender disparity, with rural families often prioritizing boys' education, resulting in lower female enrollment and literacy rates (42% vs. 67% for men). The quality of education is declining due to outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and lack of infrastructure.  

The government spends about 2% of GDP on education, with plans to increase this to 7%. Despite these efforts, issues such as poor teacher training, lack of relevance to modern needs, and inadequate access to quality education persist.  

Notable achievements include prominent alumni like Abdus Salam (Nobel laureate in physics), Atta-ur-Rahman (scientist), and Arfa Abdul Karim Randhawa (young computer prodigy).  

The system is also marked by a strong emphasis on religious education, with Islamic studies taught in all schools, and concerns over the marginalization of minorities in curricula.  

Overall, Pakistan's education system faces persistent challenges in equity, quality, and relevance, though it continues to evolve with reforms and increasing investment.
