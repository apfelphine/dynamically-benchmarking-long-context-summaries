# Education in Pakistan

Pakistan's education system is a multi-tiered structure heavily influenced by the English model, characterized by significant challenges in access, quality, and equity. **Formal education** is divided into primary (years 1–8), secondary (years 9–12), and tertiary levels. The primary education completion rate remains low, with only about 67.5% of children finishing primary school, and national expenditure on education has historically hovered around 2% of GDP. The curriculum includes eight core disciplines such as Arts, Science, Mathematics, and Religious Education (Islamic Studies), though the teaching of Religious Education is often restricted in many public schools. Secondary education culminates in the Secondary School Certificate (SSC) and Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC) exams administered by regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education (BISE), with alternative qualifications like GCE and IGCSE available in private institutions.

The system faces profound structural issues, including **gender disparity**, where female enrollment and literacy rates lag significantly behind males due to patriarchal norms, cultural barriers, and unsafe infrastructure. While government policies aim to provide free education for girls up to class 10, dropout rates remain high in rural areas. **Quality of education** is frequently criticized for being outdated, overcrowded, and reliant on rote learning rather than critical thinking, exacerbated by a shortage of qualified teachers and poor laboratory facilities. A diverse alternative system exists, including **Madrassas** (Islamic seminaries) which serve many impoverished families and offer free boarding, and **non-formal education** provided by NGOs like BRAC to assist dropouts. Tertiary education is expanding but faces hurdles with accreditation, high costs, and a mismatch between academic output and market needs, despite efforts to increase the percentage of university graduates.
