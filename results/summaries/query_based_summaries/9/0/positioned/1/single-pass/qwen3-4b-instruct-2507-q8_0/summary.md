**Education in Pakistan**

- **Primary Education**: 
  - Standard system inspired by the English model. 
  - Pre-school (Play Group, Nursery, Kindergarten) for ages 3–5. 
  - Junior school (Grades 1–5), followed by middle school (Grades 6–8). 
  - Curriculum includes Arts, Computer Studies, General Science, Modern Languages, Mathematics, Religious Education (Islamic Studies), and Social Studies. 
  - Single-sex education is preferred in many areas, though co-education exists in cities. 
  - Only about 67.5% of children complete primary education. 

- **Secondary Education**:
  - Begins in Grade 9 and lasts for four years. 
  - Students take national exams (SSC-I and SSC-II) at the end of Grade 9 and Grade 10, leading to the Secondary School Certificate (SSC). 
  - Intermediate education (Grades 11–12) leads to the Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC). 
  - Students can choose from pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities, computer science, or commerce streams. 
  - Alternative qualifications include GCE, IGCSE, and AP. 

- **Tertiary Education**:
  - 6% of Pakistanis have university degrees (as of 2007), with a goal to increase to 10% by 2015 and 15% by 2020. 
  - Bachelor’s degrees (4–5 years) are offered in fields like Engineering, Medicine, Law, Architecture, and Pharmacy. 
  - Pass and Honours degrees are available; Honours degrees are now being phased out. 
  - Teacher education includes 12 years of schooling, followed by an associate degree and a bachelor’s degree in education. 

- **Non-formal and Informal Education**:
  - Public vocational schools, technical training centers, and agriculture centers provide training. 
  - Apprenticeship systems are framed by the state. 
  - Informal education includes school-leavers and low-skilled individuals trained under senior craftsmen. 
  - Non-profit organizations run projects like the MagnifiScience Centre in Karachi. 

- **Madrassas**:
  - Islamic seminaries teaching Quran, Hadith, Fiqh, Arabic, and some non-Islamic subjects. 
  - Popular among the poorest families due to free food and housing. 
  - Estimates range from 12,000 to 40,000 madrassas; they outnumber public schools in some areas. 

- **Gender Disparity**:
  - Deeply patriarchal society; gender disparity in education is significant. 
  - Only 18% of Pakistani women have 10+ years of schooling. 
  - Female enrollment has improved slightly (F/M ratio increased from 0.47 to 0.74 between 1990–2000), but remains lower than male enrollment. 
  - Literacy rates are lower for women (42%) compared to men (67%). 
  - Male teachers dominate the profession (2:1 ratio), and female university leadership is rare. 

- **Qualitative Dimension**:
  - Quality of education is declining due to teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and lack of professional skills. 
  - Students lack communication and professional skills upon graduation. 
  - Universities are expensive, limiting access for many students. 

- **Teacher Education**:
  - Teacher training programs are criticized for being knowledge-based and not application-based. 
  - Teachers are often not highly qualified or trained in human rights. 
  - There is a shortage of qualified teachers, especially in rural areas. 

- **Technical and Vocational Education (TVET)**:
  - Offers technical and vocational training from Grade 5 to Grade 10. 
  - Courses include Technical School Certificate (TSC) and Diploma of Associate Engineering (DAE). 
  - DAE is a three-year program leading to a professional qualification. 

- **Distance Learning**:
  - Teleschool, an educational TV channel, broadcasts lessons for kindergarten to high school. 
  - Distance learning is available via institutions like Allama Iqbal Open University (AIOU). 
  - Online learning is limited in rural areas due to poor internet access. 

- **Achievements**:
  - Notable alumni include Abdus Salam (Nobel laureate in Physics), Atta-ur-Rahman (UNESCO Science Prize), Ayub Ommaya (neurosurgeon), Mahbub-ul-Haq (Human Development Index), Ismat Beg (mathematician), Arfa Abdul Karim Randhawa (computer prodigy), and Nergis Mavalvala (gravitational wave researcher). 

- **Education Expenditure**:
  - Public expenditure on education is about 2% of GDP. 
  - A 2009 national policy aimed to increase it to 7% of GDP, though this target has not been achieved. 
  - Chronic underinvestment in infrastructure (e.g., unsafe school buildings) undermines learning environments. 

- **Religion and Education**:
  - Religious content is deeply embedded in the curriculum. 
  - The Islamization of the national curriculum in the 1980s influenced public education. 
  - Textbooks often depict Jews and Israel negatively, omit the Holocaust, and promote ideological narratives. 
  - Religious education is mandatory, and the curriculum promotes traditional gender roles. 

- **Literacy Rate**:
  - 2023 literacy rate: 60.65% (68% for men, 52.84% for women). 
  - Higher in urban areas (74.09%) than rural areas (51.56%). 
  - Literacy rate is lower than in neighboring countries, especially in South Asia. 

- **University Rankings**:
  - As of 2022, 6 universities are ranked in the top 1000 QS Asia University rankings. 
  - NUST is ranked #358, Quaid-i-Azam University #378, PIEAS #398, LUMS #651, UET Lahore #801, and University of the Punjab #801. 

- **Gender and Social Issues**:
  - Women face significant barriers in education and employment. 
  - Transgender individuals are recognized under the Pakistan Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act of 2018, but face marginalization and abuse. 

- **Policy and Reform**:
  - Government policies aim to improve access and quality, but implementation remains inconsistent. 
  - Reforms to address gender disparity, curriculum relevance, and teacher quality are ongoing.
