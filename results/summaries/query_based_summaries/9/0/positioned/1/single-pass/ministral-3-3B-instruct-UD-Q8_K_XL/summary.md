In **Pakistan**, the education system is structured into **primary, secondary, and tertiary education**, with a notable emphasis on compulsory schooling from age 5 to 18. **Primary education** (grades 1–8) is typically taught in English or Urdu, with subjects including mathematics, science, social studies, religious education, and Urdu/English literature. Attendance rates are relatively low, with only about **67.5%** of children completing primary school, and the system faces challenges such as inadequate infrastructure, teacher shortages, and limited resources in rural areas.

**Secondary education** (grades 9–12) is divided into **matriculation (SSC)** and **intermediate (HSSC)** levels, with standardized national exams administered by regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education (BISE). Students can pursue vocational streams (e.g., technical education) or academic tracks (e.g., pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities). While **tertiary education** (universities and colleges) has seen growth, it remains underfunded, with only **6%** of Pakistanis holding university degrees as of 2007. Higher education institutions offer degrees in fields like engineering, medicine, law, and the arts, though quality and accessibility remain issues.

The system also grapples with **gender disparities**, with female enrollment rates significantly lower than males, particularly in rural areas. Despite progress, cultural norms, economic constraints, and limited infrastructure persist as barriers to equitable education. Additionally, **madrassas** (Islamic seminaries) remain a significant alternative, particularly in poorer communities, though they often lack formal accreditation. Overall, Pakistan’s education system continues to evolve, with ongoing reforms aimed at improving access, quality, and inclusivity.
