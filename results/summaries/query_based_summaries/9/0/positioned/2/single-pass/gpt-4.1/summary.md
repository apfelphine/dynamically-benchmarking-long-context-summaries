## Education in Pakistan: Key Features and Challenges

Education in Pakistan encompasses several stages: primary, secondary, tertiary, and non-formal systems. The national curriculum, inspired mainly by the British educational model, offers instruction in Urdu and English, with provincial and regional languages taught selectively. Primary education comprises pre-school and grades 1-8, yet only about 67.5% of children complete this level, hindered by chronic underfunding (education expenditure has hovered around 2% of GDP). Secondary education begins at year 9 and culminates with a Higher Secondary School Certificate after year 12, with students evaluated through standardized examinations. Alternative tracks include technical/vocational education and international curricula (O/A Levels, IGCSE, AP), while religious madrasas play a notable role, especially in underserved regions. Tertiary education, including universities and professional institutes, remains underfunded and predominantly inaccessible; only 6% of Pakistanis held university degrees as of 2007, with notable gender and regional disparities.

Systemic challenges persist, such as low literacy rates (60.65% nationally; lower for women and rural populations), gender disparities (only 18% of women have ten or more years of schooling), outdated curricula, and inadequate teacher training. The quality of education is marred by rote learning, insufficient infrastructure, and limited professional relevance. While reforms like compulsory education for ages 5–16 and initiatives to improve female and transgender inclusion have been introduced, access and equity remain problematic. Efforts in technical/vocational training and distance learning (e.g., Teleschool and Allama Iqbal Open University) aim to broaden opportunities, especially for marginalized groups. Overall, Pakistan’s education sector faces significant hurdles in funding, inclusion, and quality, though incremental progress has been made in enrollment, female participation, and literacy.
