**Education in Pakistan**

Pakistan's formal education system is structured into primary (grades 1–5), middle (grades 6–8), secondary (grades 9–12), and tertiary levels. Primary education covers core subjects like arts, sciences, languages (Urdu and English), mathematics, religious and social studies, with curricula varying by institution. Secondary education culminates in standardized examinations (SSC and HSSC) administered regionally, granting certificates essential for higher studies. Parallel alternative qualifications include British GCE O/A Levels and technical/vocational certifications. Tertiary education offers bachelor’s (pass and honours), master's, MPhil, and PhD degrees across various disciplines, though only 6% of the population held university degrees as of 2007, with plans to increase this. The education sector faces challenges like low attendance (66% primary net attendance), inadequate funding (around 2% of GDP), especially for higher education, and quality concerns including over-reliance on rote learning and insufficient practical skills training.

Gender disparity is pronounced, with significantly lower female enrolment and literacy rates due to socio-cultural barriers, inadequate infrastructure, and perceptions limiting female education and employment, despite some progress over recent decades. Teacher training is inadequate, often knowledge-based rather than practical, with shortages in qualified teachers and poor facilities impacting education quality. Madrassas play a significant role, particularly in underserved areas, focusing primarily on Islamic education but also serving poor families by providing food and shelter. Non-formal and informal education, including NGO initiatives, attempt to fill gaps left by the formal system. During the COVID-19 pandemic, distance learning tools like Teleschool were launched to increase access, though rural and poor students face connectivity issues.

Prominent Pakistani academics and scientists, such as Nobel laureate Abdus Salam and chemist Atta-ur-Rahman, highlight the country’s potential despite systemic problems. University rankings have improved with institutions like NUST gaining regional recognition. Ongoing efforts to increase education expenditure and reform curricula aim to address Pakistan’s educational challenges, but political, economic, and societal factors complicate progress.
