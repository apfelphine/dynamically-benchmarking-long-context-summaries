## Education in Pakistan: Key Features and Challenges

Pakistan’s formal education system comprises primary (years 1–8), secondary (years 9–12), and tertiary levels. Only about 67.5% of children complete primary education, with net attendance below the world average. Secondary education involves standardized exams for certificates (SSC and HSSC), while alternative qualifications like O/A Levels and vocational tracks exist. Tertiary education is accessed by a small percentage (about 6% as of 2007), offering a mix of professional and academic degrees with accrediting councils for various fields. Degree programs range from two-year Pass to four-year Honours bachelor's, with postgraduate opportunities including MPhil and PhD. Teacher education is criticized for lacking application and qualified trainers, contributing to a shortage of teachers and outdated curricula. Non-formal education, vocational centers, apprenticeships, and a network of madrassas supplement the formal system, often filling gaps where government schools are lacking.

The education system faces major challenges, notably chronic underfunding (about 2% of GDP is spent, among the world's lowest), deteriorating infrastructure, and significant gender disparity, particularly for women and rural populations. Quality concerns are prevalent, with rote learning dominating and professional skills lagging. Literacy rates remain low compared to neighboring countries, with 2023 census figures at 60.65% overall, and stark differences by gender and region. Reforms and policies have aimed to address these gaps, but progress is slow. Religious education is deeply entwined in both state schools and madrassas, with curriculum content at times reinforcing social biases and excluding minorities. Despite these challenges, Pakistan has produced notable scholars and professionals, and ongoing reforms seek to improve accessibility, equity, and quality across the educational landscape.
