**Education in Pakistan**

Pakistan's education system is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a strong influence from the English system. Primary education spans 8 years (Grades 1–8), including pre-school (Play Group, Nursery, Kindergarten), and is marked by a curriculum covering Arts, Computer Studies, General Science, Modern Languages, Mathematics, Religious Education (Islamic Studies), and Social Studies. Single-sex education is common, though co-education exists in cities. The system faces challenges including low enrollment (67.5% completion rate), gender disparity (especially in rural areas), and outdated content due to teacher shortages and outdated curricula.

Secondary education begins at Grade 9 and lasts for four years, culminating in the Secondary School Certificate (SSC), followed by the Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC). Students take standardized exams (SSC-I and SSC-II) in core subjects, with a focus on a mix of compulsory and elective courses. The HSSC leads to entry into intermediate programs such as pre-medical, pre-engineering, or commerce, with students choosing from multiple streams. Alternative qualifications like GCE, IGCSE, and AP are available, primarily in private schools.

Tertiary education includes a range of degrees: Bachelor's (Pass or Honours), professional degrees (e.g., MBBS, BSc Engineering), and teacher education programs. Teacher training is a critical area, with programs offering associate degrees and bachelor's degrees in education, though they are often criticized for being knowledge-based and lacking practical application. Pakistan plans to increase university graduation rates from 6% (2007) to 10% by 2015 and 15% by 2020.

Non-formal education includes vocational training, technical schools, and apprenticeships, with institutions like PBTE, KPKBTE, and SBTE offering technical diplomas (e.g., DAE). Madrassas, though widespread, especially among poorer families, provide religious instruction and are often criticized for promoting extremist ideologies. The system faces gender disparities, with female enrollment and literacy rates lagging behind males (42% vs. 67% literacy), and cultural barriers limiting female access to education and leadership roles.

Education expenditure is around 2% of GDP, with a proposed target of 7% by 2015, though this has been criticized as unrealistic given historical underinvestment. Infrastructure is deteriorating, with over 3,000 government school buildings declared unsafe in Punjab. The curriculum often emphasizes rote learning, with limited relevance to modern societal needs. Despite reforms, quality remains low, and access to education is unequal, particularly in rural and marginalized communities.

The system also includes a complex relationship with religion, where Islamic content dominates public education, and religious textbooks are criticized for promoting negative stereotypes of minorities and a militarized worldview. Literacy rates have improved (from 43.92% in 1998 to 60.65% in 2023), but disparities persist, especially among women and in rural areas. Gender parity has improved slightly, but structural inequalities remain. The system is undergoing reforms to improve quality, equity, and relevance, though challenges in teacher training, infrastructure, and access continue.
