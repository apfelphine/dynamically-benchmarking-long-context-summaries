## Education in Pakistan: Key Features and Challenges

Education in Pakistan is structured across primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with formal schooling beginning with pre-school and progressing through primary (years 1–5), middle (years 6–8), secondary (years 9–12), and post-secondary education. The national curriculum is inspired by the English system, offering subjects such as sciences, mathematics, languages, religious studies, social studies, and Pakistan studies. The language of instruction varies between English and Urdu, and regional languages and foreign languages are offered in some institutions. Alternative education tracks include vocational, technical, and religious schools (madrassas), with madrasas prevalent in poorer communities. The state also supports non-formal and informal education initiatives, alongside distance learning options like Teleschool and Allama Iqbal Open University.

Despite progress in enrollment and literacy, Pakistan faces chronic challenges: low government expenditure—about 2% of GDP, significantly below policy targets and neighboring countries; deteriorating school infrastructure; persistent gender disparities (female literacy and enrollment rates lag male rates, especially among lower-income groups); and quality issues rooted in outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and a reliance on rote learning. Teacher education reform and technical/vocational training are recognized as crucial for economic development, yet face implementation issues. Religious content in the curriculum is pervasive, with minorities and women often marginalized. As of the 2023 census, total literacy is about 60.65%, with stark disparities among provinces and between rural/urban areas. Although education is legally a right for children aged 5–16, accessibility, quality, and equity remain pressing concerns.
