**Education in Pakistan**  

Pakistan's education system is structured into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Primary education spans six years (grades 1–6), followed by middle school (grades 6–8). Secondary education begins at grade 9 and lasts four years, culminating in the Secondary School Certificate (SSC) and Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC). Students can choose from streams such as pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities, commerce, or computer science. Alternative qualifications like GCE, IGCSE, and A-levels are available through British examination boards.  

Technical education is offered via the Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) system, providing diplomas in engineering, agriculture, and other fields. The system includes a three-year Diploma of Associate Engineering (DAE) equivalent to grade 12.  

Tertiary education includes bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees. Most programs are offered by public universities, with private institutions also present. Bachelor's degrees typically take four years, while professional degrees like medicine and law require five to six years. Teacher education programs are available, though they face criticism for being knowledge-based and lacking practical skills.  

The system faces significant gender disparities, with women having lower enrollment and literacy rates than men. Female students often face cultural and economic barriers, and despite progress, women remain underrepresented in higher education and leadership roles.  

The quality of education is often criticized due to outdated curricula, teacher shortages, and poor infrastructure. Public spending on education is around 2% of GDP, with plans to increase it to 7%.  

Religious education is prominent, with Islamic studies required in all schools. The curriculum has been criticized for promoting intolerance and negative stereotypes of minorities.  

Literacy rates have improved, rising from 43.92% in 1998 to 60.65% in 2023, though rural areas still lag behind.  

The education system also includes non-formal and informal education, including madrassas—Islamic seminaries that provide religious instruction and are widely accessible, especially in rural areas.  

Distance learning and digital education are being promoted, especially during the pandemic, with initiatives like Teleschool and Allama Iqbal Open University (AIOU) providing accessible education.  

Notable alumni include Nobel laureate Abdus Salam, scientist Atta-ur-Rahman, and computer prodigy Arfa Abdul Karim Randhawa.  

Despite challenges, Pakistan continues to develop its education system, with reforms aimed at improving access, equity, and quality.
