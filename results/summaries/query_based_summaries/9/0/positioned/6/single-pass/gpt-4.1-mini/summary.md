## Education in Pakistan

Education in Pakistan follows a formal structure inspired by the English system, comprising pre-school (Play Group, Nursery, Kindergarten), primary (years 1–5), middle (years 6–8), secondary (years 9–12), and tertiary education. Primary education covers core subjects including arts, sciences, languages (Urdu, English, regional languages), mathematics, religious and social studies, but only about 67.5% of children complete this level, with a 66% net primary attendance rate as of 2009. Secondary education (years 9–12) involves national standardized exams resulting in Secondary School Certificate (SSC) after year 10 and Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC) after year 12, with multiple academic streams available. Alternative qualifications such as GCE (O and A Levels) and technical education exist, offering vocational diplomas like DAE. Tertiary education includes professional degrees (engineering, medicine, law, etc.) and general bachelor’s degrees, although only 6% of the population had university degrees as of 2007, with planned increases by 2020.

The education system faces significant gender disparities, with females lagging behind males in enrollment, literacy, and academic participation due to socio-cultural barriers and infrastructure challenges. Despite some improvements, female education remains limited, particularly in rural areas. Quality issues persist, including outdated curricula, teacher shortages, inadequate training, and ineffective teaching methods heavily reliant on rote learning. Religious seminaries (madrassas) serve many underprivileged children but have isolated and religiously focused curricula. Recent efforts include distance learning initiatives (e.g., Teleschool during COVID-19), technical and vocational education expansions, and reforms targeting teacher education. Pakistan spends around 2% of GDP on education with ambitions to raise this but faces long-term challenges in funding stability and educational development.
