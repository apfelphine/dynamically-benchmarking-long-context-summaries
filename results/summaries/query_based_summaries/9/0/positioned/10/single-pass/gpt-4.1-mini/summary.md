# Education in Pakistan

Education in Pakistan follows a structure inspired mainly by the English system, beginning with pre-school education (ages 3–5) consisting of Play Group, Nursery, and Kindergarten. Primary education spans years 1 to 5, followed by middle school (years 6 to 8), where co-education is more common in cities but single-sex education is generally preferred. The core curriculum covers arts, sciences, languages (including Urdu and English), mathematics, religious studies, social studies, and Pakistan studies, with some schools offering extracurricular topics, foreign languages, and regional languages. As of 2009, net primary school attendance stood at about 66%, and only 67.5% of children finish primary school. Secondary education (years 9 to 12) culminates in national examinations administered by regional Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education, awarding Secondary School Certificates (matriculation) and Higher Secondary School Certificates (intermediate). Alternative certifications such as the British GCE O and A Levels, IGCSE, and AP programs are also available, especially in private education.

Tertiary education requires completion of higher secondary education, after which students pursue bachelor's degrees (often lasting two to four years), professional degrees (engineering, medicine, law, etc.), and postgraduate programs (master’s, MPhil, PhD). Teacher education varies, with critiques on limited faculty qualifications and focus on memorization rather than application. Vocational and technical education is part of the system, with programs spanning multiple years. Gender disparity remains a significant issue, especially in rural areas, with cultural, economic, and infrastructural barriers limiting female enrollment and retention despite some progress. Religious schools (madrasas) have a notable presence, often supplementing the public education system, but sometimes promoting rote learning and conservative ideologies. The literacy rate in Pakistan was 60.65% as of 2023, with higher rates in urban and northern regions. Public expenditure on education hovers around 2% of GDP, with official targets to increase but facing challenges. Distance learning initiatives like Teleschool emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic to address educational access issues, particularly among disadvantaged populations. Despite challenges, Pakistan has produced notable figures in science, technology, and economics who have achieved national and international acclaim.
