## Education in Pakistan: Key Features, Challenges, and Trends

Education in Pakistan is structured into primary (years 1–5), secondary (years 6–10), intermediate (years 11–12), and tertiary levels. The system is inspired by the English model, with instruction in Urdu or English, and includes both public and private institutions. As of 2023, the national literacy rate is 60.65%, with significant provincial and district disparities—higher rates in urban districts like Islamabad and Lahore and lower in areas such as Balochistan and Gilgit-Baltistan. Youth literacy rates are higher, exceeding 85% in some districts. Education expenditure has historically been low and unpredictable, with public spending ranging from less than 2% up to 2.8% of GDP, despite policy proposals to raise it to 7%. Infrastructure challenges persist, highlighted by thousands of unsafe school buildings in Punjab.

There is pronounced gender disparity in education; although improvements have occurred, only 18% of women have completed ten years of schooling, and the female literacy rate remains at 42% compared to 67% for men. Socio-cultural norms, lack of infrastructure, poverty, and insufficient government oversight are major obstacles, further exacerbated in rural areas. While some progress has been made through legal reforms—such as making primary education a right—dropout rates and access issues, especially for girls and marginalized groups like transgender individuals, remain persistent. Technical and vocational education as well as distance learning options like Teleschool and Allama Iqbal Open University are expanding but face challenges related to access and teacher training.

The quality of education is affected by outdated curricula, teacher shortages, inadequate facilities, and overemphasis on rote learning. Education is deeply intertwined with religion, with many madrasas offering religious instruction along with limited secular subjects, particularly in areas with failing public provision. Universities are expensive and often not aligned with market needs, hampering graduates' employment. International and private schools are increasing, with 439 international schools reported in 2015. Pakistan has produced globally recognized scientists and scholars, but systemic issues in governance, financing, gender equity, and educational relevance remain significant barriers to broader advancement.
