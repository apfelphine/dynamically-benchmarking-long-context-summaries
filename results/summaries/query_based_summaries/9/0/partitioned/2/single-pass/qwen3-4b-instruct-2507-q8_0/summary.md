### **Achievements in Education in Pakistan**

Pakistan has produced several globally recognized scientists and scholars, highlighting its contributions to science and education:
- **Abdus Salam**, a theoretical physicist and Nobel laureate in physics, was the first Pakistani to win a Nobel Prize. He significantly advanced Pakistan's standing in theoretical physics.
- **Atta-ur-Rahman**, a prominent scientist in natural product chemistry, has authored over 1,200 research papers and holds a high h-index. He has received numerous national and international awards, including the Nishan-i-Imtiaz and the UNESCO Science Prize.
- **Ayub Ommaya**, a neurosurgeon, invented the Ommaya Reservoir, a medical device used to deliver drugs to patients with brain tumors.
- **Mahbub-ul-Haq**, an economist, co-developed the Human Development Index (HDI), a key international standard for measuring human development.
- **Ismat Beg**, a mathematician, is a Distinguished National Professor and has made significant contributions to fixed point theory and multicriteria decision-making.
- **Arfa Abdul Karim Randhawa**, a young computer prodigy, became the youngest Microsoft Certified Professional (MCP) at age nine and received the President's Award for Pride of Performance. A science park in Lahore is named after her.
- **Dr. Naweed Syed**, a Canadian scientist, is the first to connect brain cells to a silicon chip.
- **Nergis Mavalvala**, a Pakistani-American astrophysicist, played a key role in the first detection of gravitational waves and has received prestigious awards like the MacArthur Fellowship and the Lahore Technology Reward.

### **Education Expenditure and Policy**

Pakistan's education expenditure stands at approximately **2% of GDP**, significantly below the proposed **7% target** set in 2009. Critics argue that achieving this target is unrealistic due to decades of underinvestment, inconsistent public spending, and political instability. Despite the policy, public expenditure has remained stagnant or decreased in 21 out of 37 years since 1972. Experts suggest that education must be treated as a special, protected sector—immunized from fiscal stress and political instability—to achieve growth.

### **University Rankings**

Pakistan has seen modest progress in university rankings:
- In 2018, **NUST** ranked **91st** in the QS Asia University Rankings.
- As of 2022, **six universities** were ranked in the top 1,000 QS Asia University Rankings: NUST (#358), Quaid-i-Azam University (#378), PIEAS (#398), LUMS (#651), UET Lahore (#801), and University of the Punjab (#801).

### **Religion and Education**

The relationship between religion and education in Pakistan is complex and deeply rooted in societal dynamics:
- Due to underfunding and poor infrastructure, many families turn to **madrasas** (Islamic religious schools) for education, especially in rural areas. These schools often provide free food, shelter, and religious instruction, though they focus on rote memorization and have been associated with extremist interpretations of Islam.
- Religious content is embedded in the national curriculum, especially in the 1980s with the Islamization of education. This has led to resistance from educators and scientists who argue that scientific theories like evolution are often rejected on religious grounds, despite some teachers acknowledging scientific consensus.
- School textbooks frequently contain **negative stereotypes of Jews and Israel**, omit the Holocaust, and promote a religiously charged narrative of Pakistan as a global leader in opposition to the West.
- Religious minorities face lower literacy rates, with Christians in Punjab at 34%, and Hindus at 34% (upper caste), compared to the national average of 46.56%.

### **Literacy Rate**

Pakistan’s literacy rate has improved over time:
- In **1951**, the literacy rate was **17.9%**.
- By **1998**, it reached **43.92%**, and in **2023**, it rose to **60.65%**.
- The rate varies by province:
  - **Punjab**: 66.25% (2023)
  - **Sindh**: 57.54% (2023)
  - **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa**: 51.09% (2023)
  - **Balochistan**: 42.01% (2023)
- **Districts with literacy rates ≥75%** include Islamabad, Karachi Central, Rawalpindi, Gujrat, Jhelum, Karachi East, Korangi (Karachi), Lahore, Sialkot, Chakwal, and Abbottabad.
- **Youth literacy rate (15–24 years)** is higher, with 13 districts at 85% or above, including Upper Chitral (95%), Abbottabad (92%), and Gujrat (90.90%).

### **Gender Disparity**

Gender disparity remains a significant challenge:
- Only **18% of women** have received 10 years or more of schooling.
- The gender ratio in enrollment improved from **0.47 (1990–91)** to **0.74 (1999–2000)** at the primary level and from **0.42 to 0.68** at the secondary level.
- Despite progress, **men dominate** the education sector, with a **1100:1000 male-to-female ratio** in teaching.
- Women face systemic barriers, including lower literacy rates (42% vs. 67% for men), limited access to education, and cultural norms that prioritize male education.
- Women in medicine face additional challenges, including a lack of maternity leave and systemic sexism, despite comprising 80% of medical college students.

### **Qualitative Challenges**

- The quality of education is declining due to outdated curricula, lack of qualified teachers, poor infrastructure, and over-reliance on rote learning.
- Teachers often lack training in human rights and critical thinking, and many hold conservative beliefs that align with national Islamic ideology.
- Technical and vocational education is underdeveloped and lacks modern curricula and training facilities.
- Distance learning, such as **Teleschool**, has been introduced during the pandemic, but internet access remains a barrier, especially in rural areas.
- Teacher satisfaction is higher in special education institutions, where emotional intelligence and life satisfaction are greater.

### **Technical and Vocational Education**

- TVET (Technical and Vocational Education and Training) is promoted as a way to prepare students for employment, offering courses in electronics, healthcare, and business.
- Challenges include outdated curricula, lack of teacher training, and insufficient investment in infrastructure and programs.

### **Non-Formal and Informal Education**

- Madrassas are widespread, especially among poor families, providing free food, shelter, and religious instruction. However, they are criticized for promoting rote learning and extremist ideologies.
- Informal education includes apprenticeships and training by private companies or NGOs, especially in technical fields.

### **International Education**

- Pakistan has **439 international schools**, offering curricula in English and other international programs.
- Students from Pakistan study abroad through government scholarships, with over 5,000 receiving support to study in the U.S., U.K., Canada, and Australia.

### **Conclusion**

Pakistan has notable achievements in science and education, with several Nobel laureates and prominent scientists. However, the system faces persistent challenges including underfunding, gender disparity, outdated curricula, and religious bias in education. Despite policy efforts to increase spending and improve access, structural and cultural barriers continue to limit educational equity and quality. Addressing these issues requires comprehensive reforms, increased investment, and a shift toward more inclusive, modern, and equitable education systems.
