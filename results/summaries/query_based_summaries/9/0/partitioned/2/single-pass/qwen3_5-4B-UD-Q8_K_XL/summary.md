Education in Pakistan is characterized by significant challenges, including chronic underfunding, inadequate infrastructure, and low literacy rates. Public expenditure on education has historically hovered around 2% of GDP, falling short of the government's 2009 policy target of 7%. This lack of investment has led to deteriorating school conditions, with thousands of government buildings declared unsafe, and a curriculum that is often outdated, overcrowded, and criticized for relying on rote memorization rather than critical thinking. The system faces severe gender disparities, particularly in rural areas where cultural norms and safety concerns limit female enrollment and academic performance. While progress has been made in increasing female literacy and university enrollment, the sector continues to struggle with teacher shortages, a lack of qualified professionals, and a disconnect between the education system and market needs.

The Pakistani education system is diverse, encompassing a mix of English and Urdu-medium public schools, private institutions, and a significant presence of madrasas (Islamic seminaries) that serve as a crucial, albeit sometimes controversial, alternative in impoverished regions. Tertiary education is expanding, with a goal to increase the percentage of university graduates from roughly 6% to 15% by 2020, though access remains limited by high costs and a lack of skilled faculty. Alternative pathways include technical and vocational training, distance learning initiatives like Allama Iqbal Open University, and international curricula such as GCE and IB. Despite the presence of globally recognized achievements in science and technology, the national education landscape is defined by ongoing efforts to modernize infrastructure, reform teacher training, and address deep-seated issues of equity and relevance.
