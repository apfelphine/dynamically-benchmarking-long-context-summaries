Pakistan’s education system faces significant challenges, particularly in **funding, infrastructure, and quality**. Education expenditure remains low, at around **2% of GDP**, with a 2009 national education policy aiming for 7%—though past spending trends show stagnation and unpredictability. Despite this, **universities have gained recognition**, with NUST (Islamabad) ranked among the top 100 in QS Asia University Rankings (2018), and six universities ranked in the top 1000.

**Literacy rates** are low, at **60.65%** (2023), with disparities between provinces and genders. Rural areas and lower-income groups face greater barriers, while urban areas like Karachi and Lahore have higher literacy rates. **Gender disparity** persists, with women’s education significantly underrepresented, though progress has been made in recent decades. For example, **primary enrollment improved by 57.44%** for girls between 1990–2000, and **secondary enrollment saw a 67.5%** increase in the same period.

**Education infrastructure** is deteriorating, with over **3,000 government school buildings in Punjab declared structurally unsafe** due to the 2025 monsoon season, despite repeated assessments. This reflects broader issues of **underinvestment and neglect**, particularly in rural and underfunded areas.

**Religious influence** is deeply embedded in the curriculum, with Islamization during the 1980s shaping education. While **madrasas** (Islamic schools) cater to some families due to limited access to state schools, they often focus on rote memorization and conservative interpretations of Islam. **Science and evolution** are sometimes resisted due to religious objections, though most teachers accept evolution in theory.

**Higher education** remains limited, with only **6% of Pakistanis holding university degrees** (2007). The system is criticized for **lacking relevance to modern needs**, with universities not providing marketable skills. Teacher training programs are also criticized for being **knowledge-based rather than application-oriented**, and many institutions lack qualified faculty.

**Vocational and technical education** is underdeveloped, though efforts are being made to improve it. **Distance learning** has gained traction, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, with initiatives like the **Teleschool** program providing educational content via television and radio.

**International rankings** reflect Pakistan’s progress, with universities like NUST and Quaid-i-Azam University gaining visibility. However, **systemic issues**—such as **poor infrastructure, gender disparities, and underfunding**—continue to hinder educational equity and quality. The government’s push for **7% education expenditure by 2015** remains unfulfilled, highlighting the need for sustained investment and reform.
