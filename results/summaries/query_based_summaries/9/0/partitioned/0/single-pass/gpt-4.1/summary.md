## Education in Pakistan: Overview and Key Issues

Education in Pakistan is structured into stages: pre-school (ages 3–5), primary (years 1–5), middle (years 6–8), secondary (years 9–12, including SSC and HSSC exams), tertiary (university-level), and includes informal, non-formal, technical/vocational, and religious (madrassa) education. The curriculum is commonly modeled on the English system, and instruction occurs in Urdu or English, with regional and foreign languages also taught in some institutions. The education system struggles with significant challenges, notably low completion rates (67.5% for primary, with a national primary net attendance rate of 66%), outdated curricula, shortages of qualified teachers, underfunding (typically just 2% of GDP), and poor infrastructure—especially in rural areas. Access issues, teacher absenteeism, and a focus on memorization over critical skills further hamper quality and equity.

Gender disparity remains a pronounced issue, with female literacy and educational attainment lagging behind males, especially in poor households and rural regions. While progress has been made in female enrollment, there are still cultural, infrastructural, and economic barriers. As of 2023, overall literacy stands at 60.65% (male: 68%, female: 52.84%), with urban literacy higher than rural. The education system is also deeply intertwined with religion, impacting both public and madrassa education. Reforms have introduced free and compulsory education for children aged 5–16 and expanded distance learning modalities (such as Teleschool), particularly highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Pakistan has produced notable achievements and alumni in science and technology fields, yet university rankings remain modest compared to global standards. Overall, chronic underinvestment, persistent gender gaps, and systemic quality issues continue to limit the country's educational development.
