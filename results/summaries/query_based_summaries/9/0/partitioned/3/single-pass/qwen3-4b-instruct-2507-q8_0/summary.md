**Education in Pakistan**  

Pakistan's education system is characterized by significant gender disparities, with males outperforming females in enrollment, literacy, and access to education. Literacy rates, at 60.65% (2023), are lower than neighboring South Asian countries, with women's literacy at 52.84% compared to men's 68%. The system faces challenges such as poor infrastructure, teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and a lack of relevance to modern needs.  

Primary education spans 6 years (ages 6–12), followed by a 4-year secondary education (ages 12–16), culminating in the Secondary School Certificate (SSC) and Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC). Students can pursue academic streams (pre-medical, pre-engineering, humanities) or vocational training. Tertiary education includes bachelor's, master's, and PhD degrees, with a growing emphasis on science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM).  

The education system is heavily influenced by religious content, particularly in rural areas, where madrasas provide free education and often emphasize rote memorization. This has led to criticism over the suppression of scientific inquiry and the promotion of extremist ideologies.  

Despite notable achievements—such as Nobel laureate Abdus Salam in physics and prominent scientists like Atta-ur-Rahman and Nergis Mavalvala—systemic issues persist. Government expenditure on education remains low, around 2% of GDP, with a national policy target of 7% yet to be achieved.  

Efforts to reform education include the introduction of English as a compulsory subject from primary school, curriculum modernization, and increased investment in teacher training. However, cultural, economic, and structural barriers—especially gender-based discrimination and rural-urban disparities—continue to hinder equitable access and quality outcomes.  

In summary, Pakistan’s education system is marked by progress in key areas, but remains constrained by deep-rooted social, economic, and infrastructural challenges.
