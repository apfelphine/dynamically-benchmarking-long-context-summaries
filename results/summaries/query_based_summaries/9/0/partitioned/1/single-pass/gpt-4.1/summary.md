## Education in Pakistan

Education in Pakistan is divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a formal system inspired by the English educational system. Primary schooling consists of pre-school through year 5, followed by middle school (years 6–8), and secondary schooling (years 9–12), culminating in standardized national examinations for certificates. Tertiary education comprises university and professional degree programs, though only about 6% of Pakistanis are university graduates. Multiple alternative qualifications exist, such as British O and A Levels. Technical and vocational education options are available, with boards overseeing diplomas and associate engineering programs. Teacher training programs have grown but face criticism for their emphasis on memorization rather than application and inadequate preparation.

Key challenges in Pakistan’s education sector include chronic underfunding (usually below 2% of GDP), poor infrastructure, insufficient qualified teachers, outdated curricula, and substantial gender disparity—only 18% of women have received 10 years or more of schooling, and literacy rates differ significantly by gender and region. Religious education via madrasas is prevalent, especially among Pakistan’s poorest, and religion also shapes public school curricula. Literacy rates are low compared to other South Asian countries, with marked urban-rural differences and some districts achieving much higher rates than others. Inclusive education remains limited, with marginalized groups and minorities facing persistent barriers. Despite reforms, access and quality remain major issues, and Pakistan’s achievement in university rankings and international schools is growing but still relatively limited.
