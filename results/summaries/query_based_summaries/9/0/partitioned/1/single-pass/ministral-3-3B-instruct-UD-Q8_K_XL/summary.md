Pakistan’s education system faces significant challenges, particularly in **gender disparity** and **education quality**. Despite recent improvements, gender inequality persists, with only **18% of Pakistani women** having completed 10+ years of schooling, and a **gender enrollment ratio (F/M)** improving modestly from 0.47 in 1990–91 to 0.74 in 1999–2000. Progress has been seen in primary and secondary education, but disparities remain pronounced, especially in rural areas, due to cultural norms, economic constraints, and limited access to infrastructure. Women’s enrollment in STEM fields remains low, with many women doctors facing systemic barriers, including lack of maternity leave and career support.

The **quality of education** in Pakistan is declining, marked by outdated curricula, underqualified teachers, and insufficient resources. Teacher training programs lack professionalism, and many schools suffer from overcrowding and outdated facilities. The system also struggles with **vocational education**, where technical and vocational training (TVET) is underdeveloped, despite its importance for economic growth. Distance learning initiatives, such as Teleschool and Allama Iqbal Open University (AIOU), have expanded access but remain limited due to rural internet access issues.

**Literacy rates** are improving but remain low, with **60.65% of the population literate** (2023), though disparities persist between urban and rural areas, and among different provinces. The **Ministry of Education** has introduced reforms, including free primary education and the **Millennium Development Goals (MDGs)**, but progress has been slow due to political instability, funding shortages, and cultural resistance. Madrasas, which offer free education and shelter, remain influential in rural areas, though they often prioritize rote learning over critical thinking.

**Teacher education** is a critical area for reform, with many educators lacking professional training and qualifications. The system also faces issues like **teacher absenteeism**, poor working conditions, and insufficient in-service training. Additionally, **gender discrimination** in education persists, with women often excluded from advanced education and professional roles due to societal norms and limited opportunities.

Overall, while Pakistan has made strides in improving education access, systemic issues—including gender inequality, poor infrastructure, and underfunding—continue to hinder progress. The government’s recent focus on **TVET, digital education, and teacher training** offers hope, but sustained investment and policy reforms are essential to address long-standing challenges.
