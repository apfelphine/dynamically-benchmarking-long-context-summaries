# Education in Pakistan

**Gender Disparity:**  
Pakistan faces significant gender disparity in education, particularly among the poorest households. Female enrollment in primary education is low, with only 18% of women having 10 years or more of schooling. Despite progress in enrollment ratios—rising from 0.47 to 0.74 in primary and 0.42 to 0.68 in middle school—the gender gap persists. Female literacy stands at 42% compared to 67% for men. Cultural norms, parental preference for educating boys, lack of infrastructure, and limited access to education contribute to this imbalance. Women face systemic barriers in teacher roles, with a 1100:1000 male-to-female ratio, and are underrepresented in STEM fields. Female university students face gender discrimination, and the absence of female university leaders underscores the issue.

**Qualitative Dimension:**  
The quality of education in Pakistan is declining due to teacher shortages, outdated curricula, and poor infrastructure. The system emphasizes rote learning, lacks relevance to real-world needs, and provides limited professional and communicative skills. Government reforms, such as the Convention on the Rights of the Child and the Sustainable Development Goals, aim to improve access and quality, including free and compulsory education for children aged 5–16. However, implementation remains inconsistent.

**Teacher Education:**  
Teacher training programs are underfunded and lack qualified professionals. In Balochistan, many universities lack professors and PhDs in education. Teachers face knowledge gaps in human rights and pedagogical skills. Teacher education reforms are needed to improve curriculum, infrastructure, and training, with a focus on reflective writing and case studies to promote human rights awareness.

**Technical and Vocational Education (TVET):**  
TVET is vital for workforce development but faces challenges in access, curriculum relevance, and teacher training. Despite efforts to integrate TVET with academic streams, it remains underdeveloped. The system aims to equip students with practical skills, including financial literacy and health education, but implementation is inconsistent.

**Distance Learning:**  
Distance learning, such as through Allama Iqbal Open University (AIOU), provides access to education, especially for women in remote areas. During the pandemic, Teleschool was launched to deliver lessons, though internet access remains a barrier for rural students.

**Achievements:**  
Pakistan has produced notable alumni in science, technology, and education, including Nobel laureate Abdus Salam, physicist Atta-ur-Rahman, and neurosurgeon Ayub Ommaya. These achievements reflect the country’s potential in science and innovation.

**Education Expenditure:**  
Public spending on education is around 2% of GDP, falling short of the proposed 7% target. The lack of consistent funding and investment in infrastructure, especially in rural areas, hampers educational outcomes.

**Religious and Cultural Influences:**  
Religious content dominates the curriculum, with Islamization of education since the 1980s. Textbooks often portray minorities negatively, and religious instruction is compulsory. This has led to criticism for promoting intolerance and limiting critical thinking.

**Literacy Rate:**  
The national literacy rate is 60.65% (2023), with significant regional disparities. Punjab leads at 71.98%, while Balochistan lags at 42.01%. Youth literacy rates are also lower, with only 85% in some districts. Literacy is closely tied to poverty, gender, and regional access.

**Policy and Reform:**  
The government has introduced policies to increase education spending and improve access, but challenges remain in implementation, teacher quality, and equity. Reforms are needed to address gender disparities, curriculum relevance, and teacher training.
