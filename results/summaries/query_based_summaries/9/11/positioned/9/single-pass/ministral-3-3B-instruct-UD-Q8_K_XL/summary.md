Poland’s education system is structured to ensure compulsory education for all children, emphasizing both academic and vocational pathways. **Compulsory education** begins at age six, with primary school (szkoła podstawowa) lasting eight years (grades 1–8). Previously, primary education was six years, followed by a three-year mandatory junior high school (gimnazjum), but this structure was abolished in 2017, merging the two into an eight-year primary school. After primary school, students can pursue either general secondary education (liceum) for four years, leading to the "Matura" exam for university admission, or vocational/technical education through schools like *technikum* (five years) or *zasadnicza szkoła zawodowa* (three years), granting vocational certificates.

**Tertiary education** follows the Bologna Process model, with most programs divided into a three-year bachelor’s degree and a two-year master’s degree. Doctoral studies (PhD) typically take three years. Teacher training programs require three years of study, while vocational education is handled by post-secondary schools (szkoła policealna) with programs lasting two and a half years. The grading system at university level ranges from 2.0 (fail) to 5.0 (excellent), with a 5.5 or 6.0 occasionally used for "exceeds expectations."

**Foreign languages** are a key component of the curriculum, with English being the most widely taught, followed by German, French, and Spanish. The education system has undergone reforms, including a 2017 overhaul that shifted from a 6+3+3 structure to an 8+4/5-year model, emphasizing core subjects and reducing vocational study delays. **Historically**, Poland’s education system was influenced by European models, with compulsory education introduced in 1919 and expanded under the Communist regime, though recent reforms aim to modernize and align with international standards.
