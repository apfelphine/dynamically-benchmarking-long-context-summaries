Education in Poland features a comprehensive and evolving system with a strong emphasis on compulsory education and structured progression through primary and secondary levels. Primary school (podstawówka) typically spans eight years (grades 1–8), replacing the previous six-year system with a three-year gimnazjum (middle school) in 2017. This reform, introduced by the 2016 "Law on School Education," extended primary education to better align with international standards and reduce early academic pressure.

Upper secondary education offers diverse pathways: general education in general secondary schools (liceum), which leads to the Matura exam for higher education, and vocational training in technical schools (technikum) or basic vocational schools (zasadnicza szkoła zawodowa). Students can also pursue specialized profiled secondary schools (liceum profilowane) in fields defined by the Polish Classification of Activities (PKD).

Tertiary education follows the Bologna process, with a standard three-year bachelor's degree followed by a two-year master's degree. Some programs, such as medicine and pharmacy, offer longer, unique cycles (five to six years). The grading system at the university level uses a numeric scale from 2.0 to 5.0, with 3.0 being the lowest passing grade and 5.0 the highest. Failures are common and typically corrected through retake exams.

Foreign language instruction is mandatory, with students required to study two foreign languages by the 2017 reform, including English and another language taught at least twice a week. The curriculum emphasizes a balance between core academic subjects and practical skills, with a focus on preparing students for both higher education and the workforce.

Historically, Poland's education system has been rooted in strong state involvement, with the establishment of the Commission of National Education in 1773 and the founding of the Jagiellonian University in 1364. Major reforms in 1999 and 2017 have modernized the structure, shifting from a rigid, centralized model to a more flexible, student-centered approach. The 2017 reforms, while criticized for some historical and curricular shifts, aim to improve equity and accessibility in education.

Violence in schools has been addressed through a "zero tolerance" policy introduced in 2006, which grants teachers legal status as civil servants and enables them to take disciplinary actions against aggressive students, including community service and fines for parents. This policy reflects a broader societal effort to ensure safety and accountability within the educational environment.
