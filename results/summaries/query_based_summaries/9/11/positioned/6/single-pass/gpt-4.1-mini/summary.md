## Education in Poland

In Poland, compulsory education typically begins at age six or seven, starting with primary school (szkoła podstawowa), which now lasts eight years (grades 1–8). This replaced the former system (prior to 2017) of six years of primary school followed by three years of junior high school (gimnazjum), which was abolished following a 2016 reform. Junior high school previously covered lower secondary education (three years) and ended with exams in humanities, science, and foreign languages. Upper secondary education follows compulsory schooling and includes general secondary schools (liceum) offering four years of study culminating in the "Matura" exam for university eligibility, as well as vocational and technical schools (technikum and zasadnicza szkoła zawodowa) focusing on trades and occupations. Technical schools last five years and also lead to the Matura, while basic vocational schools provide three years of vocational training with certificates of competence. Special and profiled schools serve students with handicaps or specific career tracks.

At the tertiary level, Poland adheres to the Bologna Process with most programs structured into a three-year bachelor’s degree followed by a two-year master’s degree; some master’s degrees (e.g., medicine, pharmacy) are offered through long-cycle programs of four to six years. Doctoral programs typically take around three years. University grading employs a numeric scale from 2.0 (fail) to 5.0 (highest), with half-point increments and some informal extended grades.

Polish students typically study one or two foreign languages, with English (67.9%) and German (33.3%) being the most common as of 2005/06. Educational reforms have brought changes in curriculum focus and school structure, with the 2017 reform emphasizing nationalist and Eurocentric historical perspectives and increasing length of primary education to eight years, alongside the extension of upper secondary schooling.

Historically, Poland has a rich educational tradition dating back to the 12th century, with the founding of the Jagiellonian University in 1364 – one of Europe’s oldest universities. Compulsory education laws were first introduced in 1919 across Polish territories following the partitions. Illiteracy rates dramatically declined post-World War II, with compulsory education now ending at age 18.

Poland also has measures addressing school violence; in 2006, reforms introduced a "zero tolerance" policy giving teachers civil servant status and legal protections against violence, along with disciplinary options against aggressive students and their parents.
