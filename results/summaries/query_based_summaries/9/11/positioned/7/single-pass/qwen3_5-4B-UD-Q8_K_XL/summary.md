**Education in Poland** follows a unified, state-centric model where compulsory education lasts until age 18. Historically divided into primary, junior high (*gimnazjum*), and upper secondary stages, the system underwent a major 2017 reform that abolished the *gimnazjum*, extending primary education to eight years (grades 1–8) and adjusting upper secondary school lengths. The curriculum is standardized nationally, with a strong emphasis on Polish language, history, and civic education, though recent reforms have sparked debate regarding historical narratives and nationalism. The university system operates under the Bologna Process, featuring a three-cycle structure of three-year bachelor's degrees, two-year master's degrees, and three-year doctoral programs. Poland has a rich history of higher education, anchored by the 1364-founded Jagiellonian University, and faces ongoing challenges regarding teacher shortages, infrastructure, and the integration of diverse student populations.

The Polish education landscape includes a mix of public, private, and special education institutions, with significant efforts to support students with disabilities through inclusive education models and specialized centers. Foreign language education is a key component, with English being the most widely taught subject. The system is characterized by centralized control through the Ministry of National Education, which sets curriculum standards and grading policies. Despite its historical prestige, the system faces criticism regarding the quality of teacher training, outdated curricula in some areas, and the need for modernization to better align with global educational standards and labor market demands.

**Education in Bangladesh** is characterized by a three-tier structure of primary, secondary, and tertiary education, managed by the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education and the Ministry of Education, respectively. The national curriculum, administered by the National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB), mandates 12 years of schooling and offers instruction in both Bengali and English, with the latter becoming increasingly prominent. A significant portion of the education system is delivered through alternative channels, including *madrasas* (Islamic seminaries), which account for a substantial share of enrollment, and international schools that follow global curricula. Compulsory education covers primary and secondary levels, but challenges such as low teacher retention, inadequate infrastructure in rural areas, and gender disparities persist. The tertiary sector is expanding rapidly with the rise of private universities, though public institutions remain the backbone of higher education.

**Education in New Zealand** features a highly decentralized system where the Ministry of Education sets national standards while individual schools maintain significant autonomy. The system is free for all citizens and permanent residents from age five to eighteen, with compulsory attendance required between ages six and sixteen. It is structured around 13 year levels, divided into primary (Years 1–8) and secondary (Years 9–13), with a strong emphasis on early childhood education through licensed centers and parent-led groups. A unique feature is the availability of Māori-medium education, ranging from immersion preschools (*kōhanga reo*) to fully Māori-language schools (*kura kaupapa*), aimed at revitalizing the indigenous language and culture. The system also includes various funding models, from government-funded state schools to private institutions, and has faced criticism regarding school enrollment schemes that can disadvantage lower socio-economic groups.

**Education in Canada** is a provincial responsibility, leading to significant variations across the 13 provinces and territories. Compulsory education typically begins at age five or six and ends between ages 16 and 18, with most students completing 12 years of schooling. The system is predominantly co-educational and publicly funded, though private schools exist, particularly in Quebec. A defining characteristic is the constitutional right to education in the minority language, ensuring publicly funded French schools in English-speaking provinces and vice versa. The curriculum focuses on core subjects like language arts, mathematics, and science, with provincial boards managing admissions and grading. Tertiary education is competitive, with universities relying heavily on academic performance and extracurricular achievements for admission, and the system faces challenges related to rising tuition costs and student debt.

**Education in Mexico** follows a structure of basic education (primary and junior high) and higher education, with compulsory attendance mandated up to age 15 or 16. The system has historically struggled with high illiteracy rates and a lack of infrastructure, particularly in rural areas, though recent decades have seen improvements in enrollment and literacy. The national curriculum is secular and state-controlled, aiming to promote social justice and modernization, though it has faced conflict with the Catholic Church. Higher education is structured similarly to the U.S. model with four-year bachelor's degrees (*Licenciatura*), two-year master's degrees (*Maestría*), and three-year doctorates (*Doctorado*). The system is plagued by issues such as a shortage of qualified teachers, outdated curricula, and a high cost of higher education that limits access for many students.

**Education in Pakistan** consists of a primary, secondary, and tertiary level system, heavily influenced by the English educational model. Primary education is compulsory, but completion rates remain low due to poverty, lack of infrastructure, and gender disparities. Secondary education is divided into secondary school (grades 9–10) and intermediate (grades 11–12), with students taking national board examinations to gain access to higher education. The system faces significant challenges, including a shortage of qualified teachers, outdated curricula, and a heavy reliance on rote learning. Tertiary education is highly competitive, with a strong preference for university degrees over vocational training, leading to a mismatch between the skills graduates possess and the needs of the labor market.

**Education in South Korea** is renowned for its rigorous, highly competitive, and centralized system, driven by a strong cultural emphasis on academic achievement and social mobility. The curriculum is standardized by the Ministry of Education, with a focus on core subjects like mathematics and science, often leading to intense pressure on students and high rates of stress and suicide. The system is characterized by the dominance of private tutoring institutions (*hagwons*) and a fierce competition for university spots, particularly at the prestigious "SKY" universities. While the country has achieved high literacy and high enrollment rates, the system is increasingly being reformed to include competency-based education and vocational training to address issues of over-education and youth unemployment.

**Education in Russia** is a unified system with a strong emphasis on state control and standardized testing. The structure includes preschool, primary, basic, and secondary general education, followed by professional and higher education. Compulsory education covers nine years, with an extension to eleven years in many regions. The system is characterized by a standardized grading scale and the Unified State Exam (USE), which determines university admissions and is seen as a way to ensure fairness across the vast country. Higher education has undergone significant reforms to align with the Bologna Process, introducing a two-tier system of bachelor's and master's degrees, though the traditional specialist model remains prevalent in many institutions. The system faces challenges related to the high number of universities, quality control, and the integration of diverse regional educational standards.
