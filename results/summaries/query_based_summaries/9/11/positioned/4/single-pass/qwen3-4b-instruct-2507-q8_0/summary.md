# Education in Poland

Poland's education system features a comprehensive structure with compulsory education beginning at age 6 and extending through primary and secondary levels. Primary school (podstawówka) was previously six years long (grades 1–6), followed by a three-year middle school (gimnazjum), but this was changed in 2017 by the "Law on School Education," which extended primary education to eight years (grades 1–8) and abolished middle schools. Upper secondary education begins after primary school and offers two main pathways: general education in general secondary schools (liceum), which leads to the Matura exam and grants access to higher education, and vocational or technical education through technical schools (technikum) or basic vocational schools (zasadnicza szkoła zawodowa), which provide vocational training and a certificate of competence. Students from basic vocational schools can pursue the Matura after an additional two or three years of study.

Tertiary education in Poland follows the Bologna model, consisting of a three-year bachelor's degree followed by a two-year master's degree. Some programs, such as pharmacy and medicine, offer longer cycles (five to six years). Doctoral programs last about three years. The grading system at the university level uses a numeric scale from 2 to 5, with 2 being a failing grade and 5 the highest. A grade of 3.0 is the lowest passing grade. Students may retake failed courses, and the system is flexible, allowing for multiple attempts.

Foreign languages are a key component of the curriculum, with English being the most widely taught. By 2005/06, 67.9% of students studied English, followed by German (33.3%), French (13.3%), and Spanish (10.2%). The education reform introduced by Minister Katarzyna Hall required students to study two foreign languages, with English taught three times a week and a second language taught twice a week. The reform also introduced different levels of language exams to determine eligibility for upper secondary education.

The history of education in Poland dates back to the 13th century, with the founding of the Jagiellonian University in 1364. In the 19th century, compulsory education was introduced, and by 1921, one-third of the population was illiterate. A major reform in 1999 restructured the system to include 8 years of primary school, 4 years of secondary school, and a focus on core subjects. The 2017 reform further extended primary education and shifted the focus to a more holistic curriculum, though it has been criticized for removing historical perspectives on non-alignment and communism.

The education system also includes special and practical schools for students with disabilities, which offer tailored support and vocational training. In 2019, the government launched a "zero tolerance" policy to address school violence, giving teachers the legal status of civil servants and allowing for disciplinary actions against violent behavior. The policy also enables schools to send aggressive students to community service and allows for parental fines.

The Polish education system emphasizes national identity, with a curriculum that includes historical and political content. However, critics argue that the new curriculum promotes a narrow, nationalist perspective and neglects the historical roles of lower social classes. The system has faced criticism for its religious content and for placing excessive emphasis on rote learning and standardized testing, which may hinder critical thinking and creativity. Despite these concerns, the system continues to improve, with a growing focus on inclusive education and the integration of students with special needs.
