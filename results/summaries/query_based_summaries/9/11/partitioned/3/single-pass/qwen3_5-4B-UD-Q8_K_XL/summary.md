Education in Poland has a long history of state intervention, evolving from the introduction of compulsory schooling in 1919 to a modernized system that emphasizes high-quality instruction and international competitiveness. The structure is primarily defined by a 6-3-3 model of compulsory education (six years of primary school, three years of junior high, and three years of senior high), though a 2017 reform reverted this to an 8-4 structure, extending primary education and adjusting the senior high level. Compulsory education concludes at age 18, and the system is characterized by a distinction between "school" and "education," with the latter encompassing all learning activities. The curriculum is heavily regulated, featuring a strong emphasis on history and a recent shift in foreign language requirements under the Hall reform, which mandates two foreign languages for lower secondary students.

At the tertiary level, Poland adheres to the Bologna Process, utilizing a two-cycle system of three-year bachelor's degrees followed by two-year master's degrees, alongside specialized long-cycle programs for fields like medicine and pharmacy. The country has seen significant academic success, with its OECD rankings for reading and science rising to the top 10. The system also includes a robust vocational sector through technical schools and basic vocational schools, which provide specific trade qualifications. While the state provides the framework, there is a growing presence of private institutions, and ongoing reforms aim to address issues such as teacher quality, infrastructure, and the integration of modern educational philosophies to maintain its status as a central hub of higher learning in Central Europe.
