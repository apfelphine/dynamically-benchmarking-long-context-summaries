# Education in New Zealand

Education in New Zealand has a rich history beginning with Māori wānanga, which focused on traditional knowledge and were led by tribal elders. Formal European-style education was introduced in the early 19th century by missionaries, initially taught in Māori. The country established a free, secular, compulsory national education system from 1878. Early childhood education is widely attended and delivered through various centre-based and parent-led services, all required to follow the national curriculum framework, Te Whāriki.

Primary and secondary education is free and compulsory between ages 6 and 16, with school years numbered 1 to 13 (typically from age 5 to 17). Primary education covers Years 1–8, including intermediate years 7 and 8, which may be taught in various school types. Secondary education covers Years 9–13. All state and state-integrated schools follow national curricula (The New Zealand Curriculum for English-medium and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa for Māori-medium schools), with qualifications like NCEA, Cambridge, or IB at senior levels. The schooling system includes state schools (government-funded and secular), state-integrated schools (former private schools retaining a special character and charging compulsory attendance dues), and private schools (funded mainly through tuition). Enrolment schemes manage admissions in high-demand schools, balancing community rights and school capacity.

Māori language education has evolved from suppression in the early 20th century to a resurgence through immersion schools (Kura Kaupapa Māori) and inclusion in English-medium curricula. Studies show Māori students in Māori-medium education perform better academically compared to mainstream schooling. Manual and technical training was historically separate but has since been integrated into mainstream curricula.

Tertiary education encompasses a wide range of post-school learning, regulated by the New Zealand Qualifications Framework. Funding for primary, secondary, and tertiary education partly depends on government allocations with additional support through voluntary donations or compulsory dues in integrated schools. Special needs students receive ongoing funding to support inclusive education. Tertiary students often rely on government loans and allowances, with ongoing debates about funding adequacy.

New Zealand's educational standards have faced criticism, especially in mathematics performance, though broader assessments sometimes rank the country favorably. Persistent issues include addressing achievement gaps for Māori and Pacific students and combating school bullying, which remains a concern despite governmental initiatives.
