# Education in New Zealand

Education in New Zealand encompasses early childhood, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with distinctive features shaped by history, language, funding, and school types.

## Historical and Language Context  
Māori traditional education, conducted in Māori language through elders (tohunga) at wānanga, was key before European contact. Formal European-style education began in 1815, primarily in English with missionary influence. A systematic shift towards English-medium instruction for Māori children started after the 1867 Native Schools Act, leading to a decline in Māori language use in schools by 1960. However, revitalization efforts from the 1970s onward led to Māori language immersion programs and official recognition of Māori as an official language. Māori-medium education is available via kōhanga reo (pre-schools), kura kaupapa Māori (immersion schools), and Māori language courses in mainstream schools. Despite stable recent enrollments, Māori-language education forms a small but significant part of the system.

## School Structure and Types  
Primary education typically covers Years 1–8, and secondary education Years 9–13, though configurations vary and include contributing primary, full primary, intermediate, secondary, composite (Years 1–13), and special schools. Alternatives include home-schooling and distance education via correspondence schools. 

State schools are government-funded and free, while state-integrated schools are former private or religious schools now funded by the government but charging attendance dues for property costs. Private schools receive less government funding and charge significant tuition fees. Enrolment zones regulate admission in many state schools to manage capacity, prioritizing local residence and siblings.

## Funding and Resources  
Government funding for state and state-integrated schools is allocated per student, with additional support for smaller or lower socio-economic (low-decile) schools. Tuition and compulsory attendance dues apply to state-integrated and private schools. Parents are also asked for voluntary donations and bear costs for uniforms, trips, and stationery. Teacher salaries in state and integrated schools are centrally paid. Special needs students receive Ongoing Resource Scheme (ORS) funding according to their level of need.

Tertiary education funding combines government subsidies and student fees, with access to student loans and allowances. Concerns exist regarding rising fees and underfunding leading to skills shortages.

## Educational Standards and Challenges  
International assessments revealed New Zealand students lagging in numeracy and mathematics since the mid-1990s, raising concerns and prompting reform efforts. Māori and Pacific Island students typically perform below Pākehā students, highlighting a need to close achievement gaps.

## Schooling Times and Curriculum  
The school year runs from late January to mid-December, divided into four terms with two-week breaks. The daily school schedule generally starts between 8–9 am and ends around 3 pm. The curriculum incorporates academic and practical skills; manual and technical training was historically introduced in the early 20th century and is currently part of the education framework.

## Summary  
New Zealand’s education system balances historical cultural legacies, particularly Māori language and education, with a state-funded school network and emerging challenges in academic achievement and equity. Government funding mechanisms support diverse school types, with special provisions for disadvantaged and special needs students. Tertiary education is widely accessed but concerns about affordability and funding persist.
