## Education in New Zealand – Key Features

Education in New Zealand encompasses early childhood, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, with a strong tradition of free, compulsory, and secular schooling established from 1878. Early childhood education is provided through a mix of teacher-led and parent-led centers, and all licensed early childhood services must follow Te Whāriki, the national curriculum. Primary and secondary education is free for New Zealand citizens and permanent residents and compulsory from ages 6 to 16, though most start at age 5 and continue until 17. The school year runs from late January to mid-December, divided into four terms. Schools are classified by funding type: state (public), state-integrated (typically religious or special philosophy schools with “attendance dues”), and private schools, with most students attending state schools.

Primary and secondary schooling comprises 13 year levels (Years 1–13), with various school types including full/contributing primaries, intermediates, secondary, area/composite schools, and specialized schools for distance, special needs, and teen parents. State schools follow a national curriculum (NZC for English-medium, Te Marautanga o Aotearoa for Māori-medium), with eight core learning areas and optional language study. Senior students pursue national qualifications like the NCEA, while some schools offer Cambridge International or International Baccalaureate programs. Māori language and culture have seen significant revival and integration, including kōhanga reo preschools and kura kaupapa Māori schools. Funding for schools is government-based, with additional sources such as voluntary donations and attendance dues. Tertiary education features a mix of universities and institutes, funded by a combination of government subsidies and student fees, with support available via student loans and allowances.

## Additional Context – Educational Standards and Challenges

New Zealand’s approach emphasizes inclusivity, offering provisions for home-schooling and specialist education for students with disabilities. While national initiatives seek to address disparities, especially for Māori and Pacific Island students, achievement gaps remain. National and international assessments have highlighted concerns about mathematics and literacy standards. The education system has experienced challenges with bullying and calls for more consistent anti-bullying policies. Tertiary funding and high student fees have sparked debates about accessibility and graduate retention. Significant focus has been placed on revitalizing Māori language and culture within education, particularly through immersion programs and curriculum reform, contributing to improved outcomes for Māori learners enrolled in Māori-medium settings.
