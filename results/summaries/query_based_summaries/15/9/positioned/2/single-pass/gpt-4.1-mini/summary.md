The National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), founded in 1910 by Justo Sierra, was established as a secular and national institution merging Mexico City's decentralized colleges, distinct from the earlier Royal and Pontifical University of Mexico. Early challenges included political instability from the Mexican Revolution and opposition to government control. Key milestones include gaining autonomy in the 1920s, the creation of its coat-of-arms by José Vasconcelos symbolizing Latin American unity, and the construction of its consolidated main campus, Ciudad Universitaria, in the 1950s—now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The university has a rich history of student activism, including strikes and protests notably in 1968, and has expanded with satellite campuses across Mexico and abroad.

UNAM is one of the largest universities globally, with over 324,000 students spanning undergraduate to graduate levels, organized into numerous schools offering both undergraduate and graduate degrees. It is highly research-oriented with many premier institutes covering diverse disciplines, responsible for significant scientific output within Mexico. The university’s athletic programs are well-established, including the notable Club Universidad Nacional in professional football. Culturally, UNAM is a bastion of Latin American identity, symbolized by its eagle and condor seal and traditions like its Day of the Dead offerings. It has produced three Mexican Nobel laureates and a number of influential figures across politics, arts, science, and business, maintaining a vibrant role in education, research, culture, and social activism in Mexico.
