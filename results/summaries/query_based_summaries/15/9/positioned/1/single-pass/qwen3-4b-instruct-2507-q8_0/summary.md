The National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM) was founded on September 22, 1910, by Justo Sierra, then minister of education in the Porfirio Díaz regime, as a secular, national institution to modernize higher education in Mexico. It merged the Fine Arts, Business, Political Science, Jurisprudence, Engineering, Medicine, Normal, and National Preparatory schools into a single university, aiming to replace the outdated and Church-affiliated Royal and Pontifical University of Mexico. The new university faced political challenges due to the ongoing Mexican Revolution and struggled with resistance to its autonomy, leading to student strikes and administrative changes. In 1921, José Vasconcelos became rector and created the university's coat of arms and motto, "The Spirit shall speak for my people," which emphasized cultural and educational unity. UNAM's main campus, Ciudad Universitaria, was established in the 1950s, replacing scattered buildings in Mexico City. The university has grown significantly, with over 324,000 students, and has played a key role in advancing education, research, and social inclusion, including the integration of women into its academic programs and the promotion of minorities in technology. UNAM is consistently ranked as Mexico's top university and one of the top 10 in Latin America, with strong research output and international recognition. It is known for its contributions to science, particularly in artificial intelligence, and for its vibrant student life, including cultural traditions, political activism, and a rich tradition of student-led movements. The university has produced numerous notable alumni, including three Nobel laureates, and has a strong emphasis on academic excellence, research, and social responsibility.
