## Cornell University Summary

Cornell University, founded in 1865 by Ezra Cornell and Andrew Dickson White, is a large, residential, research-intensive institution with its main campus in Ithaca, New York. Operating on a 4–1–4 academic calendar, it is accredited by the Middle States Commission on Higher Education. Cornell comprises both endowed and statutory colleges, making it the only Ivy League school that is also a land-, sea-, and space-grant institution. The university is recognized for academic excellence, routinely ranking among the top institutions globally (#10 U.S., #12 world—QS 2024; #20 world—THE 2024), with particular distinction in architecture, business, landscape architecture, and international relations.

Cornell has highly competitive undergraduate admissions, with a 6.9% acceptance rate for the Class of 2026, and demonstrates commitment to diversity and accessibility through need-blind admissions for U.S. applicants and programs supporting first-generation and underrepresented minority students. Financial aid initiatives include replacing loans with scholarships for lower-income families and pledges to match grant aid offers from peer institutions.

The university has extensive research operations ($1.18 billion R&D expenditures in 2021, ranked 14th nationally), is classified as an “R1: Doctoral Universities – Very high research activity,” and is renowned for advances in engineering, physical sciences, agriculture, and medicine. International programs include partnerships, study abroad opportunities, and joint degree programs with universities worldwide. Cornell's academic resources include the 13th-largest academic library in the U.S. and the influential Cornell University Press. The campus features a vibrant student life with over 1,000 organizations, a strong tradition in athletics, Greek life, and diverse residential options. Prominent alumni and faculty include Nobel laureates and global leaders, with more than 250,000 living alumni and a legacy of innovation and public engagement.
