Cornell University is a large, primarily residential research university located in Ithaca, New York, founded in 1865 by Ezra Cornell and Andrew Dickson White as the state's land-grant institution. It operates on a 4-1-4 academic calendar and is classified as an R1 doctoral university with very high research activity. The university is distinguished by its land-, space-, and sea-grant status, which drives its extensive cooperative extension service and agricultural research. Its main campus in Ithaca features a mix of architectural styles, including Collegiate Gothic, Victorian, and modernist structures, and is surrounded by the 2,800-acre Cornell Botanic Gardens. Cornell maintains additional facilities in New York City, including Cornell Tech on Roosevelt Island, and has established the Weill Cornell Medical College in Qatar as its first U.S. medical school outside the United States.

Cornell is renowned for its significant contributions to science, technology, engineering, and philosophy. It ranks highly in national and international assessments, particularly in engineering, natural sciences, and architecture. The university's research portfolio includes leadership in high-energy physics, nuclear physics, and the development of the first U.S. cyclotron, as well as the operation of the Arecibo Observatory. Academically, it boasts a diverse student body with strong representation from underrepresented minorities and a need-blind admission policy for U.S. applicants. Financially supported by a $10.7 billion endowment, Cornell is known for its philanthropy and financial aid initiatives, ensuring broad access to education without athletic scholarships. The institution also produces notable alumni in fields ranging from politics and literature to medicine and business, including Pearl S. Buck, Toni Morrison, and Barbara McClintock.
