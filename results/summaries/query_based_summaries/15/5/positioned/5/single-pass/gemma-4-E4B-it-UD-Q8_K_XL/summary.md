Cornell University was founded on April 27, 1865, by Ezra Cornell and Andrew Dickson White, becoming New York's land-grant institution. It was formally inaugurated on October 7, 1868, with 412 male students enrolling. Cornell developed as a technologically innovative institution, incorporating academic research into its infrastructure, and became one of the first U.S. universities to be electrified in 1883.

The university's structure features 16 colleges, including four statutory colleges (College of Agriculture and Life Sciences, College of Human Ecology, School of Industrial and Labor Relations, and College of Veterinary Medicine) and nine privately endowed, non-statutory colleges. Cornell's main campus is in Ithaca, New York, and is situated in the Finger Lakes region. The university maintains a substantial endowment of $10.7 billion, and its academic profile includes having 62 Nobel laureates among its faculty members. Cornell also operates globally, including Weill Cornell Medical College in Qatar and through partnerships in India, China, and Singapore.
