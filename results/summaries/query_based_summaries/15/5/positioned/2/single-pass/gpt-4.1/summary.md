## Cornell University Summary

Cornell University, founded in 1865 by Ezra Cornell and Andrew Dickson White in Ithaca, New York, is an Ivy League institution and New York State’s land-grant university. Its main campus covers over 745 acres and features a blend of historic and modern architecture, with several buildings listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Cornell also operates prominent campuses in New York City—Weill Cornell Medical College and Cornell Tech on Roosevelt Island—and an international medical campus in Qatar. The university’s organizational structure includes 16 colleges and schools (seven undergraduate, seven graduate/professional, and two in New York City), comprising both privately endowed and state-supported statutory colleges.

Recognized for rigorous academics and research, Cornell is accredited by the Middle States Commission on Higher Education and is classified as a “R1: Doctoral Universities – Very high research activity”; it ranked 14th nationally in R&D expenditures in 2021 and holds a $10.7 billion endowment. Cornell’s research and academic reputation are global, with high rankings in architecture and engineering, business, and international relations. Admission is extremely competitive, with a 6.9% acceptance rate for the Class of 2026. The student body is economically and racially diverse, supported by extensive financial aid programs. With more than 250,000 alumni, including Nobel laureates, heads of state, and influential leaders in multiple fields, Cornell remains a leader in education, research, sustainability, and innovation, exemplified by its commitment to carbon neutrality by 2035 and sustainability initiatives such as solar farms and geothermal systems.
