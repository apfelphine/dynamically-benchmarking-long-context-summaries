The **King’s Scholar** program originates from the establishment of King’s Scholars at the University of Cambridge in 1714, though the formalized scholarships were first established in 1317 under King Edward II. These scholars were selected to study at Cambridge under the care of a master, funded by the king’s expenses. The scholarships were later expanded to include King’s Scholars at Eton College, where they were awarded a Foundation Scholarship and admitted to a house called "College," housing students aged 13 to 18. King’s Scholars at Eton are distinguished by a black academic gown and are required to maintain a high academic standard, with full waivers of school fees historically provided, though this has shifted to means-tested support since the 1960s.

At Eton, King’s Scholars live in the central College building, share academic and extracurricular activities with Oppidans (non-scholars), and participate in formal traditions unique to the College. They are recognized by the post-nominal letters **KS** and are granted privileges such as attending College Hall for meals and participating in ancient traditions. King’s Scholars at Eton are also known as "Collegers" or colloquially as "tugs," derived from the Latin *gens togata*, meaning "toga'd people." The program has historically emphasized academic excellence and moral character, though it has faced scrutiny over its legacy and the exclusion of certain groups.
