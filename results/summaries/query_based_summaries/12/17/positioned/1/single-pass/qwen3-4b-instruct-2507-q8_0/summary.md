**King's Scholar** refers to a prestigious academic designation with historical roots in England and the United States, primarily associated with elite educational institutions.

In **Eton College**, a King's Scholar (also known as a "Colleger" or "tug") is a student selected through competitive examination, admitted to the central "College" building, and distinguished by wearing a black academic gown. Historically, the scholarship was established in 1440 by King Henry VI to provide free education to poor, scholarly boys. The term evolved, and by the 19th century, the scholarship became a mark of distinction, with students receiving full tuition coverage and a gown as a symbol of their status.

In **King's College, Cambridge**, a King's Scholar is a student who achieves a first-class degree and receives a prize of £350. They are entitled to attend a formal ceremony in the King's College Chapel and participate in a three-course dinner with their Director of Studies.

At the **seven King's Schools** (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester), founded by Henry VIII, the King's Scholarship is awarded to students who demonstrate academic or musical excellence. These scholarships are often accompanied by a discount on school fees and historically granted to all pupils, though the tradition has evolved to recognize achievement.

The **King's Scholars** at Eton and other institutions are known for their strong academic performance, leadership potential, and engagement in formal traditions, including dining in College Hall and participating in school events. The designation is deeply tied to institutional identity and legacy, with notable alumni including prominent figures in politics, law, science, and the arts.

The term is distinct from other scholarship programs and is rooted in historical and institutional traditions, emphasizing both academic excellence and a long-standing commitment to public service and leadership.
