The **King's Scholar** is a prestigious academic title with historical roots in British education. It was first established in 1317 by King Edward II at the University of Cambridge, where 12 boys were sent to study under a master, with their expenses covered by the king. This tradition evolved into a formal selection process, with the term being applied to students at Eton College, who are chosen through a competitive examination and awarded a Foundation Scholarship. These scholars, known as "Collegers" or "tugs," live in a central boarding house (College), wear a distinctive black academic gown, and are distinguished by their academic excellence and commitment to service. 

At Eton, the King's Scholar status is maintained through rigorous academic and personal standards, with about 70 scholars admitted annually. The tradition continues with the selection of King's Scholars at other institutions, such as Westminster School (where the title is used for students when a reigning monarch is present) and King's College Cambridge, where scholars are awarded a prize for achieving first-class degrees. Additionally, the seven King's Schools in England—Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester—award King's Scholarships based on academic or musical excellence, with scholars receiving privileges such as special rights to attend cathedral services and the right to be buried in cathedral grounds. 

The King's Scholar designation is thus a long-standing tradition of academic distinction, rooted in royal patronage and institutional commitment to excellence, service, and leadership.
