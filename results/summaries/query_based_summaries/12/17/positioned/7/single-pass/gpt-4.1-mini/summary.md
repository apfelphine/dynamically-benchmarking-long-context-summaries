# Summary: King's Scholar

The title "King's Scholar" originated on 7 July 1317 when King Edward II established the first King's Scholars at the University of Cambridge by sending 12 boys from his household to study there. Later, in 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charitable institution to educate "poor, scholarly boys," increasing the number of King's Scholars to about 70 by 1443. At Eton College, King's Scholars—also called "Collegers" or colloquially "tugs"—are students who have passed a competitive College Election examination, awarded a Foundation Scholarship, and admitted into College, the school’s original house. They wear a distinctive black academic gown and typically number about 70, admitted roughly 14 per year. While they share most school activities with other students (Oppidans), King's Scholars have unique privileges such as dining in College Hall and exclusive traditions.

Beyond Eton, the "King's Scholar" designation exists at other historic schools such as Westminster School, where foundation scholars are called King's Scholars or Queen's Scholars depending on the monarch, selected by examination and housed in College. At King’s College Cambridge, "King’s Scholar" refers to undergraduates achieving first-class degrees who receive recognition including a formal signing ceremony and banquet. The seven King's Schools in England, re-endowed by Henry VIII, award King's Scholarships based on academic or musical merit with associated fee reductions and traditional privileges. Specific schools like King's Ely and King’s School Canterbury have their own traditions for King's Scholars involving ceremonies, special attire, and lifelong affiliation with their respective cathedrals.
