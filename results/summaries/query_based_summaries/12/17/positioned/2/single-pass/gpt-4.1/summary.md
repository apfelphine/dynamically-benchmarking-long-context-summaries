## King's Scholar: Origins, Traditions, and Institutions

The title "King's Scholar" has its origins in 1317 when King Edward II sent 12 boys from his household to study at Cambridge, initiating the concept. In 1440, Eton College was founded by Henry VI for "poor, scholarly boys," and from 1443, 70 King's Scholars were housed in College, Eton’s central building. Today, Eton King's Scholars (“Collegers” or "tugs") are admitted through competitive examination, receive a Foundation Scholarship, and live in College, wearing distinctive black academic gowns. Historically, the scholarship offered a full fee waiver, though now it is means-tested. King's Scholars have access to unique traditions and privileges at Eton, such as dining in College Hall and participating in the Wall Game, with a post-nominal "KS" in school lists.

The status of King's Scholar extends beyond Eton. At Westminster School, foundation scholars are called King's or Queen's Scholars depending on the monarch, and at King's College Cambridge, undergraduates who achieve a first-class degree are designated King's Scholars, receive a monetary prize, and participate in special ceremonies. Seven King's Schools in England, re-endowed by Henry VIII, award King's Scholarships for academic or musical excellence—these scholarships confer specific traditions and privileges, including ceremonial rights within their respective cathedrals.

## Modern Practices and Notable Alumni

King's Scholarships are now often awarded based on academic or musical merit, sometimes with fee reductions or special privileges. Institutions such as King's Ely and King's School Worcester hold formal admissions and ceremonies for Scholars, who may gain lifelong association with their school's cathedral, special gowns, and the right to be married or buried within its grounds. King's Scholars at Eton and other schools have included numerous distinguished individuals in politics, science, literature, and public service, such as Boris Johnson, John Maynard Keynes, Harold Macmillan, Timothy Gowers, Aldous Huxley, and Stephen Wolfram, highlighting the historical prestige and enduring legacy of the King’s Scholar designation in British education.
