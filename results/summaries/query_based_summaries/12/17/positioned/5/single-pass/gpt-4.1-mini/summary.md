## King's Scholar Summary

The title "King's Scholar" originated in 1317 when King Edward II sent boys from his household to study at the University of Cambridge, supported by royal funding. In 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charity school, establishing King's Scholars as academically distinguished students who received free education and boarding in the central college buildings. At Eton, King's Scholars—also called "Collegers" or "tugs"—are selected through a competitive exam and form a distinct, historic house known as College, with about 70 Scholars at a time. They wear a distinctive black academic gown over their uniform and partake in specific traditions, including dining in College Hall and playing the traditional Wall Game.

Beyond Eton, the King's Scholar designation is used in other historic British schools such as Westminster School (where scholars are called King's or Queen's Scholars depending on the monarch) and the King's Schools in Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester. There, the scholarship recognizes academic or musical excellence, often offering fee reductions and ceremonial privileges, such as the right to wear gowns during services and lifelong membership in associated cathedral foundations. Additionally, at King's College Cambridge, undergraduate students achieving first-class degrees are called King's Scholars, receiving financial awards and ceremonial recognition tied to the college’s royal origins.
