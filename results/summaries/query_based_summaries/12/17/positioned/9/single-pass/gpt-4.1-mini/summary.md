# Summary of Information on "King's Scholar"

The institution of King's Scholars dates back to 1317, when King Edward II established the first King's Scholars at the University of Cambridge, sending 12 boys from his household to study there at royal expense. Later, in 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charity school for "poor, scholarly boys," increasing the number of King's Scholars to 70 by 1443. These Scholars, known as "Collegers" or colloquially "tugs," reside in the historic central College building and wear distinguishing black academic gowns over their uniforms. Admission to this prestigious group is through a College Election examination, and traditionally, about 14 new King's Scholars join annually at age 13. Though originally scholarships provided a full fee waiver, since the 1960s financial aid has become means-tested. King's Scholars at Eton enjoy unique traditions and modest distinctions, including the post-nominal letters KS.

Beyond Eton, the term "King's Scholar" (or "Queen's Scholar," depending on the monarch’s gender) is used for foundation scholars at Westminster School, selected by competitive examination, residing in "College," and wearing gowns in Abbey services. At King's College Cambridge, "King's Scholar" refers to undergraduates receiving first-class degrees, who participate in ceremonial events tied to the college's Founder's heritage. Additionally, seven historic King's Schools (e.g., King's Ely, King's School Canterbury, King's School Worcester) award King's Scholarships, often accompanied by fee discounts and traditional privileges such as admission to the cathedral foundation, wearing distinctive gowns or ties, participation in ceremonies, and lifelong membership benefits including burial rights in cathedral grounds. These scholarships recognize academic or musical excellence and maintain longstanding historical traditions linked to royal foundations.
