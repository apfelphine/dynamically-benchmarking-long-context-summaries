The King's Scholar is a prestigious academic title with historical roots in the United Kingdom. It originated in 1317 when King Edward II established the first King's Scholars at the University of Cambridge, sending 12 boys to study under a master. The tradition evolved, and by 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College, initially known as the King's College of our Lady of Eton, to provide free education to poor, scholarly boys. At Eton, a King's Scholar (or "Colleger") is selected through a competitive examination, admitted to the central College building, and distinguished by wearing a black academic gown. The title is also used at King's College Cambridge, where undergraduates who obtain first-class degrees are awarded the title and receive a prize of £350, with an opportunity to attend a ceremony in the King's College Chapel. Additionally, the seven King's Schools in Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester, re-endowed by Henry VIII in 1541, award King's Scholarships to students based on academic or musical attainment. These scholarships often come with a discount on school fees and are accompanied by privileges such as access to cathedral services and special traditions. The King's Scholar title is thus a multi-faceted honor with deep historical and institutional significance across several British educational institutions.
