# King's Scholar

The term "King's Scholar" refers to students admitted to King's College at the University of Cambridge, Eton College, and the seven King's Schools in England, distinguished by their academic or musical achievement and historical connection to the reigning monarch. At King's College Cambridge, the title is awarded to undergraduates who attain first-class degrees; they receive a prize of £350 and attend a signing ceremony in the King's College Chapel, a tradition originating from endowments by King Henry VI. Similarly, at the seven King's Schools (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester), the scholarship is awarded based on exceptional academic or musical attainment, often accompanied by fee discounts. These institutions historically awarded scholarships exclusively to boys, but Queen Elizabeth II authorized the award of Queen's Scholarships in 1973 to include girls, reflecting the schools' transition toward co-education.

Eton College's King's Scholars, known as "Collegers" or "tugs," are selected through a competitive College Election examination and admitted to the school's original house, the College. Approximately 14 scholars are admitted annually at age 13, ranging in age from 13 to 18. Historically, they received a Foundation Scholarship that fully waived school fees, though this has been subject to means testing since the 1960s. Unlike other scholars, King's Scholars are entitled to unique privileges, including the right to marry in the cathedral where the school is located, be buried in its grounds, and wear specific red ties with golden crowns during services. At King's School Ely, the tradition includes the annual "Hoop Trundle," a race along the cathedral's east lawn, while at Worcester, the Senior Scholar demands a day's holiday from the presiding judge during a ceremony known as Judge's Day.
