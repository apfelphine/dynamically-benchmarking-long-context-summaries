# King's Scholar

King's Scholar is a prestigious title and scholarship associated with several British educational institutions, most notably Eton College, Cambridge University, and a network of King's Schools. Originally established in 1317 by King Edward II at the University of Cambridge, the concept has evolved to denote high academic achievement. At Eton College, a King's Scholar (often called a "Colleger") is a student admitted to the historic College house after passing a competitive examination, receiving a Foundation Scholarship, and wearing a distinctive black academic gown. These scholars, who have been admitted since the school's founding, are treated as family members by the founder and enjoy unique privileges such as access to the cathedral and the right to marry and be buried within its grounds.

The term also applies to undergraduates at King's College Cambridge who achieve first-class degrees, receiving a prize and signing their names in the King's Scholar book, a tradition rooted in Henry VI's original endowments. Additionally, the King's Schools (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester) award scholarships in recognition of academic or musical attainment, often accompanied by fee discounts. In 1973, Queen Elizabeth II authorized these schools to also award Queen's Scholarships to female students. While the King's Schools originally awarded endowment-funded scholarships, they now generally grant the title based on merit, though some still offer fee reductions. The distinction highlights a tradition of excellence and historical continuity across British education.
