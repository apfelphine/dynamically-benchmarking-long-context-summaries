The King's Scholar is a prestigious academic designation with historical roots in the United Kingdom. Established in 1317 by King Edward II at the University of Cambridge, it initially comprised 12 boys from the royal household. In 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charitable school for "poor, scholarly boys," establishing the tradition of King's Scholars, who are selected through competitive examinations and reside in the central College building. At Eton, King's Scholars (also known as "Collegers" or "tugs") wear a black academic gown and are distinguished by their academic and social privileges, including meals in College Hall and participation in traditions such as the Wall Game. 

The designation is also used at other institutions. At Westminster School, King's Scholars are selected through competitive exams and wear gowns during services in Westminster Abbey. At King's College Cambridge, undergraduates who achieve first-class degrees receive the title of King's Scholar and attend a formal ceremony. The seven King's Schools in England (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester) award King's Scholarships to students based on academic or musical excellence, often with a discount on fees, and these scholars are granted special privileges such as participation in cathedral services and burial rights within the cathedral grounds. The program emphasizes service to society, rooted in the motto "Return to the Society what you have gained from it," and continues to support students through scholarships, community service, and leadership development.
