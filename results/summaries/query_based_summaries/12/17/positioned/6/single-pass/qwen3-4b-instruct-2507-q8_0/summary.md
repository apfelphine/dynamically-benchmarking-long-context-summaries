The **King's Scholar** is a prestigious academic title and scholarship awarded to students at several institutions in the UK. At **Eton College**, it is given to students who pass a competitive examination and are admitted into the College (a residential house), wearing a black academic gown. The program, established in 1440 by King Henry VI, selects about 70 students annually, aged 13–18, who share aspects of school life with other students but eat in College Hall and participate in traditions like the "Hoop Trundle." The scholarship is initially provided without tuition fees, though it is now subject to means testing. Students are referred to as "tugs" due to their gowns.

At **Westminster School**, the title is awarded to students who pass a competitive examination and are admitted to the oldest house, College, and wear gowns during services. The program is named "King's Scholars" when a king is reigning.

At **King's College Cambridge**, the term refers to undergraduates who earn first-class degrees and receive a £350 prize and attend a ceremony in the college chapel, where they sign their names in the King's Scholar book. This tradition stems from the college's founding by Henry VI, who is also the namesake of Eton College.

At the **seven King's Schools** (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, Worcester), founded by Henry VIII in 1541, the King's Scholarship is awarded to students based on academic or musical achievement, often accompanied by a discount on school fees. Since 1973, these schools have also awarded Queen's Scholarships to girls. The scholarships are accompanied by privileges such as the right to marry in the cathedral or be buried within its grounds.
