## Summary: King's Scholar

The title "King's Scholar" originated on 7 July 1317 when King Edward II sent twelve boys from his household to study at the University of Cambridge. Later, in 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charity school for "poor, scholarly boys," increasing the number of King's Scholars there to about 70. At Eton, King's Scholars ("Collegers" or "tugs") are students who have passed a competitive examination to receive a Foundation Scholarship and reside in the original college buildings known as "College." Distinguished by their black academic gowns worn over a tailcoat uniform, about 14 new scholars join yearly at age 13. They participate fully in school life but have unique traditions, privileges, and live in historic central boarding houses.

The post-nominal letters "KS" denote King's Scholars at Eton, while academically distinguished fee-paying students are "Oppidan Scholars" (OS). Fees for King's Scholars were traditionally fully waived but are now means-tested, though scholarships no longer directly reduce fees. The concept of King's (and Queen's) Scholars also exists at Westminster School, where scholars board in the oldest house called College and wear gowns during Abbey services. At King's College Cambridge, the term applies to undergraduates obtaining first-class degrees, who receive monetary prizes and participate in formal college ceremonies. Additionally, the seven historic King's Schools (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester) award King's Scholarships in recognition of academic or musical merit, often with fee reductions and traditional privileges, including lifelong cathedral foundation membership and burial rights in cathedral grounds.
