## King's Scholar – Concise Summary

The title "King's Scholar" was historically established in 1317 by King Edward II at Cambridge, and became closely associated with prestigious British schools such as Eton College, Westminster School, and King's College Cambridge. At Eton, King's Scholars (Collegers or "tugs") are students who pass the College Election examination and receive a Foundation Scholarship, entitling them to residence in the College house. Traditionally numbering 70, they are distinguished by wearing black academic gowns, participating in unique traditions, and enjoying privileges such as dining in College Hall. Originally, the scholarship provided full fee waivers, but since the 1960s, means testing has applied and financial benefit varies. King's Scholars have appended the post-nominal KS to their names and historically constitute a distinct and academically select group, with many notable alumni.

The King's Scholar system was adopted at other institutions including Westminster School, King's College Cambridge (where first-class undergraduates earn the title), and the seven King's Schools re-founded by Henry VIII. These schools now award King's Scholarships based on academic or musical achievement, often accompanied by discounted fees and privileges such as special ceremonial roles, the right to marry or be buried in associated cathedrals. The term and the attendant customs thus signify historic academic distinction, institutional tradition, and membership in a prestigious scholarly community.
