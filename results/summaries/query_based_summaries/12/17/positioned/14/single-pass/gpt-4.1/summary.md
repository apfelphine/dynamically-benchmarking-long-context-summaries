## King's Scholar Summary

The title "King's Scholar" originated in 1317 when King Edward II established the first group at the University of Cambridge, and later expanded in 1440 with the founding of Eton College by King Henry VI. At Eton, King's Scholars (also known as "Collegers" or "tugs") earn a Foundation Scholarship by passing an entrance exam, reside in the historic College building, and traditionally wear a black academic gown. The group maintains several unique traditions, such as eating meals in College Hall, playing the Wall Game, and participating in ancient formal ceremonies. Although the Foundation Scholarship once provided a full waiver of school fees, it is now means-tested and may not cover fees for all scholars. King's Scholars are distinct from "Oppidan Scholars", who are recognized for academic achievement but enjoy fewer privileges.

The King's Scholar designation exists beyond Eton: at Westminster School, foundation scholars are titled King's or Queen's Scholars depending on the monarch, awarded via a competitive exam, and reside in College. At King's College, Cambridge, undergraduates who earn first-class honors become King's Scholars, gaining modest prizes and ceremonial recognition. The tradition is also maintained at seven King's Schools refounded by Henry VIII, where King's (and since 1973, Queen's) Scholarships are awarded for academic or musical merit, often accompanied by fee reductions and privileges connected to the associated cathedrals, such as the right to marry or be buried there. Notable King's Scholars have included prime ministers, distinguished academics, writers, and leaders.
