## Summary of Information on "King's Scholar"

The title of **King's Scholar** originated in 1317 when King Edward II sent 12 boys from his household to study at the University of Cambridge, funding their expenses. Later, in 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charity school for "poor, scholarly boys," establishing King's Scholars who lived in the College building, distinct from fee-paying students known as Oppidans.

At **Eton College**, a King's Scholar (or "Colleger," colloquially "tug") is a student who passes a competitive examination called the College Election and is awarded a Foundation Scholarship, admitted into the historic house known simply as "College." Around 70 King's Scholars, aged 13 to 18, reside in this original school area and wear a distinctive black academic gown over their uniform—a tradition linked to their nickname. King's Scholars share most school activities with other students but have unique privileges, such as dining in College Hall and traditional formal customs. Though originally the scholarship fully waived school fees, since the 1960s it has been increasingly means-tested. Scholars append "KS" to their names in school lists, while distinguished non-scholar pupils are Oppidan Scholars ("OS").

Notable **Eton King's Scholars** include several prominent figures across politics, literature, science, and the arts, such as Prime Ministers Boris Johnson and Harold Macmillan, economist John Maynard Keynes, philosopher A. J. Ayer, and writer George Orwell (Eric Blair).

Besides Eton, the designation of King's or Queen's Scholars exists at other historic schools: for example, at **Westminster School** (named Queen's or King's Scholars depending on the monarch), where scholars board in the oldest house and wear gowns for Abbey services. Additionally, at **King's College Cambridge**, "King's Scholar" refers to undergraduates who achieve first-class degrees, who then receive a prize and participate in ceremonial dinners.

The **King's Schools** (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, Worcester), re-endowed by King Henry VIII, award King's Scholarships based on academic or musical merit, often accompanied by fee discounts and lifelong cathedral foundation membership. Traditions include admission ceremonies and privileges such as marrying or being buried in cathedral grounds. For example, at King's Ely, scholars wear red gowns and ties and participate in an annual "Hoop Trundle" tradition; at King's School Canterbury, scholars wear special gowns and lead cathedral processions.

In all, King's Scholars historically represent academically gifted students awarded scholarships rooted in royal patronage, marked by distinct traditions, privileges, and an association with some of the oldest educational institutions in England.
