## King's Scholar: Concise Summary

The term "King's Scholar" refers to students who receive prestigious foundation scholarships at several historic British institutions, originally created to offer educational opportunities to select students supported by the Crown. The earliest King's Scholars were established in 1317 by King Edward II at the University of Cambridge for boys from the royal household, with their expenses funded by the local sheriff. At Eton College, King's Scholars—known as "Collegers" or "tugs"—are students who pass the College Election exam. They are awarded Foundation Scholarships, live in the College house, wear a distinctive black academic gown, and participate in unique traditions. Eton statutes provide for about 70 King's Scholars, admitted at age 13 for five years, and historically, scholarships covered school fees entirely; today, waivers depend on financial need.

Beyond Eton, analogous scholarships exist at Westminster School (where the title alternates between King's and Queen's Scholar depending on the monarch) and at King's College, Cambridge, where undergraduates earning first-class degrees can become King's Scholars, receiving special prizes and recognition. King's Scholarships also feature at the seven King's Schools re-endowed by Henry VIII in 1541, awarded now for academic or musical achievement and usually accompanied by fee reductions, with traditions extending to privileges such as cathedral membership and the right to be married or buried in cathedral grounds. Across these institutions, King's Scholar designates a long-standing tradition of academic distinction, special privileges, and unique cultural heritage linked to royal patronage.
