# Summary of Information on "King's Scholar"

The title "King's Scholar" originated in 1317 when King Edward II established the first King's Scholars at the University of Cambridge. King Henry VI further institutionalized the concept by founding Eton College in 1440 as a charitable school for "poor, scholarly boys," increasing the number of King's Scholars to 70 from 1443. At Eton College, a King's Scholar, also known as a "Colleger" or "tug," is a student who passes the College Election examination, earns a Foundation Scholarship, and resides in the historic College house within the original college buildings. Historically, King's Scholars were fully fee-waived, though financial requirements have evolved to means-tested arrangements. They are distinguished by a traditional black academic gown, have unique privileges such as dining in College Hall, and typically engage more extensively in certain traditions and sports (e.g., the Wall Game) than Oppidan students.

Beyond Eton, the term "King's Scholar" is also used at Westminster School (alternating with "Queen's Scholar"), King's College Cambridge (awarded to undergraduates with first-class degrees who attend ceremonies and dinners), and the seven King's Schools founded or re-endowed by Henry VIII, where King's Scholarships recognize academic or musical merit and may include fee discounts. Notably, at King's Ely, scholarships have been awarded equally to boys and girls since 1973, with scholars participating in historic traditions like the "Hoop Trundle" and enjoying privileges connected to Ely Cathedral, including lifelong membership in the foundation and rights related to marriage and burial in the cathedral grounds. Similar traditions of scholar recognition and privileges exist at King's School Canterbury and King's School Worcester, with ceremonies integrating scholars into cathedral foundations and unique customary rights.
