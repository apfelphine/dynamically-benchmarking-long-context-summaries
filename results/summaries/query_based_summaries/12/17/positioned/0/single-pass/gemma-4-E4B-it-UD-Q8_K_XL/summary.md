## King's Scholar Summaries

### Historical Overview
The tradition of King's Scholars began in 1317 when King Edward II established the first King's Scholars at the University of Cambridge, sending twelve boys from his household to study at Cambridge under the sheriff's financial sponsorship. Later, King Henry VI established Eton College in 1440, increasing the number of King's Scholars to 70 by 1443.

### King's Scholars at Eton College
At Eton College, a King's Scholar ("Colleger" or "tug") is a student who has passed the College Election examination and been awarded a Foundation Scholarship, admitting them to the ancient College house. These scholars, typically aged 13 to 18, wear a black academic gown. The college statutes stipulate 70 King's Scholars, with about 14 admitted annually at age 13. While they share many school life aspects with fee-paying "Oppidans," they eat meals in College Hall and participate in certain ancient traditions. Historically, the Foundation Scholarship waived all fees, though since the 1960s, it has been subject to means testing.

### Other Institutions
The term is also used at King's College Cambridge for undergraduates earning first-class degrees, and at the seven King's Schools (Canterbury, Chester, etc.), where the scholarship is awarded in recognition of academic or musical attainment, often accompanied by a fee discount. At Westminster School, scholars are designated as King's Scholars when a King is reigning, while foundation scholars are called Queen's Scholars when a Queen is reigning.
