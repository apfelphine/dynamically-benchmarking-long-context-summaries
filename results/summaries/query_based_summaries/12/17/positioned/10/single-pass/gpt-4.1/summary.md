## King's Scholar: Concise Summary

The King's Scholar designation originated in 1317 when King Edward II sent twelve boys from his household to study at Cambridge, establishing the first King's Scholars. The tradition evolved with King Henry VI's founding of Eton College in 1440, where King's Scholars (later increased to 70 by 1443) were provided education in College Hall and housed in the central College building. At Eton today, King's Scholars—known as "Collegers" or "tugs"—are selected via a competitive exam, awarded a Foundation Scholarship, and reside in College, distinguished by wearing black academic gowns. They join about 14 new scholars per year, remain for five years, and participate in unique traditions and privileges, such as dining in College Hall and playing the Wall Game throughout their tenure.

The title "King's Scholar" is also used at other historic institutions: at Westminster School, "King's" or "Queen's Scholars" (reflecting the monarch) are chosen by exam to board in College House and wear gowns; at King's College Cambridge, undergraduates who achieve first-class degrees become King's Scholars, receive a prize, and attend a ceremonial dinner. Several King's Schools (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, etc.) established or re-endowed by Henry VIII award King's Scholarships for academic or musical excellence, sometimes including financial benefits and traditional privileges, such as the right to marry or be buried in cathedral grounds. The number and admission of King's and Queen's Scholars may vary, reflecting the reigning monarch and a shift towards gender inclusivity. Notably, Eton King's Scholars have included individuals distinguished in politics, academia, literature, and other fields, such as Boris Johnson, John Maynard Keynes, Aldous Huxley, and Stephen Wolfram.
