The **King's Scholar** is a prestigious academic honor established in 1317 by King Edward II at the University of Cambridge, initially for 12 boys from his household. The tradition evolved, and in 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College, creating a system of King's Scholars (Collegers) who are selected through competitive examinations and reside in the original College buildings. These scholars wear a black academic gown and are distinguished by their status, with about 70 admitted annually. Notable alumni include prominent figures in politics, academia, and the arts such as George Orwell, John Maynard Keynes, and Boris Johnson.

At Westminster School, King's Scholars are selected by competitive examination and wear gowns during services, with the title changing to Queen's Scholars when a Queen is reigning. At King's College Cambridge, the term refers to undergraduates who achieve first-class degrees and receive a £350 prize and a formal dinner. The seven King's Schools—Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester—award King's Scholarships based on academic or musical achievement, often with fee reductions. These scholarships are historically tied to the reigns of monarchs and have evolved to include both boys and girls, reflecting broader societal changes. The tradition emphasizes academic excellence, leadership, and service, with scholars enjoying privileges such as special access to services and burial rights in cathedral grounds.
