## King's Scholar – Concise Summary

The term "King's Scholar" historically refers to students who were selected for academic excellence and awarded scholarships funded or endowed by the monarch. Its origins date to 1317, when King Edward II sent twelve boys from his household to study at Cambridge, sponsoring their expenses from royal funds. The most notable expansion occurred in 1440, when King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charity school, subsequently housing 70 King's Scholars in the original College buildings, distinguishing them from fee-paying students (Oppidans). At Eton, King's Scholars, known as "Collegers" or "tugs," receive Foundation Scholarships, reside in the College, wear distinctive academic gowns, and partake in unique traditions, such as dining in College Hall and playing the Wall Game. The scholarship previously covered all fees but now offers means-tested financial support.

The designation extends beyond Eton: Westminster School foundation scholars are called King's or Queen's Scholars depending on the monarch, while at King's College Cambridge, undergraduates who secure first-class degrees are deemed King's Scholars and partake in ceremonial recognition. King's Scholarships are also awarded at seven King's Schools re-founded by Henry VIII, such as Ely, Canterbury, and Worcester, typically recognizing academic or musical achievement and often including privileges like special attire, the right to marry or be buried in cathedral grounds, and celebratory ceremonies. Notable King's Scholars from Eton have included prominent politicians, writers, scientists, and public figures such as John Maynard Keynes, George Orwell (Eric Blair), Boris Johnson, and Harold Macmillan.
