**King's Scholar**

The title "King's Scholar" originates from a royal initiative dated 7 July 1317, when King Edward II established twelve scholars at the University of Cambridge, funded from the king’s revenues. Later, in 1440, King Henry VI founded Eton College as a charity school for "poor, scholarly boys," expanding the number of King's Scholars to seventy by 1443. At Eton, a King's Scholar is a student who successfully passes the College Election examination, winning a Foundation Scholarship and admission to "College," the original and oldest boarding house within Eton’s ancient buildings. Around 14 new King's Scholars are admitted yearly at age 13, forming a distinct group known as "Collegers" or colloquially "tugs," identifiable by their black academic gowns over the tailcoat uniform. Historically, the scholarship granted full fee waivers, though since the 1960s awards have been means-tested, with some or all fees potentially waived based on financial need. King's Scholars participate fully in school life, share many activities with non-scholar students (Oppidans), but enjoy privileges such as dining in the College Hall and certain ancient formal traditions.

The King's Scholar designation also exists at Westminster School, where foundation scholars are called King's or Queen's Scholars depending on the monarch. At King's College Cambridge, King's Scholars are distinguished high-achieving undergraduates who receive formal recognition including a prize, a ceremony, and privileges such as dining with Directors of Studies. Additionally, King's Schools across the UK—re-endowed by King Henry VIII—award King's Scholarships for academic or musical merit, often accompanied by fee discounts and traditional rights, with some adapting to Queen's Scholarships during female reigns. Notable King's Scholars from Eton include figures such as Prime Ministers Boris Johnson and Harold Macmillan, economist John Maynard Keynes, writer George Orwell, and mathematician Timothy Gowers.
