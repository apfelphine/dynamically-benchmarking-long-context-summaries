Based strictly on the provided text, the **King's Scholar** is a title and scholarship designation associated with several distinct institutions in the United Kingdom, primarily Eton College and the King's Schools, rather than a single unified global program. At **Eton College**, the King's Scholar (colloquially known as a "Colleger" or "tug") is a student admitted via a competitive College Election examination who receives a Foundation Scholarship and resides in the original central college buildings known as "College." There are approximately 70 scholars at a time, admitted in blocks of about 14 per year at age 13. Historically, this scholarship provided a full waiver of fees, though since the 1960s it has been subject to means testing, with current waivers dependent on financial circumstances. Scholars are distinguished by a black academic gown over their uniform, the post-nominal letters "KS," and specific privileges such as eating in College Hall and playing the Wall Game for five years.

The term "King's Scholar" is also used at the **King's Schools** (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester), which were re-endowed by King Henry VIII in 1541. At these schools, the scholarship is awarded in recognition of academic or musical attainment and often includes a discount on fees. Notably, these schools have the right to award Queen's Scholarships in recognition of the reigning monarch. Specific privileges vary by school: at **King's Ely**, scholars sign a book in Ely Cathedral, participate in the "Hoop Trundle" tradition, and have the right to marry in the cathedral and be buried within its grounds. At **King's School, Canterbury**, scholars walk down the aisle first at cathedral services and wear white gowns, with former scholars having the right to marry in the crypt and be buried in the cathedral grounds. Similarly, at **King's School, Worcester**, scholars are members of the Worcester Cathedral Foundation, wear white surplices during services, and the Senior Scholar holds the right to demand a day's holiday from the presiding judge.
