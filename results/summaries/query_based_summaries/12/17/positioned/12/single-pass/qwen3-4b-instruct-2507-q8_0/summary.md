The **King's Scholar** is a prestigious academic title awarded to students at several prominent UK institutions, with origins dating back to the 14th century. At the **University of Cambridge**, King's Scholars were established in 1317 and later expanded to include students at Eton College, where they are known as "Collegers" or "tugs" and wear a distinctive black academic gown. The selection process at Eton involves a competitive examination, and scholars are housed in the original College building, sharing meals and traditions with other students. The scholarship is historically tied to a full waiver of fees, though since the 1960s, it has been subject to means testing. 

At **Westminster School**, the title is known as a **Queen's Scholar** when a queen is reigning, and at **King's College Cambridge**, it is awarded to undergraduates who achieve first-class degrees, with a ceremonial dinner and prize. The **seven King's Schools** (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester) were re-endowed by Henry VIII in 1541 and award scholarships based on academic or musical attainment. At **King's Ely**, scholars are admitted through a special service in Ely Cathedral, participate in traditions like the "Hoop Trundle," and gain lifelong privileges such as the right to marry and be buried in the cathedral. Similar traditions exist at other King's Schools, including the right to marry in the cathedral and participate in academic ceremonies. 

Notable past scholars include prominent figures in politics, science, literature, and the arts, such as **John Maynard Keynes**, **Aldous Huxley**, **Boris Johnson**, and **Stephen Wolfram**, reflecting the program's long-standing influence on British intellectual life.
