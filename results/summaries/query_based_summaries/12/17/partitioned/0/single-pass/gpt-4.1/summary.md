## King's Scholar – Summary

The title "King's Scholar" historically refers to students awarded prestigious foundation scholarships at several distinguished English schools and colleges. At Eton College, King's Scholars (also known as "Collegers" or "tugs") are selected through the College Election examination and receive Foundation Scholarships. Traditionally, they were housed in the oldest College buildings and distinguished by wearing black academic gowns. Their designation dates back to King Henry VI's establishment of Eton in 1440, with statutes providing for 70 such scholars at any time, granted admission at age 13 for five years. Over time, the scholarships, once guaranteeing a full fee waiver, became subject to means-testing and no longer automatically cover tuition.

Beyond Eton, the term King's Scholar is also used at other institutions with royal foundations. At King's College, Cambridge, undergraduates who achieve first-class degrees are named King's Scholars and participate in a ceremonial signing and celebratory dinner. The seven King's Schools (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester) also award King's Scholarships for academic or musical achievement, often accompanied by privileges such as discounted school fees and symbolic traditions. Westminster School foundation scholars are called King's or Queen's Scholars depending on the reigning monarch and are selected by competitive examination. Notable privileges for King's Scholars across institutions include participation in ancient ceremonies, the right to marry or be buried in associated cathedrals, and lifelong affiliation with the respective foundations.
