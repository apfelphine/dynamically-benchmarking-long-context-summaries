The term "King's Scholar" historically refers to students funded by royal endowments to study at various institutions, most notably the University of Cambridge in 1317 and Eton College in the 1440s. At Eton College, a King's Scholar (colloquially a "tug") is a student who has passed the College Election examination and received a Foundation Scholarship, living in the original "College" house for five years. These scholars are distinguished by wearing a black academic gown over their uniform, a tradition deriving from the Latin *Gens Togata* ("toga'd people"). Admission occurs in blocks of approximately 14 students per year at age 13, and while they share many aspects of school life with "Oppidans" (fee-paying students), they eat in College Hall and participate in full five-year Wall Game play.

The designation is also used across the seven King's Schools of England (Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester), which were re-founded by Henry VIII. At these schools, King's Scholars are awarded for academic or musical attainment and often receive fee reductions. While historically all pupils at these schools were funded scholars, Queen's Scholarships were introduced in 1973 to recognize female students. At King's College Cambridge, the title is awarded to undergraduates who achieve first-class degrees, granting them access to a formal dinner ceremony in the King's College Chapel.
