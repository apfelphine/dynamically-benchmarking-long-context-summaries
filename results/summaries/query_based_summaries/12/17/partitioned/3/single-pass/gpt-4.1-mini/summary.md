# Summary of "King's Scholar"

The title **King's Scholar** originates from medieval royal patronage, notably established by King Edward II in 1317 and King Henry VI in 1440, linked with institutions like the University of Cambridge and Eton College. At **Eton College**, King's Scholars—also called "Collegers" or "tugs"—are selected by examination and form a distinct house known as "College," traditionally numbering about seventy. They wear distinctive gowns and participate in unique traditions, such as playing the Wall Game, dining in College Hall, and living in the historic central college buildings. Originally scholarships covered full fees; however, since the 1960s, they have been subject to means testing and currently do not automatically reduce school fees. King's Scholars append the post-nominal letters "KS" to their names, while academically distinguished Oppidans may be designated "Oppidan Scholars" (OS) without financial benefits.

Beyond Eton, the term extends to other British schools such as **Westminster School**, where scholars are called King's or Queen's Scholars depending on the monarch, and to undergraduates at **King's College Cambridge** achieving first-class degrees. Additionally, several historic **King's Schools** founded or re-endowed by Henry VIII (e.g., Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Worcester) award King's or Queen's Scholarships recognizing academic or musical excellence, often accompanied by financial benefits and privileges, with some allowing former scholars lifelong association rights such as marriage or burial within cathedral grounds. These scholarships carry a rich tradition and afford their holders distinctive privileges, affirming their status within their institutions and linked ecclesiastical foundations.
