## King's Scholar: Key Details and Traditions

The term "King's Scholar" historically refers to students awarded Foundation Scholarships at prestigious educational institutions in England, notably Eton College, Westminster School, King's College Cambridge, and the seven King's Schools re-endowed by Henry VIII. At Eton College, King's Scholars (known as "Collegers" or "tugs") are students who pass the College Election examination, wear black academic gowns, and reside in the original College building. There are up to 70 King's Scholars at Eton, distinguished by particular traditions such as meals in College Hall and participation in the Wall Game. The post-nominal "KS" identifies them in school records.

The King's Schools—Canterbury, Chester, Ely, Gloucester, Peterborough, Rochester, and Worcester—award King's Scholarships for academic or musical achievement, often including reduced fees. Notably, King's Ely and King's School Worcester include formal admission ceremonies, lifelong Cathedral privileges (such as the right to marry or be buried within the Cathedral grounds), and distinctive attire. At King's College Cambridge, the title is given to undergraduates who earn first-class degrees, accompanied by ceremonial recognition in the King's College Chapel and a celebratory dinner. Westminster School foundation scholars are termed King’s or Queen’s Scholars depending on the monarch, selected by competitive exam, and have distinct traditions including gown-wearing during Abbey services.

Throughout these schools, being a King's Scholar represents academic distinction and long-standing historical privilege, tied to royal foundations and maintained through specific rites, honours, and lifelong affiliations with their respective institutions and cathedrals.
