**Wasserstein GAN**

Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) is a variant of the original Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) framework designed to improve training stability and address common GAN issues such as mode collapse and vanishing gradients. Unlike the original GAN, whose discriminator estimates the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the real and generated data distributions, WGAN utilizes the Wasserstein-1 distance (Earth Mover’s distance) as a measure of divergence. This employs the Kantorovich-Rubenstein duality, formulating the discriminator (called the "critic") as a K-Lipschitz continuous function that scores real and generated samples, where the generator aims to minimize the Wasserstein distance to the real data distribution.

Mathematically, the WGAN game involves a zero-sum objective:  \(\min_{\mu_G} \max_{D:\|D\|_L \leq K} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu_G}[D(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu_{\text{ref}}}[D(x)]\), where \(\mu_{\text{ref}}\) is the real data distribution and \(\mu_G\) is the generator’s distribution. The discriminator must satisfy a Lipschitz constraint to ensure the Wasserstein distance is well-defined and learnable via gradient methods. This leads to more meaningful gradients for the generator and improved convergence compared to the original GAN’s Jensen-Shannon divergence, especially in high-dimensional spaces where distributions are often disjoint.

Training WGANs involves enforcing the Lipschitz constraint on the discriminator through methods such as weight clipping, spectral normalization, or a gradient penalty term. Weight clipping restricts discriminator weights within a compact interval to bound its Lipschitz constant; spectral normalization controls the spectral norm of weight matrices for tighter Lipschitz bounds; and gradient penalty adds a loss term penalizing deviations of the gradient norm from a target value. These techniques enable more stable training dynamics and have empirically been shown to alleviate mode collapse, with the original authors reporting no observed mode collapse in WGAN experiments.
