The Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) modifies the standard Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) by replacing the original objective function with the Wasserstein distance (specifically the 1-Wasserstein distance) and constraining the discriminator's Lipschitz norm. This modification addresses two primary issues in standard GANs: the vanishing gradient problem, which occurs when the generator distribution is disjoint from the reference distribution, and the instability caused by the vanishing gradient when the discriminator is too strong. In the WGAN framework, the discriminator acts as a "critic" with a bounded Lipschitz norm, ensuring that gradients are provided consistently to the generator even when the distributions are far apart.

To enforce the Lipschitz constraint, WGAN employs several training techniques. One common method is weight clipping, where the weights of the discriminator's neural network are clamped to a small interval to manually bound the Lipschitz norm. Alternatively, spectral normalization is used, which normalizes the weight matrices of the discriminator layers by their spectral radius (the largest singular value), allowing for more efficient computation than weight clipping. A third approach, the gradient penalty, adds a regularization term to the loss function that penalizes the discriminator for violating the Lipschitz constraint by ensuring the norm of its gradient with respect to the input remains close to a specific constant. These methods collectively enable WGANs to train more stably and achieve higher-quality generated samples compared to standard GANs.
