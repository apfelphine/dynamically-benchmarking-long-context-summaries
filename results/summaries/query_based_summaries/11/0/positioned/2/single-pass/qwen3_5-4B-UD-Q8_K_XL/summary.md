**Generative adversarial networks (GANs)** are a class of generative models defined as a zero-sum game between two neural networks: a generator and a discriminator. The generator's objective is to produce synthetic data samples that approximate a reference distribution, while the discriminator aims to distinguish between real data from the reference distribution and the generator's synthetic outputs. This adversarial training creates a competitive dynamic where the generator improves its ability to fool the discriminator, and the discriminator becomes better at detecting fakes. Mathematically, the game seeks to minimize the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the generated and reference distributions. While the original GAN formulation has theoretical guarantees, practical training often faces challenges such as unstable convergence, vanishing gradients, and mode collapse, where the generator fails to capture the full diversity of the data distribution.

To address these issues, several variants and training strategies have been developed. **Wasserstein GANs (WGANs)** replace the Jensen-Shannon divergence with the Wasserstein distance, providing more stable gradients and mitigating mode collapse by constraining the discriminator's Lipschitz norm. Other architectures include **Conditional GANs**, which allow generation conditioned on specific inputs (e.g., class labels); **CycleGAN**, which performs unpaired image-to-image translation using cycle consistency; and **StyleGAN**, which uses progressive growth and style modulation to generate high-resolution, photorealistic images. Evaluation of GANs typically relies on metrics like the Inception Score and Fréchet Inception Distance to measure sample quality and diversity. GANs have found widespread application in image generation, style transfer, medical imaging, and scientific simulation, though they remain sensitive to hyperparameters and training difficulties.
