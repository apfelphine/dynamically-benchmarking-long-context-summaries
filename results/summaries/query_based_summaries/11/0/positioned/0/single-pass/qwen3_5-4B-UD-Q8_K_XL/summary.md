A **Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)** is a class of generative models based on a game-theoretic framework involving two competing neural networks: a **generator** and a **discriminator**. The generator aims to create synthetic data samples from a latent space that become indistinguishable from real data, while the discriminator attempts to distinguish between real and generated samples. This adversarial process creates a zero-sum game where the generator's objective is to minimize the discriminator's ability to detect fakes, and the discriminator's objective is to maximize its accuracy. Mathematically, this is modeled as a minimax optimization problem where the generator tries to match a reference distribution, and the optimal discriminator computes the likelihood ratio between the reference and generated distributions, effectively maximizing the Jensen-Shannon divergence.

In practice, GANs utilize implicit generative models that do not explicitly model likelihood functions, allowing them to generate complete samples in a single pass, unlike autoregressive models. However, training GANs is often unstable due to issues such as **mode collapse** (where the generator produces limited variety) and **vanishing gradients** (when the discriminator becomes too good). To address these challenges, variants like the **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)** have been developed to ensure better gradient flow and convergence. Additionally, architectures such as **Conditional GANs** allow generation based on specific conditions, while others like **CycleGAN** enable translation between unpaired domains. Evaluation typically relies on metrics like the Inception Score (IS) and Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) to assess sample quality and diversity.
