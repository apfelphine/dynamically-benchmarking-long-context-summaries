A **Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)** is a deep learning framework consisting of two neural networks—**generator** and **discriminator**—that compete in a zero-sum game. The generator aims to produce data that closely resembles the reference distribution, while the discriminator attempts to distinguish between real data and generated samples. This adversarial interaction allows the generator to learn complex data distributions, enabling tasks like image synthesis, data augmentation, and generative modeling.

Mathematically, the GAN game is defined over a probability space with the generator’s strategy set being all probability measures on the input space, and the discriminator’s strategy set being Markov kernels mapping inputs to probability distributions over [0,1]. The objective function is a combination of log-likelihood terms, where the generator minimizes and the discriminator maximizes it. The generator’s goal is to minimize the Jensen–Shannon divergence between its output distribution and the reference distribution, while the discriminator’s role is to maximize this divergence by distinguishing real from fake data. The adversarial training process involves iteratively updating both networks, with the generator learning to fool the discriminator into accepting its outputs as real.

In practice, GANs are implemented using neural networks, where the generator typically employs deconvolutional neural networks (e.g., for image generation), and the discriminator uses convolutional neural networks (CNNs). The generator is often initialized with random noise sampled from a latent space, such as a Gaussian distribution, and iteratively refined through backpropagation. The training process can be unstable, leading to issues like mode collapse (where the generator fails to generate diverse samples) or vanishing gradients (where the discriminator becomes overly accurate, preventing the generator from learning). To address these challenges, variants like **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)** and **Conditional GAN (cGAN)** have been developed, focusing on improved gradient stability and conditional generation capabilities. GANs are widely used in applications such as image synthesis, medical imaging, and data augmentation, leveraging their ability to generate realistic and diverse samples from complex data distributions.
