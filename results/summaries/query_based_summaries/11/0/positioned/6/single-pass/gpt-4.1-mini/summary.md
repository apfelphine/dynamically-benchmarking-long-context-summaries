## Summary of Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are a framework in which two neural networks, a generator and a discriminator, compete in a zero-sum game. The generator creates candidate data samples aiming to replicate the true data distribution, while the discriminator evaluates whether samples come from the true data or the generator. Formally, the generator seeks to minimize, and the discriminator to maximize, a value function reflecting classification of real versus generated data. The discriminator outputs values approaching 1 for true data and 0 for generated data, guiding the generator to produce increasingly realistic samples.

Mathematically, the GAN game is defined over a probability space with the generator producing a probability distribution by transforming latent variables drawn from a known distribution (e.g., Gaussian), and the discriminator modeled as a function (often a neural network) that outputs probabilities of data origin. The optimal discriminator corresponds to the likelihood ratio between the real and generated distributions, and the unique Nash equilibrium occurs when the generator perfectly matches the real data distribution and the discriminator outputs 0.5 for all inputs.

Training GANs is challenging due to unstable convergence, mode collapse (where the generator fails to produce diverse samples), and vanishing gradients (when the discriminator overpowers the generator). Solutions include carefully balancing learning rates (two time-scale update rule), alternative loss functions such as Wasserstein GAN, and architectural modifications. Evaluation metrics include Inception Score and Fréchet Inception Distance, reflecting diversity and realism of generated samples.

Variants of GANs extend the framework to conditional generation (conditional GANs), architectures employing alternative neural structures (e.g., convolutional, transformer-based generators and discriminators), and objectives (e.g., hinge loss, Wasserstein distance). Notable variants include Wasserstein GANs which use the Wasserstein distance as a loss to provide better gradient behavior, InfoGANs that maximize mutual information to learn interpretable latent codes, and Bidirectional GANs that jointly learn generation and inference mappings.

GANs have been successfully applied in numerous fields including image and video synthesis, medical imaging, scientific simulation, art generation, and more. However, concerns exist about misuse for deepfakes and synthetic content creation without consent. Advances in training stability, data augmentation methods, and architectures (e.g., StyleGAN series, CycleGAN) have enabled high-quality, diverse, and controllable generative models.
