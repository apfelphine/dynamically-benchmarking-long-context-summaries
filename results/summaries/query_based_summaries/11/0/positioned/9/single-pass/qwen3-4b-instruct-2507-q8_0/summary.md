A **Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)** is a type of machine learning model based on a zero-sum game between two neural networks: a **generator** and a **discriminator**. The generator creates synthetic data samples that aim to mimic real data, while the discriminator learns to distinguish between real and generated samples. The two networks are trained simultaneously, with the generator improving to produce more realistic data and the discriminator improving to better detect fakes, leading to a dynamic equilibrium where the generator produces data indistinguishable from real data.

The original GAN objective function is defined as minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the discriminator's output, which is equivalent to maximizing the discriminator's ability to classify real and fake samples. The game converges to a unique equilibrium where the generator perfectly mimics the real data distribution, and the discriminator outputs a constant value of 0.5 for all inputs.

GANs are implicit generative models, meaning they do not explicitly model the likelihood function or provide a direct way to generate samples from a latent space. They are known for their ability to generate high-quality, realistic data, though they often suffer from issues such as **mode collapse** (where the generator produces only a limited set of outputs) and **unstable training** due to vanishing gradients. To address these issues, various variants have been developed, including **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)**, which uses a different loss function based on the Wasserstein distance to improve training stability, and **conditional GANs**, which allow the generation of samples based on specific conditions or labels.

Applications of GANs span across diverse domains, including image and video generation, medical imaging (e.g., generating synthetic medical images), scientific modeling (e.g., simulating physical phenomena), and even fashion and art. Despite challenges, GANs have become a foundational tool in generative modeling, with ongoing research focused on improving stability, diversity, and real-world applicability.
