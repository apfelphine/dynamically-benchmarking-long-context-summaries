Based strictly on the provided text, a **Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)** is defined as a zero-sum game between two neural networks: a **generator** and a **discriminator**. The generator's objective is to produce samples from a latent space that approximate a reference data distribution ($\mu_{\text{ref}}$), while the discriminator aims to distinguish real data from generated samples. Mathematically, this is formulated as a minimax optimization problem where the generator minimizes the objective function $L(\mu_G, \mu_D)$ and the discriminator maximizes it. Theoretically, the optimal discriminator computes the likelihood ratio between the two distributions, and the unique Nash equilibrium occurs when the generator perfectly mimics the reference distribution and the discriminator outputs a constant value of $1/2$.

In practice, GANs are trained using independent backpropagation procedures to update the weights of both networks simultaneously. The generator is typically initialized with random inputs from a simple distribution (e.g., Gaussian) and is often implemented as a deconvolutional network for image generation, while the discriminator is commonly a convolutional network. Training is prone to instability and failure modes such as **mode collapse** (where the generator produces limited variety) and **vanishing gradients** (when the discriminator is too good). To address these issues, variants like the **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)** were developed, which restrict the discriminator's Lipschitz norm and use the Wasserstein distance to provide more stable gradients. GANs are considered implicit generative models that can generate complete samples in a single pass and are asymptotically consistent due to the universal approximation capabilities of neural networks.
