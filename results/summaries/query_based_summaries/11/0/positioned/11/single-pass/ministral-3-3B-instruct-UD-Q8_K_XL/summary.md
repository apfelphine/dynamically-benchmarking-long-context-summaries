The **Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)** is a deep learning framework consisting of two competing neural networks: a **generator** and a **discriminator**. The generator creates synthetic data samples from a latent space, while the discriminator evaluates these samples to distinguish them from real data. This adversarial training process, where both networks improve iteratively, aims to make the generator's outputs indistinguishable from real data.

Mathematically, the GAN game is defined over a probability space, where the generator's objective is to minimize the loss function, and the discriminator's goal is to maximize it. The original GAN paper introduced a minimax game where the generator and discriminator alternately optimize their strategies. The optimal discriminator computes the Jensen–Shannon divergence, and the equilibrium point is when the generator perfectly mimics the reference distribution, while the discriminator outputs a constant value of 0.5. This equilibrium ensures that the generator's output distribution matches the reference distribution, achieving the desired generative capability.

In practice, GANs are trained using gradient descent, where the generator and discriminator are updated alternately. The generator is typically initialized with random noise sampled from a predefined latent distribution, such as a Gaussian or uniform distribution. The discriminator is trained on real data samples, and the generator is trained to fool the discriminator. Training can be challenging, often leading to issues like mode collapse (where the generator fails to generalize properly) or vanishing gradients (where the discriminator becomes too good, preventing the generator from learning effectively). Techniques like the **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)** and **two-time-scale update rule (TTUR)** have been proposed to address these problems, improving convergence stability and performance. Variants of GANs, such as **conditional GANs (cGANs)**, **CycleGAN**, and **StyleGAN**, extend the framework to handle conditional generation, domain translation, and high-resolution image synthesis, respectively. GANs have found applications across various domains, including image generation, medical imaging, scientific simulations, and more, leveraging their ability to generate realistic and diverse synthetic data.
