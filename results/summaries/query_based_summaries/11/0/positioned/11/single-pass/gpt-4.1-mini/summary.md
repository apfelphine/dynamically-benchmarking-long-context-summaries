## Summary of Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are a class of implicit generative models formulated as a two-player zero-sum game between a generator and a discriminator. The generator aims to produce samples from a probability distribution that approximates the real data distribution, while the discriminator's role is to distinguish real samples from generated (fake) ones. Mathematically, the game is defined over probability spaces, with the generator selecting a probability measure on the data space and the discriminator choosing a measurable function outputting probabilities that indicate whether a sample is real or generated. The objective function involves maximizing (for the discriminator) and minimizing (for the generator) the expected log-likelihoods of correct classification of real and generated samples.

In practice, the generator typically maps a latent random variable (e.g., drawn from a normal distribution) through a neural network to produce data-like samples, while the discriminator is a neural network trained to output a value close to 1 for real data and close to 0 for generated data. Both networks are trained iteratively via backpropagation, with the generator improving its samples based on the discriminator's feedback, and the discriminator refining its classification capabilities.

GANs have connections to other machine learning frameworks but differ by not modeling explicit likelihoods or requiring inference of latent variables for samples. Important theoretical results show that the optimal discriminator corresponds to the likelihood ratio between real and generated distributions, and at equilibrium the generator perfectly replicates the data distribution, with the discriminator outputting 0.5 for all inputs.

Several practical issues arise in GAN training, including unstable convergence, mode collapse (where the generator produces a limited variety of outputs), and vanishing gradients (where the generator's updates become negligible because the discriminator is too accurate). Techniques such as the two time-scale update rule (slowing generator learning relative to discriminator) and Wasserstein GANs address some of these problems.

GANs have been extended into numerous variants including conditional GANs (which generate outputs conditioned on class labels), adversarial autoencoders, InfoGAN (which maximizes mutual information to learn disentangled representations), bidirectional GANs (with learned encoders), and CycleGANs (for unpaired image-to-image translation). Architecturally, GAN components have been adapted to convolutional, attention-based, and transformer-based networks.

Evaluation metrics for GANs include Inception score, Fréchet Inception Distance, and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity. GANs have found diverse applications in image synthesis, style transfer, data augmentation, medical imaging, scientific simulations, art, and more. Variations like StyleGAN enable high-quality image generation with controllable styles. Data augmentation strategies, including invertible augmentations, help GANs train effectively with limited data.

Historically, GANs were formalized in 2014 by Goodfellow et al., though precursors and related ideas existed earlier. Since then, GANs have become central tools in generative modeling research and applications spanning computer vision, medicine, physics, and creative industries.
