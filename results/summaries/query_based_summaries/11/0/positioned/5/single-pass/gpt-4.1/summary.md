## Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) Summary

A Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) consists of two competing neural networks: a generator and a discriminator. The generator maps random noise or latent variables to sample data, attempting to produce outputs indistinguishable from real data. The discriminator receives examples from both the generator and the actual data distribution, classifying them as real or fake. The networks train in a zero-sum game: the generator aims to minimize its ability to be distinguished by the discriminator, while the discriminator aims to maximize its detection accuracy. Mathematically, the optimal discriminator computes the likelihood ratio between the reference and generated distributions, and at equilibrium, the generator's output matches the data distribution and the discriminator outputs uniform ambiguity.

GANs are fundamentally implicit generative models, not explicitly modeling likelihood or latent variables. They are widely used for tasks such as image synthesis, data augmentation, super-resolution, and translation between domains (e.g., CycleGAN). Numerous GAN variants exist, including conditional GANs (conditioning outputs on auxiliary information), architectures with alternative losses (e.g., Wasserstein GAN to address mode collapse and vanishing gradients), and multi-player GAN setups (e.g., InfoGAN, BiGAN). Advanced GANs like StyleGAN have achieved high-quality, high-resolution image generation. GANs find applications in science, medicine, art, and entertainment, but also raise concerns over misuse in creating realistic fake media. Training GANs is challenging due to instability, mode collapse, and vanishing gradients, which various strategies and architectural improvements attempt to address.
