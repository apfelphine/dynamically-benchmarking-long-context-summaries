A **Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)** is a type of machine learning model consisting of two neural networks: a **generator** and a **discriminator**, engaged in a strategic, zero-sum game. The generator creates synthetic data samples that aim to mimic real data, while the discriminator evaluates whether a sample is real or fake. The generator's objective is to produce data that the discriminator cannot distinguish from real data, and the discriminator's goal is to correctly identify real versus fake samples. This adversarial training process drives both networks to improve iteratively—generating more realistic data and becoming better at detecting fakes.

The original GAN framework is mathematically defined as a minimax game, where the generator minimizes the loss function (the probability of being detected as fake), and the discriminator maximizes it (the probability of correctly classifying real/fake data). The game's equilibrium point is theoretically unique, where the generator perfectly mimics the real data distribution and the discriminator outputs a constant 0.5 for all inputs, indicating no ability to distinguish between real and fake data.

In practice, GANs face challenges such as **mode collapse** (where the generator produces only a limited set of outputs, missing diverse data patterns) and **vanishing gradients** (where the generator receives little feedback, preventing learning). To improve stability, variants like **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)** use a different loss function based on the Wasserstein distance, which provides smoother gradients and avoids mode collapse. Other variants include **conditional GANs** (which generate data conditioned on labels), **infoGAN** (which encourages the generator to learn meaningful latent variables), and **cycleGAN** (which performs translation between domains without paired data).

GANs have been widely applied beyond image generation, including in **scientific simulations** (e.g., reconstructing astronomical images), **medical imaging** (generating synthetic data to preserve privacy), **malicious use** (creating fake media), and **artistic creation** (e.g., generating art and fashion models). Despite their success, they remain an active area of research due to challenges in training stability, generalization, and interpretability.
