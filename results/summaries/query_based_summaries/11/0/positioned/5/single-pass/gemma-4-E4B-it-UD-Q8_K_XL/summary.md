Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are defined as a zero-sum game involving two players: a **generator** ($\mu_G$) and a **discriminator** ($\mu_D$). The generator's objective is to minimize the objective function, aiming to make its output distribution ($\mu_G$) closely match a reference distribution ($\mu_{ref}$). Conversely, the discriminator's objective is to maximize the same objective function, aiming to distinguish between real data ($\mu_{ref}$) and generated data ($\mu_G$).

Mathematically, the original GAN game objective is $L(\mu_G,\mu_D):=\operatorname {E} _{x\sim \mu _{\text{ref}},y\sim \mu _{D}(x)}[\ln y]+\operatorname {E} _{x\sim \mu _{G},y\sim \mu _{D}(x)}[\ln(1-y)]$. In practice, the generator maps from an easily sampled latent space ($\mu_Z$) to the data space ($\Omega$), making the generator's strategy $G$. GANs are implicit generative models; they do not explicitly model the likelihood function but can generate a complete sample in a single pass. Variations include Conditional GANs (CGANs) and Wasserstein GANs (WGANs), which modify the objective function and the discriminator's strategy set to address training instabilities and improve gradient quality.
