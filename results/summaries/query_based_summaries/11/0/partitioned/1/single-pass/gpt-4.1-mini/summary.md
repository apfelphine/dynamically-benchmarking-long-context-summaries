## Summary of Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are defined as a zero-sum game between two players: a generator and a discriminator. The generator aims to produce samples whose distribution matches a reference distribution, while the discriminator aims to distinguish real samples from generated ones. The game objective function is given by the expected log-probabilities of discriminator outputs on real versus generated data. Theoretically, the unique equilibrium occurs when the generator distribution equals the reference distribution and the discriminator outputs ½ everywhere, corresponding to minimization of the Jensen–Shannon divergence. In practice, the generator is modeled as a function mapping a known noise distribution to the data space, and the discriminator is a function estimating the probability that a sample comes from the reference distribution.

Training GANs can be unstable due to restricted parametrization by neural networks and resulting nonconvex optimization landscapes. Common issues include mode collapse—where the generator produces limited diversity—and vanishing gradients—where the discriminator becomes too powerful to provide useful learning signals to the generator. Strategies such as the two time-scale update rule (TTUR), curriculum learning, and architectural innovations have been proposed to mitigate these problems.

Various GAN variants have been developed, including alternative loss functions like hinge loss and least squares GAN, and architectures such as Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) that replace Jensen–Shannon divergence with the Wasserstein distance to improve training stability and reduce mode collapse. Other multi-player GANs include conditional GANs that generate samples conditioned on labels, InfoGANs that maximize mutual information to learn interpretable representations, bidirectional GANs that jointly train encoder and generator, and CycleGANs for domain translation without paired data. Large-scale GANs like BigGAN utilize extensive parameters and training tricks to generate high-resolution images.

Invertible data augmentation techniques have been introduced to allow effective GAN training on small datasets by applying transformations that preserve the true underlying distribution. Furthermore, architectures like StyleGAN series progressively improve image generation quality through style-based synthesis and training techniques addressing issues like texture sticking and data augmentation.

Overall, GANs provide a powerful framework for implicit generative modeling, enabling one-pass generation of complex data, widely applied in image synthesis, art, scientific simulations, and privacy-preserving synthetic data generation, although challenges in training stability and mode coverage remain active areas of research.
