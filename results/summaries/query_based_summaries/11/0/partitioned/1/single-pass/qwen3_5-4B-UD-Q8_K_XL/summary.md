### Generative Adversarial Network

A Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) is an implicit generative model defined as a zero-sum game between two neural networks: a generator and a discriminator. The generator's objective is to produce synthetic data samples that approximate a reference distribution, while the discriminator attempts to distinguish between real samples from the reference distribution and fake samples generated by the generator. Mathematically, the optimal discriminator strategy computes the likelihood ratio between the reference and generator distributions, maximizing the Jensen-Shannon divergence; however, in practice, the discriminator often collapses to outputting a constant value (e.g., 0.5) at equilibrium, resulting in a vanishing gradient problem that hinders the generator's learning.

Training GANs is challenging due to issues like mode collapse, where the generator fails to cover the entire data distribution by focusing on a few representative patterns, and unstable convergence caused by mismatched learning rates between the generator and discriminator. Various solutions have been proposed to address these challenges, including the Wasserstein GAN (WGAN), which replaces the original loss function with the Wasserstein distance to ensure non-vanishing gradients, and the Two-Time-Step Update Rule (TTUR), which stabilizes training by updating the discriminator more frequently than the generator. GANs have evolved into numerous variants, such as Conditional GANs (cGANs) for generating specific classes, StyleGAN for high-quality image synthesis, and CycleGAN for unpaired image-to-image translation, finding applications in image generation, medical imaging, and scientific simulation.

### Variants and Related Architectures

Beyond the standard GAN framework, several variants have been developed to enhance performance and versatility. Conditional GANs (cGANs) allow the generator to produce specific outputs based on additional input labels, enabling tasks like generating images of specific categories. The Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) modifies the game mechanics by restricting the discriminator's strategy set to functions with bounded Lipschitz norms and using the Wasserstein distance as the objective, which solves mode collapse and provides more stable gradients. Other variants include Adversarial Autoencoders, InfoGAN (which maximizes mutual information to learn interpretable latent features), Bidirectional GANs (BiGANs) that jointly learn encoding and decoding, and CycleGAN, which performs translation between two domains without requiring paired training data by enforcing cycle consistency.

In the broader context of deep learning, GANs are distinct from other generative models like Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) because they do not explicitly model a likelihood function or provide a means to find the latent variable for a given sample; instead, they learn the data distribution implicitly through the adversarial process. While VAEs use a variational lower bound (ELBO) to maximize log-likelihood and minimize KL divergence, GANs rely on the minimax game to approximate the data distribution, offering the advantage of generating complete samples in a single pass. The rise of Vision Transformers (ViT) has also influenced GAN development, with architectures like ViT-VQGAN combining transformer-based encoding with vector quantization and adversarial training for image generation.
