Autoencoders are defined by two main components: an encoder family, $E_{\phi}:{\mathcal {X}}\rightarrow {\mathcal {Z}}$, parametrized by $\phi$, and a decoder family, $D_{\theta}:{\mathcal {Z}}\rightarrow {\mathcal {X}}$, parametrized by $\theta$. Typically, both are multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), where $\mathcal{X}$ is the space of encoded messages and $\mathcal{Z}$ is the space of decoded messages, with $\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}^n$ where $m>n$. Training an autoencoder involves minimizing a loss function, $L(\theta ,\phi )=\mathbb {\mathbb {E} } _{x\sim \mu _{ref}}[d(x,D_{\theta }(E_{\phi }(x)))]$, using an optimization technique like gradient descent.

An ideal undercomplete autoencoder can compress a message such that its decoder can generate messages indistinguishable from real ones by feeding the decoder arbitrary codes $z \in \mathcal{Z}$. Variations exist, such as Denoising Autoencoders (DAE), which corrupt inputs during training to achieve more robust representations, and Sparse Autoencoders (SAE), which enforce sparsity in the latent codes. Other advanced models include the Variational Autoencoder (VAE), which models the latent space as a mixture of distributions.
