## Accelerationism: Key Concepts and Variants

**Accelerationism** refers to a diverse and often contradictory set of philosophical and political ideas focused on the notion that advancing—or "accelerating"—the forces of technology, capitalism, or modernization may be the key to provoking radical social transformation. Originally coined by Benjamin Noys to describe post-structuralist Marxists like Deleuze and Guattari, accelerationism has historical antecedents in Marx, Nietzsche, and various French theorists. Core concepts involve the belief that the contradictions and destabilizing effects of capitalism and technological development can be actively intensified, not as a means of immediate social critique, but to push society through and beyond existing structures. Prometheanism (especially left-accelerationist) celebrates the transformative power of technology, rationality, and "inhumanism"—the move beyond centering human concerns—while hyperstition describes the recursive process by which ideas about the future accelerate their own actualization.

### Major Variants

Accelerationism splits into key variants:

- **Right-wing accelerationism** (e.g., Nick Land) champions the runaway optimization of capitalism and technological progress, ultimately embracing the replacement or extinction of humanity by more advanced forms of intelligence. Land envisions an anti-human, technocapital singularity, welcoming the complete dissolution of human culture in favor of planetary technosentience and drawing influences from neoreactionary thought, sinofuturism, and anti-democratic models.

- **Left-wing accelerationism** (e.g., Mark Fisher, Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams, Ray Brassier) seeks to "accelerate past capitalism" by democratizing technology and modernity, using existing capitalist infrastructure as a springboard for post-capitalist experimentation. This variant is critical of "folk politics" focused on local and direct action, instead advocating for technoscientific ambition, rational transformation of society, and collective control over technological systems. Prominent theorists promote "inhumanist" rationality as a force for human transformation, while some currents (e.g., xenofeminism) envision technology as a route to gender abolition.

- **Effective accelerationism** (e/acc) prioritizes unrestricted technological progress—especially artificial general intelligence—as a route to solving global problems, contrasting with effective altruism’s focus on careful alignment and regulation.

Accelerationism has also been misinterpreted or co-opted by far-right extremist groups, who advocate for violent societal collapse to achieve their own ends, as well as by political figures employing accelerationist disruption strategies. Critiques of accelerationism highlight its risks of technocratic elitism and loss of individual agency, as well as possible perpetuation of colonialism when applied globally. In all, accelerationism remains central to debates on the intersections across technology, capitalism, and social change.
