# Summary of Accelerationism

Accelerationism is a philosophical and political theory with multiple, often conflicting interpretations, tracing its roots to late-20th-century post-Marxist French thinkers such as Deleuze, Guattari, Lyotard, and Baudrillard. It has undergone three waves: the late 1960s-70s French post-Marxists, the 1990s development by Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU), and the 2010s Promethean left-accelerationism. The term itself was popularized by Benjamin Noys to describe efforts to harness capitalist dynamics to destabilize the system. Accelerationism engages with concepts like capitalist deterritorialization, posthumanism, and technological progress, often emphasizing the intensification of technological and social contradictions.

There are distinct variants of accelerationism. **Right-wing accelerationism**, associated with Nick Land, views capitalism’s self-accelerating technological evolution as inevitable and advocates embracing it fully, even endorsing human extinction in favor of hyperintelligent machinic futures and technocapital singularities. Land critiques democratic constraints, supports authoritarian capitalist models (neoreactionary “dark enlightenment”), and praises states like China for their technoproductivity. Conversely, **left-wing accelerationism**, advocated by thinkers like Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams, Ray Brassier, and Reza Negarestani, seeks to accelerate technological development and rational capacity to move beyond capitalism, focusing on democratizing productive technologies and embracing 'inhuman' rationalism. It promotes a break from capitalism’s stagnation through collective control and technological experimentation, though it faces criticism for potentially neglecting individual welfare. Related currents include feminist xenofeminism and effective accelerationism, the latter advocating unrestricted tech progress relying on AI to solve global issues. The term has also been controversially adopted by far-right extremist groups advocating violent societal collapse as a pathway to ethnostates.
