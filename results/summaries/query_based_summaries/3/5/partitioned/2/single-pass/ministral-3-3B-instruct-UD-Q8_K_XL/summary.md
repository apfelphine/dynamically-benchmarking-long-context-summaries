Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement characterized by a focus on accelerating technological, economic, and social progress, often to the point of transforming human society beyond its current boundaries. The term emerged in the 2010s, drawing from earlier influences such as French post-structuralist thought (e.g., Gilles Deleuze, Félix Guattari) and cyberpunk culture, which celebrated the disruptive potential of capitalism and technology.

### **Variants of Accelerationism**
1. **Right-Wing Accelerationism**
   - Advocated by figures like Nick Land, this variant embraces capitalism’s self-amplifying tendencies, viewing technological and economic acceleration as inevitable and even desirable. Land, in particular, celebrated China’s rapid modernization as an example of accelerationist progress, framing it as a natural evolution of capitalism. He criticized traditional politics as a brake on this progress, advocating for a "liquidation of the human" to achieve a hyperintelligent, post-human future. This perspective often aligns with libertarian and anti-statist ideologies, emphasizing the liberation of desire and the dissolution of traditional social hierarchies through unchecked technological and economic growth.

2. **Left-Wing Accelerationism**
   - Influenced by thinkers like Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams, Ray Brassier, and Reza Negarestani, this variant seeks to harness technological progress to dismantle capitalism. It draws on Deleuze and Guattari’s concept of *desiring-production*—where human desire is not merely satiated by consumption but actively reshapes society through technological and economic forces. Left-accelerationists advocate for using existing capitalist infrastructure as a tool to transition beyond capitalism, emphasizing the need to democratize and control technological advancements to achieve a more equitable and post-capitalist future. They often critique traditional left-wing politics for being too focused on localism and direct action, instead advocating for a politics that embraces modernity’s complexities and technological potential.

### **Core Tenets**
- **Prometheanism**: The idea that humans can and should transcend their biological limitations through technology, rejecting any fixed or natural limits to human progress. This aligns with the belief that capitalism’s inherent accelerationism can be harnessed to create a more advanced, inhuman future.
- **Hyperstition**: The notion that beliefs and expectations about the future can retroactively shape reality, accelerating change through self-fulfilling prophecies. This concept is central to Land’s work, where he argues that capitalism’s hyperstitional nature—its reliance on confidence and self-reinforcing cycles—drives relentless technological and economic growth.
- **Anti-Humanism**: A rejection of anthropocentrism, where human concerns are not the primary focus but rather a means to achieve broader, inhuman goals. This is often tied to the idea of becoming "bodies without organs," where human limitations are transcended through technological and societal transformation.

### **Criticisms and Controversies**
- **Elitism and Exclusion**: Critics argue that accelerationism, particularly in its left-wing form, risks becoming technocratic and elitist, prioritizing technological progress over human welfare. The movement’s focus on inhumanism and the "liquidation of the human" has been seen as potentially subjugating individuals to a larger, impersonal system.
- **Misalignment with Political Realities**: Some left-accelerationists have faced backlash for their perceived detachment from traditional political struggles, leading to the abandonment of the term in favor of more pragmatic alternatives like "post-capitalism."
- **Far-Right Accelerationism**: A fringe variant of right-wing accelerationism has emerged among far-right and neo-fascist groups, advocating for violent acceleration of racial conflict and societal collapse to achieve a white ethnostate. This interpretation of accelerationism is often linked to conspiracy theories and extremist ideologies, diverging sharply from Land’s original, more philosophical and technocratic approach.

Accelerationism remains a polarizing and evolving movement, with its proponents arguing that it offers a necessary critique of stagnation and a pathway to a more dynamic, technologically advanced future, while critics warn of its potential to marginalize human agency and ethical considerations.
