## Accelerationism: Key Concepts and Variants

Accelerationism is a heterogeneous philosophical and political movement focused on the idea that intensifying certain tendencies within capitalism and technological development can hasten radical societal transformation. Its origins trace back to French post-Marxists such as Deleuze, Guattari, Lyotard, and Baudrillard, and was later shaped by thinkers like Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU). Accelerationists contend that aggravating contradictions within capitalism and leveraging rapid technological growth could overcome existing limitations, with approaches ranging from the affirmation of inhuman progress (technocapitalist or Prometheanism) to hyperstitional feedback loops, where imagined futures influence present reality.

Accelerationism has split into several competing variants:  
- **Right-wing accelerationism**, led by Nick Land, embraces unregulated capitalism as a self-improving positive feedback circuit, arguing for the total dissolution of traditional constraints and human-centric politics to pave the way for technological singularity—even at the expense of humanity itself.  
- **Left-wing accelerationism** (espoused by figures like Brassier, Negarestani, Srnicek, and Williams) advocates using technological advances to move beyond capitalism, democratize productive technologies, and reconfigure society for collective liberation, though critics warn this could risk technocratic elitism and the liquidation of the human in favor of inhuman rationalist systems. Subvariants include xenofeminism, which integrates technology for gender abolition.  
- **Effective accelerationism (e/acc)** urges unrestrained technological progress to solve global issues, diverging from effective altruism’s caution against uncontrolled AI.  
Accelerationism has also prompted alternative interpretations in political strategy—for example, voting for disruptive figures to hasten systemic crisis—and has been co-opted by far-right groups to mean accelerating societal collapse through violence. While central to accelerationism is the belief in harnessing or intensifying capitalism’s and technology’s transformative potential, critics highlight risks of instability, elitism, and ethical concerns about the fate of humanity itself.
