## Accelerationism: Overview and Key Concepts

Accelerationism refers to a loosely connected set of ideas and movements advocating for speeding up technological, social, and economic processes—often linked to capitalism—with the aim of provoking radical transformation or overcoming perceived limitations. Originating in the late 1960s and early 1970s among French post-Marxists such as Deleuze, Guattari, Lyotard, and Baudrillard, accelerationism evolved through a 1990s second wave influenced by Nick Land and the CCRU, and a third wave comprising Promethean left-accelerationism in the 2010s. The philosophy draws on concepts like deterritorialization (the constant dissolution of boundaries under capitalism), posthumanism, and Prometheanism (maximal mastery over nature and society through technology), as well as the notion of hyperstition—future-oriented myths or fictions that shape present reality.

Accelerationism exists in multiple, often conflicting variants. **Right-wing accelerationism**, championed by Land, endorses unfettered capitalism as a self-optimizing force capable of dissolving human limitations, even if it leads to human extinction and AI dominance. **Left-wing accelerationism**, associated with thinkers such as Srnicek, Williams, Brassier, Negarestani, and Fisher, seeks to "accelerate past capitalism" by harnessing technological advances for collectivist or emancipatory ends, rather than merely deepening capitalist logics. The movement has spawned further offshoots, such as xenofeminism and effective accelerationism (which promotes uninhibited technological development), and has been misconstrued or appropriated for aims as diverse as political upheaval and, detrimentally, far-right extremism advocating violent societal collapse. Critics warn that accelerationism can risk technocratic elitism, loss of human agency, and even human liquidation in favor of abstract systems.
