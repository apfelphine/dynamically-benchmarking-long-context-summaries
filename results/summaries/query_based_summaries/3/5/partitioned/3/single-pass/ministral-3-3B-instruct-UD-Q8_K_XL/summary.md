Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the rapid acceleration of technological, economic, and social progress, often critiquing traditional left-wing politics for its perceived stagnation and resistance to change. The term emerged from the work of French post-structuralists like Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, who explored the idea of capitalism's inherent tendency toward self-improvement and destabilization. Accelerationism is divided into several variants:

### **Left-Wing Accelerationism**
This strand, championed by figures like Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams, Ray Brassier, and Reza Negarestani, seeks to harness the power of technological and scientific advancements to transcend capitalism. They argue that capitalism, despite its contradictions, remains the most advanced economic system, and that its inherent dynamism can be redirected toward liberatory ends. Left-accelerationists advocate for:
- **Prometheanism**: The idea that humans should embrace technological and scientific progress to surpass natural limitations, often rejecting humanism in favor of an "inhuman" future.
- **Inhumanism**: A rejection of traditional humanist values, advocating for a radical transformation of humanity through technology and rationalism.
- **Critique of stagnation**: They argue that the left's focus on localism and direct action has failed to address the global and systemic challenges posed by capitalism, instead proposing a politics of acceleration that leverages existing technological infrastructure for post-capitalist goals.

Critics, such as Bill McKibben and Noys, highlight risks like technocratic elitism and the potential for human subjugation under such a framework, though proponents emphasize the necessity of technological progress to overcome capitalist constraints.

### **Effective Accelerationism**
Influenced by effective altruism, this variant prioritizes unrestricted technological progress, believing that artificial general intelligence (AGI) will solve global problems like poverty and climate change. It rejects deceleration, viewing it as a greater risk than potential AI dangers. Effective accelerationists advocate for rapid technological advancement without regulatory constraints, contrasting with traditional effective altruism, which emphasizes careful alignment and regulation of AI.

### **Right-Wing Accelerationism**
This variant, associated with figures like Nick Land, embraces the destructive and inhuman tendencies of capitalism, viewing it as an engine of exponential technological growth. Land argues that capitalism's self-revolutionizing nature is inherently accelerationist, and that resistance to this process is futile. He advocates for a "technocapital singularity," where technology and capitalism merge to create a hyperintelligent, inhuman future. Critics, including Slavoj Žižek and Yuk Hui, note the potential for this ideology to reinforce authoritarianism and colonialist narratives, particularly in its celebration of Asian economic models like China’s rapid modernization.

### **Xenofeminism**
A feminist variant of accelerationism, exemplified by Laboria Cuboniks, uses technology to challenge gender norms and advocate for gender abolition. It rejects the sex-gender distinction, critiquing biological essentialism and promoting a technologically driven liberation from traditional gender roles.

### **Critiques and Controversies**
Accelerationism has faced significant critique from various quarters:
- **Prometheanism and Humanism**: Critics like Noys and Fluss argue that left-accelerationism risks becoming elitist and technocratic, prioritizing technological progress over human welfare.
- **Inhumanism and Ethics**: The rejection of humanism raises ethical concerns about the potential for dehumanization and the loss of individual agency.
- **Colonialism and Power Dynamics**: Yuk Hui critiques accelerationism’s Prometheanism for its potential to perpetuate colonialist and Western-centric narratives, ignoring non-Western technological traditions.
- **Far-Right Accelerationism**: Far-right groups, such as neo-Nazis and white supremacists, have appropriated the term to advocate for violent acceleration of racial conflict, often through sabotage and societal collapse.

Overall, accelerationism represents a radical departure from traditional political and philosophical frameworks, emphasizing the necessity of embracing technological and systemic change to address the challenges of the modern world.
