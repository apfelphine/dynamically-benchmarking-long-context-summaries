Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement characterized by the belief that the forces of capitalism, technology, and globalization should be accelerated to their logical extremes, often resulting in the collapse of the existing order. Emerging from French post-Marxist thought in the late 1960s, it evolved into the 1990s Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU) led by Nick Land, which introduced concepts like "hyperstition" (self-fulfilling prophecies) and a form of right-wing accelerationism celebrating unregulated capitalist growth. In the 2010s, the movement bifurcated into right-wing accelerationism, which advocates for the unrestricted acceleration of techno-capitalist processes, and left-wing accelerationism, which seeks to democratize productive technologies to surpass capitalism and achieve a post-capitalist future. Both variants generally reject human agency in favor of embracing inhumanism and posthumanism, viewing the inevitable intensification of these forces as a way to transcend human limitations or dismantle oppressive structures.

Critics of accelerationism include figures like Slavoj Žižek, who argue that the movement is deterministic and overly optimistic, and political scientists who highlight its co-option by far-right extremist groups who use the term to justify violent destabilization. While proponents like Robin Mackay and Armen Avanessian compile key texts in anthologies such as *#Accelerate*, the movement remains controversial due to its association with anti-democratic narratives, its rejection of traditional ethics, and its ambiguous stance on whether accelerating technological progress will lead to liberation or human extinction. Despite these critiques, accelerationism continues to influence discussions on the future of technology, AI, and the potential for a "technocapital singularity."
