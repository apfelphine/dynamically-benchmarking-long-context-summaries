Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the rapid acceleration of societal and technological progress, often in opposition to traditional political and economic systems. Emerging from the late 1960s and 1970s French post-Marxist thought, it gained prominence through figures like Gilles Deleuze, Félix Guattari, and Nick Land. The term was popularized by Benjamin Noys, who described it as a trajectory that embraces unorthodox critiques of capitalism, particularly its growth and destabilizing effects.

Accelerationism is divided into two main currents: **right-wing accelerationism**, championed by Land and others, which celebrates unregulated capitalism's exponential growth and technological advancement, often viewing it as inherently progressive and self-improving. This perspective sees capitalism as a force of radical change, pushing humanity toward a future where human limitations are transcended through technological and societal transformation. Right-wing accelerationism often aligns with libertarian and anti-statist ideologies, emphasizing individual agency and the dissolution of traditional social hierarchies.

**Left-wing accelerationism**, exemplified by thinkers like Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams, and Ray Brassier, advocates for a more critical and transformative approach. It seeks to harness the destabilizing forces of capitalism to accelerate beyond its constraints, aiming to create a post-capitalist society. Left-accelerationists often draw on posthumanist and antihumanist ideas, emphasizing the need to transcend human limitations through technological and societal innovation. They advocate for a politics that embraces complexity, globality, and technological advancement, aiming to redefine human existence beyond traditional boundaries.

The movement is also associated with concepts like **prometheanism**, which advocates for the use of technology to surpass human limitations, and **hyperstition**, the idea that beliefs about the future can shape and influence present realities. Left-accelerationism often critiques traditional left-wing politics for its focus on localism and direct action, arguing that it fails to address the global, abstract, and technological challenges of the modern world. It emphasizes the need for a politics that is both technologically adept and socially transformative, aiming to redefine human agency and societal structures in the face of rapid technological change.
