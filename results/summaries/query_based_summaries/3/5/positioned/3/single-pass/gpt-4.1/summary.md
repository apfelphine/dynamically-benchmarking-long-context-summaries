## Accelerationism Summary

Accelerationism is a philosophical and political concept with origins in mid-20th-century European thought, notably among French thinkers such as Deleuze, Guattari, Lyotard, and Baudrillard. It describes the strategy of embracing and amplifying the disruptive forces of capitalist or technological development to destabilize existing systems and potentially catalyze radical change. The term was first used in Roger Zelazny's 1967 novel *Lord of Light*, and later defined by Benjamin Noys to represent both the theoretical position and practical engagement with the increasing speed, abstraction, and transformation brought about by advancing capitalism, technology, and rationality. The movement has evolved through three waves: French post-Marxists, the 1990s Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU), and 2010s left-accelerationists.

Accelerationism encompasses diverse and often conflicting variants. Right-wing accelerationists like Nick Land advocate for unrestrained capitalist and technological growth, celebrating the inhuman progress and deterritorializing force of markets and AI—sometimes welcoming human obsolescence. Left-wing accelerationists, such as Srnicek and Williams, promote using technological infrastructure to overcome the constraints of capitalism and build post-capitalist futures centered on rationality, automation, and collective human transformation. Associated concepts include Prometheanism (maximal mastery over nature via technology), posthumanism/inhumanism (de-prioritizing human essence), and hyperstition (ideas that realize their own future by shaping present realities). The movement is criticized for its ambivalence toward human agency and the risk of reinforcing elitism, or even supporting violent collapse, as seen in some far-right adaptations. Accelerationism has substantial influence on contemporary discussions of technology, politics, and ethics, as well as on niche and extremist ideologies.
