Accelerationism is a political and philosophical movement divided into three waves, ranging from French post-Marxist thought in the late 1960s and early 1970s to the cybernetic culture research of the 1990s and contemporary left-accelerationism in the 2010s. It generally rejects the traditional left-wing strategy of resisting or slowing technological progress, instead advocating for the acceleration of capitalist and technological forces to destabilize existing systems or achieve a post-human future. The movement is characterized by concepts such as hyperstition (self-fulfilling prophecies), posthumanism, and Prometheanism, which view technology as a means to transcend human limitations. It encompasses distinct variants, including right-wing accelerationism associated with Nick Land, which celebrates unregulated capitalism and often aligns with neoreactionary ideas; left-wing accelerationism, which seeks to democratize technology to build a post-capitalist society; and effective accelerationism, which prioritizes unrestricted technological growth to solve global problems like poverty and climate change.

Accelerationism has faced significant criticism and controversy, particularly regarding its association with far-right extremism and white supremacist terrorism, as seen in the manifestos of perpetrators of violent attacks. Critics argue that the movement's embrace of inhumanism and the "acceleration of contradictions" leads to the destruction of human agency and societal stability. Furthermore, the term has been co-opted by anti-democratic movements and conspiracy theories to frame technological advancement as an existential threat to traditional values. While proponents view accelerationism as a necessary strategy to navigate the complexities of modernity and overcome the stagnation of late capitalism, opponents contend that it fails to address the ethical and political consequences of rapid technological change, often prioritizing abstract theoretical goals over tangible human welfare.
