Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the acceleration of technological and social change, particularly within capitalist systems, to achieve transformative outcomes. It emerged in three waves: first through French post-Marxist thinkers like Deleuze and Guattari in the 1960s–70s, who viewed capitalism as a deterritorializing force; second through Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU) in the 1990s, who emphasized unregulated technological progress; and third through the "Promethean left" in the 2010s, which seeks to democratize technology for post-capitalist futures.

The movement is characterized by core concepts such as posthumanism, antihumanism, and hyperstition—the idea that future beliefs can retroactively shape present realities. Right-wing accelerationism, associated with Land, celebrates capitalism’s self-expanding, destructive tendencies, viewing technological acceleration as a path to a post-human, machine-dominated world, sometimes advocating for human extinction. Left-wing accelerationism, represented by figures like Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams, critiques capitalism’s stagnation and proposes using existing technological infrastructure to build a more rational, equitable society through automation, universal basic income, and reduced work hours.

A key divergence lies in the political orientation: right-wing accelerationism promotes technological progress as inherently anti-human and destructive, while left-wing accelerationism seeks to harness technology for emancipatory ends. Effective accelerationism, influenced by effective altruism, prioritizes unregulated technological advancement as the best path to solving global problems like poverty and climate change.

The term has also been co-opted by far-right extremists to justify violent actions aimed at destabilizing society and establishing a white ethnostate, often citing neo-fascist ideologies and conspiracy theories. Critics, including Marxist scholars and cultural theorists, argue that accelerationism risks promoting technological determinism, colonialism, or human subjugation, and that its focus on technological progress may overlook social and historical inequalities. Despite its controversies, accelerationism remains a significant and evolving discourse in philosophy, political theory, and technology studies.
