**Accelerationism** is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the rapid acceleration of societal and technological progress, often rejecting traditional political and economic constraints. Emerging from French post-structuralist thought in the 1960s and 1970s, it gained prominence in the 1990s through figures like Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU). Accelerationists argue that capitalism inherently drives exponential technological and social change, and that resisting this trajectory would only prolong stagnation. They advocate for a rejection of human-centric norms, embracing instead what they see as the inherent inhumanity of capitalism and technological progress.

The movement is divided into **right-wing accelerationism**, which celebrates capitalism's self-amplifying tendencies and advocates for unrestricted technological and economic growth, and **left-wing accelerationism**, which seeks to harness these forces for revolutionary change, often critiquing traditional left-wing politics for its resistance to technological innovation. Left-accelerationists, such as Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams, propose using existing capitalist infrastructure as a springboard for post-capitalist transformation, emphasizing the need for collective control over technological advancements. Both strands reject the idea of a "human exception" and advocate for a future where humanity transcends its biological limitations through technological enhancement.

Accelerationism has been associated with critiques of humanism, traditional politics, and the slow pace of societal change, often drawing parallels to cyberpunk aesthetics and futuristic visions of technological singularity. However, it has also faced criticism for its deterministic view of history, its potential to reinforce existing power structures, and its lack of concrete political strategies. The term has also been co-opted by far-right movements advocating for violent societal collapse to achieve a white ethnostate, highlighting its complex and often polarizing implications.
