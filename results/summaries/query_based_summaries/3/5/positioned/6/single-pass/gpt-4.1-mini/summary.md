**Accelerationism** is a philosophical and political concept with origins in late 1960s and 70s French post-Marxist thought, developed notably by thinkers such as Gilles Deleuze, Félix Guattari, Jean-François Lyotard, and Jean Baudrillard. It gained further elaboration in the 1990s through Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU), and resurfaced in the 2010s as left-accelerationism. The term was initially popularized by Benjamin Noys (2010) to describe ideas that embrace and intensify capitalist processes, aiming to use capitalism’s own dynamics to destabilize and transcend it.

Accelerationism incorporates influences from posthumanism and antihumanism and draws on concepts like Deleuze and Guattari’s desiring-production and Nietzsche’s philosophy. It emphasizes a radical engagement with the destructive and transformative potentials of technological and capitalist acceleration, often celebrating the dissolution of traditional humanist norms and boundaries. Hyperstition, the belief that ideas about the future can retroactively bring about their reality, is a key mechanism in accelerationist thought, employed to hasten technological transformation.

There are distinct variants of accelerationism: right-wing accelerationism, associated with Nick Land and characterized by a techno-capitalist embrace of unregulated capitalism and anti-democratic elitism; and left-wing accelerationism, represented by figures like Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams, which seeks to harness technological progress democratically to surpass capitalism and realize emancipatory futures. Left-accelerationism advocates leveraging existing capitalist infrastructure to enable post-capitalist transformation and technological advancement. Critiques of accelerationism point to its contradictory ideas, potential elitism, and risks related to disregarding human welfare in favor of inhuman rationality. Additionally, a far-right form of accelerationism, promoting violent societal collapse to build ethnonationalist states, has emerged as a dangerous and distinct appropriation of the concept.
