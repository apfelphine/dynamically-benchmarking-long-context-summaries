Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the unimpeded acceleration of technological and societal development, particularly in the context of capitalism and technological progress. Rooted in the works of French post-Marxist thinkers like Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, it emerged in three waves: a foundational phase in the 1960s–70s, a more radical phase in the 1990s through the work of Nick Land and the CCRU, and a contemporary "Promethean" variant in the 2010s. The movement is characterized by the rejection of human-centered constraints and a belief in the self-organizing, deterritorializing nature of capitalism, which is viewed as inherently accelerating and self-improving.

A central concept is "hyperstition," the idea that future beliefs and visions can retroactively shape present realities. Accelerationism posits that by constructing imaginative, prefigurative visions of a future technological world, society can actively hasten its realization. This is exemplified in the belief that capitalism's self-reinforcing dynamics—such as technological innovation and market expansion—naturally drive progress toward a posthuman, technologically integrated future.

The movement is divided into distinct variants. Right-wing accelerationism, associated with Nick Land, embraces unregulated capitalism and sees technological self-improvement as a path to human transcendence, often culminating in the idea of a technological singularity and human extinction. Left-wing accelerationism, represented by figures like Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams, seeks to harness capitalist technological momentum to achieve a more equitable, post-capitalist society through democratic control of technology and automation. This variant emphasizes the need to democratize and redirect technological progress toward social good, such as universal basic income and reduced work hours.

A controversial and increasingly misused aspect of accelerationism is its adoption by far-right extremist groups, who use the term to justify violent, destabilizing actions aimed at dismantling modern society in favor of a white ethnostate. This misuse has led to significant criticism and concern, particularly regarding its potential to promote extremist ideologies.

Despite its diverse forms, accelerationism remains a contested and evolving framework, with debates over its ethical implications, its relationship to human agency, and its potential to either liberate or destabilize society.
