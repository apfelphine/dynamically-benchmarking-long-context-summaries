**Accelerationism** is a complex philosophical and political concept with origins in French post-Marxist thought of the late 1960s and 1970s, notably developed by thinkers such as Gilles Deleuze, Félix Guattari, Jean Baudrillard, and Jean-François Lyotard. It broadly involves embracing and intensifying the processes of capitalism and technological change to catalyze systemic transformation or collapse. The movement has evolved in three waves: the initial post-Marxist wave, a 1990s development led by Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU), and a 2010s left-accelerationist wave advocating for the democratization of productive technologies to surpass capitalism.

Accelerationism draws heavily on ideas of deterritorialization and reterritorialization from Deleuze and Guattari and engages with posthumanism and anti-humanism, particularly exemplified in "Prometheanism," which celebrates technological innovation to transcend human limitations. Central to accelerationism is the notion of “hyperstition,” the idea that future-oriented beliefs can shape present realities, fueling capitalist and technological acceleration. Variants include right-wing accelerationism, associated with Land, which advocates for unchecked capitalist technological progress possibly culminating in human extinction, and left-wing accelerationism, promoted by figures like Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams, aiming to harness capitalism’s potentials toward emancipatory, post-capitalist futures. Effective accelerationism emphasizes rapid technological advancement as a solution to global challenges, prioritizing AI development despite risks. Accelerationism remains a debated and often misunderstood framework, critiqued for its implications on human identity, ethics, and political agency.
