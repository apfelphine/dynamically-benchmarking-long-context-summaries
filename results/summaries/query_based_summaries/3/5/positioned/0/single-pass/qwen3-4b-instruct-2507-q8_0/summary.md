Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the unimpeded acceleration of technological and economic processes, often viewing capitalism as a self-sustaining force capable of driving progress. It has evolved through three distinct waves: (1) French post-Marxist thinkers in the late 1960s and early 1970s, such as Deleuze, Guattari, Lyotard, and Baudrillard, who theorized capitalism as a deterritorializing force; (2) the 1990s, led by Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU), who promoted a radical, anti-political, and technologically driven accelerationism; and (3) the 2010s, represented by the "left-accelerationist" movement, which seeks to use existing capitalist infrastructure to achieve post-capitalist outcomes through technological democratization.

Key concepts include **posthumanism** and **antihumanism**, with left-accelerationists embracing "inhumanism" to transcend traditional human limitations. The movement is closely tied to **Prometheanism**, which views technology as a force capable of surpassing human constraints and achieving a post-human future. **Hyperstition**—the idea that our beliefs about the future can retroactively shape present realities—is a core theoretical framework, emphasizing prefigurative political imaginaries.

Accelerationism is divided into variants: **right-wing accelerationism**, championed by Nick Land, which celebrates capitalism’s destructive potential and advocates for its unimpeded growth; and **left-wing accelerationism**, led by figures like Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams, and Ray Brassier, which seeks to use technological progress to dismantle capitalism through democratic and technological innovation. Left-accelerationism emphasizes democratizing productive technologies, using existing capitalist systems as a springboard for post-capitalist transformation.

Critics argue that accelerationism risks promoting technological determinism, elitism, and a loss of human agency. The movement has also been co-opted by far-right ideologies, where it is used to justify violent societal collapse. Despite its philosophical diversity, accelerationism remains a contested and evolving framework that challenges traditional political and economic paradigms.
