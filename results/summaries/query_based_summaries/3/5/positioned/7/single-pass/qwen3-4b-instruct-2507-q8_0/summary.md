Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the acceleration of technological and social change, often through the unregulated development of capitalism, artificial intelligence, and other advanced technologies. It emerged in three waves: first in the 1960s–70s with French post-Marxist thinkers like Deleuze, Guattari, and Lyotard; second in the 1990s through the work of Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU); and third in the 2010s with "Promethean" left-accelerationism. The movement is characterized by a rejection of human-centered norms, embracing posthumanism and antihumanism, and viewing technological progress as inherently transformative.

Key concepts include **hyperstition**, the idea that beliefs about the future can retroactively shape present realities, and **prometheanism**, the belief that technology and rationality can transcend human limitations to create a superior, inhuman form of existence. Accelerationism is divided into variants: **right-wing accelerationism**, which celebrates uncontrolled capitalist growth and technological self-improvement, and **left-wing accelerationism**, which seeks to democratize technology to overcome capitalism's stagnation. Left-wing proponents like Nick Srnicek and Alex Williams advocate for using capitalist infrastructure to build post-capitalist societies through automation, universal basic income, and technological democratization.

The movement has also been appropriated by far-right groups to promote violent, destabilizing actions, such as racial purges and societal collapse, under the guise of "accelerating" toward a white ethnostate. Critics argue that accelerationism is overly optimistic, potentially dangerous, and rooted in a technocratic elitism that undermines democratic values and human agency. Despite its influence, the movement remains controversial, with scholars highlighting its philosophical inconsistencies, risks, and potential for misuse.
