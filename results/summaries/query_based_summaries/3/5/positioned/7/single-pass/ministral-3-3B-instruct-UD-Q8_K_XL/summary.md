Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the rapid acceleration of societal, technological, and cultural change. Emerging from the work of French post-structuralists like Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari in the late 20th century, accelerationism critiques traditional Marxist and liberalist approaches by emphasizing the inherent dynamism and self-amplifying nature of capitalism and technological progress. The core idea is that capitalism, driven by market forces and technological innovation, inherently accelerates its own evolution, and this process should be harnessed rather than resisted.

The movement is divided into right-wing and left-wing variants. **Right-wing accelerationism**, exemplified by figures like Nick Land, argues that unchecked capitalism and technological progress are inherently destabilizing and should be accelerated to achieve a technologically driven, inhuman future. Land and others in this tradition often advocate for the dissolution of human social structures, viewing them as obstacles to the unbridled pursuit of technological advancement. This perspective is sometimes linked to neoreactionary and libertarian ideologies, emphasizing the potential of technology to transcend human limitations and societal constraints.

**Left-wing accelerationism**, represented by thinkers such as Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams, and Ray Brassier, takes a more critical stance on capitalism but still advocates for the acceleration of technological and social change. They argue that capitalism’s inherent contradictions and stagnation can be overcome by leveraging its own technological and scientific advancements to create a more equitable and rational society. Left-accelerationists often draw on concepts like hyperstition (the idea that beliefs shape reality) and inhumanism to propose a future where technology and reason replace human-centric norms. This strand of accelerationism is sometimes associated with posthumanist and transhumanist ideas, focusing on the potential of technology to augment human capabilities and redefine societal structures.

A key aspect of accelerationism is its embrace of **hyperstition**, the notion that human beliefs and actions can retroactively shape reality, often through technological and cultural forces. This perspective often leads to a rejection of traditional political frameworks, favoring instead a more fluid, technologically driven approach to societal transformation. Critics of accelerationism, including some Marxists and humanists, argue that it risks depoliticizing social change, prioritizing technological progress over ethical and social considerations, and potentially leading to dystopian outcomes where human agency is diminished in favor of machine-driven systems. The movement’s influence extends into discussions on artificial intelligence, digital culture, and the future of human-machine interaction, often framing these developments as inevitable and deserving of acceleration.
