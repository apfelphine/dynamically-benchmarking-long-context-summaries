## Summary of Accelerationism

Accelerationism originated with French post-Marxist thinkers in the late 1960s and 1970s (Deleuze, Guattari, Lyotard, Baudrillard), who theorized capitalism as a radically deterritorializing force, advocating for "accelerating the process" rather than resisting capitalist dynamics. The term was popularized in 2010 by Benjamin Noys, referencing the notion that intensifying capitalist contradictions can destabilize or transform the system itself. Subsequent waves included Nick Land and the CCRU in the 1990s, who merged antihumanism and cybernetic capitalism, and the more recent Promethean left-accelerationism, which sees technological and rational mastery as a means to transcend capitalist constraints and liberate humanity.

Accelerationist thought is split into right-wing and left-wing variants. Right-accelerationism, mainly espoused by Nick Land, celebrates unchecked capitalist and technological progress, equating it to a runaway process leading to inhuman transformation and even human extinction, with capitalism viewed as a hyperstitional, self-optimizing intelligence. Left-accelerationism, developed by figures like Srnicek, Williams, and Brassier, advocates using the infrastructure and technologies of capitalism to move beyond it, aiming for democratization of technology and rationalist transformation of society. Accelerationism’s influence extends to cultural critique, philosophy (notably Promethean and inhuman approaches), and hyperstitional feedback loops, while the term has also been appropriated by far-right groups advocating societal collapse through violence. Debate continues regarding its risks, its liberatory potential, and its relation to technology, capitalism, and posthuman futures.
