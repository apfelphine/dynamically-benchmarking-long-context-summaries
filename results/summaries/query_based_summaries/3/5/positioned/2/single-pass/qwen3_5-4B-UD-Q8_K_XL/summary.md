Based strictly on the provided text, **Accelerationism** is a political and philosophical framework characterized by the belief that capitalism's contradictions must be countered by their own aggravation, often through the acceleration of technological progress and social change. The concept has evolved through three waves, beginning with French post-Marxists in the late 1960s who viewed capitalism as a deterritorializing force that could destabilize traditional hierarchies. It was later popularized in the 1990s by Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU), who developed "hyperstition"—the idea that chosen beliefs about the future can retroactively shape present realities—and argued for a "technocapital singularity" where human extinction might be a solution to accessing a higher reality. This is divided into right-wing accelerationism, which embraces unregulated capitalism and often aligns with neoreactionary or far-right ideologies, and left-wing accelerationism, which seeks to use advanced technologies to democratize production and accelerate past capitalism toward post-capitalism. The movement rejects human agency in favor of "inhumanism" or posthumanism, viewing technology as a site of liberation from biological and social constraints, though it has been co-opted by far-right terrorists for the purpose of societal collapse.

The text also details the related concept of the **Technological Singularity**, a hypothetical future point where artificial intelligence surpasses human intelligence, leading to an "intelligence explosion" and an uncontrollable, rapid change in human affairs. Proponents like I. J. Good, Vernor Vinge, and Ray Kurzweil predict this via recursive self-improvement, where AI designs increasingly capable machines, potentially reaching superintelligence within decades. Conversely, critics like Steven Pinker, Jaron Lanier, and Stephen Hawking dispute the plausibility or inevitability of such an event, citing diminishing returns in technological progress, the lack of scientific rigor in exponential growth predictions, and the risks of misaligned AI goals. The discourse includes debates on "hard" versus "soft" takeoffs, the ethical alignment of AI, and the potential for existential risks versus the benefits of solving global problems like disease and poverty.
