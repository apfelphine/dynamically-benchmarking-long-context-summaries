**Accelerationism** is a philosophical and political movement that advocates for the rapid acceleration of societal and technological progress, often rejecting gradual or conservative approaches in favor of radical change. Emerging from the 1960s–70s French post-Marxist thought (e.g., Gilles Deleuze, Félix Guattari), accelerationism gained prominence in the 1990s through figures like Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU). Central to its ideology is the belief that capitalism’s inherent tendency toward self-improvement and technological advancement should be harnessed to destabilize existing systems, particularly capitalism itself.

The movement is divided into **right-wing accelerationism** (e.g., Land, Mencius Moldbug) and **left-wing accelerationism** (e.g., Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams). Right-wing accelerationism, often associated with neoreactionary or libertarian ideologies, embraces unregulated capitalism’s exponential growth, viewing it as an engine of progress and liberation from human limitations. Left-wing accelerationism, however, critiques capitalism’s stagnation and advocates for leveraging technological and scientific advancements to dismantle it, often through a focus on post-capitalist futures, automation, and democratic control of resources. Both variants reject traditional political frameworks, prioritizing speed and disruption over incremental reform.

Accelerationism’s core tenets include:
- **Prometheanism**: The idea of transcending human limitations through technology, often framed as a rejection of humanism in favor of inhuman rationalism.
- **Hyperstition**: The notion that beliefs and visions can retroactively shape reality, encouraging the creation of prefigurative political imaginaries to accelerate desired transformations.
- **Posthumanism**: A rejection of anthropocentrism, advocating for the integration of technology into human (or non-human) evolution to transcend biological constraints.

Critics argue that accelerationism’s emphasis on speed and disruption risks destabilizing societies, exacerbating inequalities, or leading to unintended consequences. The movement’s influence extends into cyberpunk aesthetics, electronic dance music, and speculative fiction, reflecting its fascination with the boundaries between human and machine, technology and society.
