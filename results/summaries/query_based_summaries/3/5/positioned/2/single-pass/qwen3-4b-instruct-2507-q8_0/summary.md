Accelerationism is a philosophical and political movement that emphasizes the acceleration of technological and societal change, often viewing technological progress as inherently self-reinforcing and unstoppable. Rooted in the works of French post-Marxist thinkers like Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, it posits that capitalism and technological development are in a state of perpetual acceleration, driven by deterritorialization and the breakdown of traditional social structures. The movement is divided into three waves: early French post-Marxist thought, the 1990s work of Nick Land and the CCRU, and the 2010s "Promethean left" and right-wing variants.

Key concepts include **posthumanism** and **antihumanism**, rejecting traditional human-centered values in favor of technological transcendence. **Prometheanism**, a core idea, holds that technology can surpass human limitations and free us from biological constraints, advocating for a radical transformation of humanity through technological innovation. This is often framed as a "speed superintelligence" or a "technocapital singularity" where machines become self-improving and autonomous.

Accelerationism is further defined by **hyperstition**, the belief that our cultural and technological imaginaries (such as science fiction or cyberpunk) can retroactively shape reality—creating a feedback loop where visions of the future become actualized. This is exemplified by the idea that the belief in a technological singularity or posthuman future can accelerate its own realization.

The movement is split into **left-wing** and **right-wing** variants. Left-wing accelerationism (e.g., Nick Srnicek, Alex Williams) seeks to democratize and harness technological progress to create a more equitable society, advocating for the use of technology to address social issues like poverty and climate change. Right-wing accelerationism (e.g., Nick Land) celebrates unregulated capitalism and sees technological self-improvement as a natural and inevitable process, often viewing it as a form of technological liberation from human constraints.

Critics argue that accelerationism is overly deterministic, lacks grounding in human agency, and can lead to a dehumanized, inhuman future. It is also criticized for its potential to reinforce social inequalities, promote technological elitism, or enable authoritarian control. Some critics, such as Slavoj Žižek and Jaron Lanier, argue it reflects a dangerous form of technological determinism and a denial of human agency.

Accelerationism has been adopted and adapted in various forms by political movements, including by far-right groups that use the term to justify violent disruption of society in pursuit of a "white ethnostate." It has also been used in political discourse to critique or promote technological change, often in ways that reflect broader ideological conflicts.
