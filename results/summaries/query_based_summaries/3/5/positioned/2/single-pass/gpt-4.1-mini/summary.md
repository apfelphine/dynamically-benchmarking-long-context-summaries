**Accelerationism** is a socio-philosophical movement that has evolved through three main waves: the late-1960s/early-70s French post-Marxist thinkers (e.g., Deleuze, Guattari, Lyotard, Baudrillard) who explored capitalism's dynamics, the 1990s Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU) led by Nick Land promoting techno-capitalist acceleration, and the 2010s Promethean left-accelerationism emphasizing technological and social transformation. The term “accelerationism” was popularized by Benjamin Noys (2010) and originally drew on ideas from Nietzsche, Marx, and Deleuze and Guattari’s concept of desiring-production, which views desire as a productive flow that capitalism constantly deterritorializes and reterritorializes.

Accelerationism combines posthumanism and antihumanism, embracing technological progress as a force that both destabilizes and transcends capitalism and human limits. Key concepts include *hyperstition*—the idea that fictional futures can shape present realities—and *Prometheanism*, which envisions leveraging technology to surpass natural and social constraints, often de-emphasizing human-centered concerns. The movement divides mainly into right-wing accelerationism—championed by Land and associated with unregulated capitalism and neoreactionary critiques of democracy—and left-wing accelerationism, represented by thinkers like Srnicek, Williams, and Brassier, which seeks to democratize and repurpose advanced technologies to transcend capitalism. Left-accelerationists criticize traditional leftist politics for neglecting technological possibilities and advocate for harnessing capitalism's inherent dynamism toward post-capitalist futures.

Accelerationism also manifests in diverse forms, including *effective accelerationism* (prioritizing accelerated technology development at any cost), *xenofeminism* (left-accelerationist feminism advocating the use of technology to dismantle essentialist notions of gender), and darker reinterpretations linked to far-right extremist and neo-Nazi groups advocating violent "acceleration" of societal collapse as a political strategy. The latter appropriation has drawn extensive attention due to its association with terrorist acts and online radicalization, distinguishing it sharply from academic and philosophical accelerationism. Overall, accelerationism grapples with capitalism's contradictions, technological potential, and the inhuman trajectories of future societies, often challenging human-centric normative frameworks.
