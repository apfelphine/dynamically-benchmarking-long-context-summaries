John L. Pollock (1940–2023) was a prominent American philosopher known primarily for his foundational contributions to epistemology and defeasible reasoning. He earned a triple-major degree in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota in 1961 and completed his doctoral dissertation at UC Berkeley in 1965, which included an appendix on defeasible reasoning that became his main contribution. Pollock held faculty positions at SUNY Buffalo, the University of Rochester, the University of Michigan, and the University of Arizona, where he helped found the Cognitive Science Program. His seminal work, *Knowledge and Justification* (1974), established him in epistemology by introducing defeasible reasoning as a framework for understanding knowledge, moving beyond the traditional "justified true belief" analysis to address the Gettier problem. He is often referred to as "Mr. Defeasible Reasoning" for his introduction of concepts such as "undercutting defeat" and "rebutting defeat," which are now standard in the field.

In the latter part of his career, Pollock applied his philosophical theories to the development of artificial intelligence, most notably through his project OSCAR (*Operating System for Cognitive Agents*). OSCAR was an "artilect" or artificial intelligence prototype that served as an implementation of his ideas on defeasible reasoning, interests, and rational agency. It was a LISP-based program capable of reasoning about perception, change, causation, and decision-making, featuring an interest-driven reasoner that prioritized "interesting" conclusions. Polock's extensive publication record includes numerous influential papers and books spanning topics from nomic probability and induction to the theory of moral reasoning and practical agency. His work bridged the gap between philosophy, logic, and computer science, significantly impacting the fields of artificial intelligence, law, and epistemology.
