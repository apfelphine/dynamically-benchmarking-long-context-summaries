## Summary: John L. Pollock

John L. Pollock (1940–2009) was a prominent American philosopher whose work substantially shaped contemporary epistemology and artificial intelligence. After earning a triple-major in physics, mathematics, and philosophy at the University of Minnesota and completing his PhD at UC Berkeley, Pollock held academic roles at several universities, with most of his career spent at the University of Arizona, where he contributed to founding the Cognitive Science Program. His pioneering book *Knowledge and Justification* (1974) introduced the concept of defeasible reasoning into epistemology, emphasizing that epistemic norms are governed by ceteris paribus conditions and can admit exceptions, influencing other major philosophers and making defeasible reasoning mainstream in philosophical debate.

Pollock is best known in philosophy and AI as "Mr. Defeasible Reasoning," especially for his distinction between "undercutting defeat" and "rebutting defeat," now standard in the literature. His investigations into probability and induction led to *Nomic Probability and the Foundations of Induction* (1990), which explored statistical inference and deepened connections between reasoning and probability. Later in his career, Pollock developed the OSCAR project—a LISP-based artificial intelligence prototype that embodied his ideas about defeasible reasoning, interests, intentions, and cognitive architecture. OSCAR could reason about perception, causation, change, probabilities, planning, and decision-making, representing Pollock's ambition to create artificial systems with anthropomorphic cognitive features. Throughout his career, Pollock authored foundational books and influential papers across logic, epistemology, philosophy of language, and AI.
