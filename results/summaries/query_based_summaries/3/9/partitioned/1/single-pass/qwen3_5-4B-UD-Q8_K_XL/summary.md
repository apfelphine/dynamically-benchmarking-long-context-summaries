John L. Pollock (1940–2010) was a prominent American philosopher best known for his foundational work on defeasible reasoning, a framework that addresses non-monotonic logic and allows for reasoning that can be overturned by new evidence. He became recognized as "Mr. Defeasible Reasoning" for his clarity and early commitment to the field, significantly influencing both artificial intelligence and legal theory. His key contributions include the distinctions between "undercutting defeat" and "rebutting defeat," which remain central to the literature on argumentation and rationality. Pollock argued that epistemic norms are ceteris paribus conditions governed by defeasible reasoning rather than strict analytic criteria, a perspective solidified in his influential 1974 book *Knowledge and Justification*.

Beyond epistemology, Pollock dedicated considerable time to the OSCAR project, an ambitious artificial intelligence software prototype he termed an "artilect." OSCAR was a LISP-based program designed to implement his theories on defeasible reasoning alongside other cognitive architectural concepts such as intentions, interests, and problem-solving strategies. It featured an "interest-based" reasoner that avoided drawing "uninteresting" conclusions to enhance efficiency. Although the project was largely unpublished and developed without a large team of programmers, it was noted for its inventor's ambition and served as a practical realization of Pollock's ideas on building a person, distinguishing itself from other contemporary cognitive architectures like Soar and Novamente. Pollock's career was marked by a deep engagement with the intersection of philosophy, logic, and computer science, culminating in a legacy where defeasible reasoning transitioned from an obscure concept to a mainstream topic in philosophy and AI.
