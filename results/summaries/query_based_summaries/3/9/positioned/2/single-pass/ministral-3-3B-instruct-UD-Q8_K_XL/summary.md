John L. Pollock was a prominent philosopher known for his contributions to epistemology and defeasible reasoning. Born in 1940 in Atchison, Kansas, Pollock earned a triple-major degree in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota in 1961. His doctoral dissertation, *Analyticity and Implication*, at the University of California, Berkeley, laid the foundation for his influential work on reasoning and knowledge.

Pollock’s most significant philosophical work includes *Knowledge and Justification* (1974), which introduced the concept of defeasible reasoning, a framework that allows for exceptions and contextual conditions in knowledge acquisition. This approach was pivotal in the field of artificial intelligence, where non-monotonic reasoning was crucial. His ideas were later formalized in his 1987 paper *"Defeasible Reasoning"* and expanded through his work on the OSCAR project, an artificial intelligence system designed to handle complex, context-dependent reasoning tasks.

Pollock also explored nomic probability, investigating how statistical inferences could be grounded in frequencies, and contributed to broader discussions on the foundations of induction. His work on cognitive architecture, including the development of OSCAR, aimed to create a robust framework for human-like reasoning, integrating elements of perception, causation, decision-making, and problem-solving. His publications span foundational texts like *Cognitive Carpentry: A Blueprint for How to Build a Person* (1995) and influential papers on epistemology, probability, and practical reasoning.

Pollock’s legacy lies in his emphasis on the flexibility and contextual nature of knowledge and reasoning, challenging traditional absolutist views in favor of a more nuanced, adaptive approach. His ideas have had a lasting impact on both philosophy and artificial intelligence, shaping how we understand and model human-like reasoning in computational systems.
