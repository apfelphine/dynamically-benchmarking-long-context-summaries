# John L. Pollock

John Leslie Pollock (1940–2008) was a prominent American philosopher and computer scientist known for his foundational work in epistemology, artificial intelligence, and defeasible reasoning. Born in Atchison, Kansas, he earned a triple major in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota in 1961 and later completed his doctoral dissertation at the University of California, Berkeley under the advisement of Ernest Adams. Pollock spent much of his career at the University of Arizona, where he helped found the Cognitive Science Program, and previously held faculty positions at SUNY Buffalo, the University of Rochester, and the University of Michigan. He was an avid mountain biker and founded a riding club in Southern Arizona.

Pollock is widely recognized as "Mr. Defeasible Reasoning," a term used to describe his significant contribution to the field. His seminal book, *Knowledge and Justification* (1974), shifted American epistemology away from seeking necessary and sufficient conditions for knowledge toward a framework of defeasible reasoning, characterized by ceteris paribus conditions that admit exceptions. He introduced key concepts such as "undercutting defeat" and "rebutting defeat," which became fixtures in philosophical and artificial intelligence literature. His work was deeply influential in the development of non-monotonic reasoning in AI, earning him high esteem among scholars sympathetic to his approach.

Beyond epistemology, Pollock dedicated considerable time to the development of OSCAR, an artificial intelligence software prototype he termed an "artilect." OSCAR, based on LISP and his theories of defeasible reasoning, embodied his ideas on intentions, interests, and cognitive architecture. It was designed to reason about perception, change, causation, probabilities, and decision-making, with an "interest-based" reasoner that prioritized "interesting" conclusions. Pollock also authored numerous other influential works, including *Nomic Probability and the Foundations of Induction* (1990), *How to Build a Person* (1989), and *Cognitive Carpentry* (1995), and published extensively on topics ranging from logic and semantics to the logical foundations of rational decision-making and goal-regression planning.
