John L. Pollock (born January 28, 1940, Atchison, Kansas) earned a triple-major degree in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota in 1961 and a Ph.D. in philosophy from the University of California, Berkeley, under Ernest Adams. He held faculty positions at SUNY Buffalo, the University of Rochester, the University of Michigan, and the University of Arizona, where he spent most of his career and helped found the Cognitive Science Program. A passionate mountain biker, he founded a riding club in Southern Arizona.

Pollock is best known for his foundational work in epistemology, particularly in the area of defeasible reasoning. His 1974 book *Knowledge and Justification* established him as a leading figure in epistemology by introducing defeasible reasoning—epistemic norms that are ceteris paribus and can admit exceptions—as a framework for knowledge, moving beyond rigid "justified true belief" models. This work influenced contemporaries such as Ernest Sosa, Roderick Chisholm, and Keith Lehrer.

His key contributions include the concepts of "undercutting defeat" and "rebutting defeat," which are central to defeasible reasoning, and later expanded to include "self-defeat." These ideas were pivotal in shaping non-monotonic reasoning in artificial intelligence and have had lasting impacts in AI, law, and cognitive science. Pollock also developed the OSCAR project, a software prototype he called an "artilect," which implemented defeasible reasoning and explored cognitive architecture, including intentions, interests, and problem-solving strategies.

In addition to defeasible reasoning, Pollock authored *Nomic Probability and the Foundations of Induction* (1990), which examined the relationship between defeasible reasoning and probabilistic inference from frequencies. He also wrote extensively on topics such as subjunctive reasoning, language and thought, and rational decision-making, with publications including *How to Build a Person* (1989) and *Cognitive Carpentry* (1995). His work bridges philosophy, artificial intelligence, and cognitive science, and he remains a significant figure in the development of rationality and knowledge theories.
