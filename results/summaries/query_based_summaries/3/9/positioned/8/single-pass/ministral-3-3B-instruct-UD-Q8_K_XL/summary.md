John L. Pollock was a prominent philosopher known for his contributions to epistemology and defeasible reasoning. Born in Atchison, Kansas, in 1940, Pollock earned a triple-major in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota in 1961. His academic career included faculty positions at institutions such as SUNY Buffalo, the University of Rochester, the University of Michigan, and the University of Arizona, where he significantly influenced the field of cognitive science.

Pollock’s most notable work, *Knowledge and Justification* (1974), established his reputation in epistemology by introducing the concept of **defeasible reasoning**, which allows for exceptions and flexibility in the conditions for knowledge. This approach diverged from traditional analytic criteria and emphasized practical, ceteris paribus conditions. His ideas were foundational in artificial intelligence, where non-monotonic reasoning became critical. Pollock’s work on defeasible reasoning, including concepts like "undercutting defeat" and "rebutting defeat," became central to debates in AI and legal reasoning.

Throughout his career, Pollock explored various philosophical topics, including the nature of knowledge, probability, and practical reasoning. His later projects, such as the OSCAR software prototype—a general-purpose defeasible reasoner—further integrated his ideas into computational frameworks. Pollock also authored books like *Nomic Probability and the Foundations of Induction* (1990) and *Thinking about Acting: Logical Foundations for Rational Decision Making* (2006), reflecting his interdisciplinary approach to philosophy and cognitive science.

Pollock’s legacy lies in his innovative contributions to epistemology and defeasible reasoning, which continue to shape discussions in AI, ethics, and the philosophy of mind. His work remains influential in understanding how knowledge and reasoning can be both rigorous and adaptable to real-world complexities.
