John L. Pollock (born January 28, 1940, in Atchison, Kansas) earned a triple-major degree in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota in 1961 and a Ph.D. in philosophy from the University of California, Berkeley in 1965 under Ernest Adams. He held faculty positions at SUNY Buffalo, the University of Rochester, the University of Michigan, and the University of Arizona, where he spent most of his career and helped found the Cognitive Science Program. A passionate mountain biker, he founded a riding club in Southern Arizona.

Pollock is best known for his foundational work in epistemology, particularly in the concept of defeasible reasoning—epistemic norms that are conditional and can be overridden. His influential book *Knowledge and Justification* (1974) established him as a leading figure in American epistemology by introducing defeasible reasoning as a framework for knowledge, moving beyond rigid "justified true belief" criteria. This work became central to both philosophy and artificial intelligence, influencing non-monotonic reasoning.

He developed the idea of "undercutting" and "rebutting" defeat, which are now standard in defeasible reasoning. Later, he expanded his work to include "self-defeat" and other defeat mechanisms. Pollock's ideas were further explored in his software project OSCAR, an AI prototype he called an "artilect," designed to implement defeasible reasoning in a cognitive architecture. The project aimed to model reasoning about perception, change, causation, and decision-making, with a focus on interest-based reasoning.

His later work, *Nomic Probability and the Foundations of Induction* (1990), examined the relationship between defeasible reasoning and probability estimation from frequencies. Pollock also contributed to philosophy of AI and cognitive science, publishing extensively on topics including rational decision-making, perception, and the foundations of knowledge. He is widely recognized as "Mr. Defeasible Reasoning" and is considered a key intellectual figure in bridging philosophy, artificial intelligence, and cognitive science.
