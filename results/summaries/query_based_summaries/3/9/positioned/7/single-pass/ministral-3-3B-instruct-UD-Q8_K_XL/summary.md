John L. Pollock was a prominent philosopher known for his contributions to epistemology and defeasible reasoning. Born in 1940, he earned a triple-major in physics, mathematics, and philosophy from the University of Minnesota (1961) and later earned his Ph.D. from the University of California, Berkeley (1965). His work, particularly *Knowledge and Justification* (1974), introduced the concept of **defeasible reasoning**, which shifted philosophical epistemology from rigid, monolithic criteria to more flexible, context-dependent norms. Pollock’s ideas emphasized that knowledge is not absolute but contingent on evidence and potential counterarguments, aligning with the broader trend in artificial intelligence and cognitive science to model human reasoning with non-monotonic logic.

Pollock’s most notable innovations include the distinction between **undercutting defeat** and **rebutting defeat** in reasoning, which became foundational in AI and logic circles. His work on **OSCAR**, a prototype artificial intelligence system, further explored how to implement defeasible reasoning in software, blending philosophical theory with practical AI development. Additionally, his research on **nomic probability** and **induction** examined how probability estimation could be grounded in empirical frequencies, offering a bridge between inductive logic and statistical inference. Pollock’s influence extended beyond academia, shaping discussions on AI ethics, legal reasoning, and the philosophical foundations of artificial intelligence. His contributions remain central to debates on how machines can simulate human-like reasoning while accounting for uncertainty and exceptions.
