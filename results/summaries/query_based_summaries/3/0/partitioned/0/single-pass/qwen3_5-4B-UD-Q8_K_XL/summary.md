**Deepfake** technology refers to the use of artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning and generative adversarial networks (GANs), to synthesize realistic but falsified video and audio content. Historically rooted in academic research on computer vision since the 1990s, the term gained prominence in late 2017 with the release of the first automated facial reanimation systems. These tools function by encoding facial features into a latent space, allowing the reconstruction of a target's face onto any body or the synthesis of new identities. While early applications focused on entertainment and art, deepfakes have rapidly expanded into commercial uses, such as creating avatars for corporate training (e.g., Synthesia) and personalized media (e.g., Zao). However, this capability also enables severe misuse, including non-consensual pornography, fraud, political disinformation, and the creation of "sockpuppets" or fake news to undermine trust.

The proliferation of deepfakes has sparked significant academic, ethical, and regulatory responses. Researchers have developed detection methods analyzing artifacts like blinking patterns and lighting inconsistencies, though these are often outpaced by improved generation techniques. Ethical concerns focus on the epistemic threat deepfakes pose to truth and the potential for malicious applications in democratic societies. Consequently, various legal frameworks have been proposed or enacted, including the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act, the UK's Online Safety Act, and U.S. state-level legislation aimed at prohibiting non-consensual intimate imagery and holding creators accountable. Despite these measures, challenges remain regarding media literacy, the speed of technological evolution, and the difficulty of distinguishing synthetic media from authentic content.
