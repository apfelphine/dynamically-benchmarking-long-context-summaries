Deepfakes are AI-generated synthetic media, encompassing both static images and dynamic video/audio, that use machine learning to create realistic representations of non-existent individuals or alter the appearance and voices of real people. Developed from academic research in the 1990s and refined by open-source tools like DeepFaceLab, the technology has evolved rapidly, making genuine content increasingly indistinguishable from fakes within a short timeframe. This capability poses significant risks, including the creation of "sockpuppets" for partisan propaganda, the dissemination of disinformation that undermines democratic trust, and severe reputational damage through defamation, blackmail, and non-consensual pornography. The proliferation of deepfakes is driven by malicious actors and commercial entities aiming to exploit public figures for scams, fraud, and political manipulation, often bypassing skepticism through high-quality visuals and convincing audio.

To combat these threats, a multi-faceted approach involving detection, regulation, and media literacy is being pursued. Detection methods range from analyzing subtle artifacts in facial blinking and eye reflections to using blockchain verification and watermarking to trace media origins. Legal frameworks, such as the EU AI Act, the UK's Online Safety Act, and various U.S. state laws, are emerging to criminalize malicious deepfakes, particularly those involving non-consensual intimate imagery or political misinformation. Simultaneously, initiatives like Microsoft's Deepfake Detection Challenge and DARPA's Semantic Forensics program aim to accelerate the development of robust identification tools. Ultimately, mitigating the societal impact of deepfakes relies heavily on fostering critical thinking and media literacy among populations to navigate the complex landscape of synthetic media.
