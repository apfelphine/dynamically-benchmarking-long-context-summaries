Deepfake technology originated from photo manipulation in the 19th century and evolved through digital video, with researchers at academic institutions beginning to develop methods in the 1990s and later in online communities. Deepfakes, which involve altering media using AI tools, are categorized by academic research into computer vision—focused on creation and identification—and humanities/social science approaches examining the ethical, social, and informational implications.

Key technical advancements include early projects like the "Video Rewrite" program (1997), which automated facial reanimation, and contemporary methods utilizing autoencoders and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to create highly realistic videos. Applications span entertainment (e.g., digital clones for films), art, and malicious purposes such as fraud, scams, and political disinformation. Concerns center on deepfakes posing an "epistemic threat" to knowledge, undermining public trust in information, and potentially harming public figures and reputations. Countermeasures being researched include detection methods focusing on subtle inconsistencies in audio and video, as well as promoting media literacy.
