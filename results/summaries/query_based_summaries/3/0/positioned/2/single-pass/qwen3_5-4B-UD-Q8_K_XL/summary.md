Deepfakes are a form of synthetic media generated using artificial intelligence, specifically deep learning techniques like autoencoders and generative adversarial networks (GANs), to create realistic but falsified images, videos, and audio. Originating from academic research in the 1990s and gaining prominence in late 2017, these technologies allow for the manipulation of facial expressions, voice cloning, and body movements, often by swapping one person's face onto another or creating entirely new identities. While early applications included amateur creations and entertainment, deepfakes have evolved into significant tools for commercial use, fraud, blackmail, and political disinformation, with researchers estimating global fraud losses reaching billions of dollars. The technology's ability to produce convincing content poses serious risks to democratic societies, personal reputations, and trust in information, leading to widespread concerns regarding the inability to distinguish between genuine and fabricated media.

Efforts to address the deepfake crisis involve a multi-faceted approach including technical detection, regulatory frameworks, and media literacy. Detection methods utilize algorithms to identify artifacts and inconsistencies in synthetic media, though the "moving goal post" challenge of rapidly improving generation and detection techniques remains. Governments and organizations have responded with legislation, such as the EU AI Act and various U.S. state laws, aiming to ban non-consensual intimate imagery and mandate transparency for AI-generated content. Social media platforms have implemented policies to remove or flag deepfakes, while experts advocate for public education on media literacy to help individuals critically evaluate sources. Despite these measures, the ease of creation and the emotional impact of deepfakes continue to drive their propagation, requiring ongoing collaboration between technologists, policymakers, and the public to mitigate potential harms.
