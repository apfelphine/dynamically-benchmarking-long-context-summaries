## Deepfake Summary

Deepfake technology uses advanced neural networks, particularly autoencoders and generative adversarial networks (GANs), to create highly realistic and convincing fake audio, images, and videos. Originally developed by academic researchers in computer vision since the 1990s, deepfakes surged into public awareness through online communities in 2017. Their applications span entertainment, art, advertising, digital resurrection of deceased individuals, satire, and film production; they have also become tools for blackmail, fraud, scams, identity masking, and targeted disinformation, including political campaigns and non-consensual pornography. Deepfakes are especially concerning for their ability to erode trust in visual media, posing risks to democratic processes, reputations, and personal safety. Forensics research has focused on detecting deepfakes through identifying artifacts and inconsistencies, but the advancement of the technology makes detection a constantly moving target.

Social science and humanities research examines deepfakes' impact on identity, gender, art, and ethical questions surrounding misinformation and consent. Studies show that engagement with deepfakes on social media is driven by emotional impact, age, and literacy about the technology. Legal and regulatory measures are rapidly evolving worldwide, with specific legislation targeting deepfakes for issues such as non-consensual intimate imagery, election interference, and fraudulent endorsements. Social media platforms and governments have responded with bans, labeling, and detection challenges, while education in media literacy is seen as an important tool for mitigating deepfake-related harms. Despite promising technical and policy-based countermeasures, concerns persist about the future of truth, detection reliability, and the vulnerability of marginalized groups to deepfake exploitation.
