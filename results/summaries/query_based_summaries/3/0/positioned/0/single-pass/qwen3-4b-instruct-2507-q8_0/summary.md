Deepfake technology, which uses artificial intelligence to create or manipulate video and audio content, has evolved from early academic research in the 1990s to widespread use by amateurs and industry. It relies on neural networks, such as autoencoders and generative adversarial networks (GANs), to generate realistic content by learning from existing data. Key applications include entertainment (e.g., creating virtual avatars or parodies), acting (e.g., de-ageing actors or replacing faces in films), and art (e.g., digital installations or satirical videos). However, deepfakes are also widely used in malicious ways, such as generating fraudulent content for scams, blackmail, and political disinformation, including deepfakes of politicians and celebrities that mislead public opinion. 

The technology poses significant societal risks, including erosion of trust in media, the potential for widespread misinformation, and threats to individual privacy and reputations. Concerns about identity masking, fraud, and the use of deepfakes in political campaigns have led to growing calls for detection, regulation, and media literacy. While detection methods, such as analyzing subtle inconsistencies in facial movements or using AI to identify artifacts, have been developed, the rapid evolution of deepfakes makes them increasingly difficult to distinguish from authentic content. Efforts to combat deepfakes include detection tools, legal frameworks (e.g., regulations in the EU, U.S., and China), and public education, though challenges remain in enforcement and public awareness. The technology's ability to fabricate messages and actions of others raises serious ethical and legal questions, particularly regarding consent, misinformation, and the potential for democratic erosion.
