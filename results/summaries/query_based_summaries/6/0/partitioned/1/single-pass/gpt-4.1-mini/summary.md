## Summary of Information on Donald Knuth

Donald Knuth, born in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, is a prominent computer scientist known for his seminal work *The Art of Computer Programming (TAOCP)*, originally intended as a single volume but expanded to multiple volumes, with volumes 1 to 4B published between 1968 and 2022. He began TAOCP after earning a PhD in mathematics from Caltech in 1963 and taught at institutions including Caltech, Princeton, and Stanford. Knuth also authored *Concrete Mathematics*, developed from coursework to support TAOCP, as well as *Surreal Numbers*, a mathematical novelette designed to foster creative mathematical research. His writings extend beyond computer science to include works on religion, such as *3:16 Bible Texts Illuminated*, reflecting his Lutheran faith.

In programming, Knuth created the TeX typesetting system and Metafont to address frustrations with existing typesetting quality. He pioneered *literate programming*, promoting programming as a human-readable literary activity, exemplified by the WEB system that integrates documentation and source code. He used WEB to develop TeX and Metafont, both published as books in 1986. Knuth is known for his humorous "finders' fee" for typographical errors in his books, reflecting his dedication to precision and engagement with readers.

Knuth has received numerous prestigious awards, including the first ACM Grace Murray Hopper Award (1971), the Turing Award (1974), the National Medal of Science (1979), the John von Neumann Medal (1995), and the Kyoto Prize (1996). He was elected to various academies and societies, such as the National Academy of Sciences, the Royal Society, and the American Mathematical Society. Knuth holds the unique academic title of Professor Emeritus of The Art of Computer Programming. Beyond academia, he is an organist and composer, creating works inspired by religious texts.

His comprehensive list of publications includes multiple volumes of *The Art of Computer Programming*, the *Computers and Typesetting* series (including *The TeXbook*), and collections of papers on algorithms, programming languages, and digital typography, cementing his legacy as a foundational figure in computer science.
