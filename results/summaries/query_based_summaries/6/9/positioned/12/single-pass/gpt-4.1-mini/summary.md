**Jacek Karpiński** (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and innovator born in Turin, Italy, into a family of intellectuals and mountaineers. He endured a challenging youth marked by active participation in the Polish resistance during World War II and severe injuries including paralysis and a gunshot wound. Post-war, Karpiński quickly advanced academically, studying electromechanical sciences and graduating from Warsaw University of Technology in 1951.

Karpiński's pioneering work in computing began in the 1950s, including the construction of the AAH (Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics) in 1957 and the transistor-based AKAT-1 differential equations analyzer in 1959, regarded as the first of its kind. He received recognition via a UNESCO scholarship, enabling extensive research visits in the United States, where he met notable scientists including Claude Shannon. Inspired by these experiences, Karpiński built one of the earliest perceptrons in 1964. He designed the KAR-65 computer (1968), the fastest Polish computer at the time, and later the highly innovative K-202 minicomputer (early 1970s), noted for its advanced architectural features like modularity, memory segmentation with paging, and superior speed, comparable to leading Western models.

Despite technical success, Karpiński’s projects faced political and institutional opposition within communist Poland, hindering production and recognition. He was forced to leave major research institutes and eventually moved to rural life before working on tape recorders in Switzerland. In the 1990s, he returned to Poland, attempting further ventures. Posthumously, Karpiński was honored with national orders for his contributions.

Controversially, archival evidence revealed Karpiński cooperated as a secret informant for Polish intelligence from 1961, providing extensive technological information during his travels. His scientific legacy is debated, with supporters emphasizing his innovations and opponents citing exaggerations and political context for his projects’ failures. His life and work remain influential in Polish computer history and culture, including literary portrayal.
