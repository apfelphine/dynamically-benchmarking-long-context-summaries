Jacek Karpiński was born in 1927 in Turin, Italy, into a family of Polish intellectuals and alpinists. His father, Adam Karpiński, was an aeronautic engineer and inventor, while his mother, Wanda Czarnocka-Karpińska, was a respected physician and later Dean of the University of Physical Education in Warsaw. The family moved to Warsaw in 1934.

During World War II, Karpiński joined the Gray Ranks, a Polish underground paramilitary organization, and participated in numerous resistance operations, including the Sieczychy Operation and Operation Kutschera. He was severely injured in 1943 during a sabotage operation, losing both eyes and facing a serious threat to his hand, but recovered fully. He also participated in the Warsaw Uprising, where he was wounded and remained paralyzed after the conflict.

After the war, Karpiński resumed his education and completed high school in one year, passing with flying colors. He studied at the Faculty of Electro-Mechanical Sciences in Łódź and later earned a degree from the Warsaw University of Technology in 1951. He worked in the Polish Academy of Sciences' Institute of Basic Problems, where he designed the prototype of an ultrasonography device.

His first major project was the AAH — Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics, a device developed in 1957 to improve weather forecasting precision. The machine was later destroyed. His breakthrough achievement was the construction of AKAT-1 in 1959, the world's first differential equations analyzer based on transistors. The machine was praised for its aesthetic design, with a panel designed by leading Polish artists.

In 1960, Karpiński was awarded the UNESCO scholarship for young engineers. Inspired by his American experience, he developed the perceptron, a device capable of recognizing and discerning objects and shapes, one of the first of its kind in the world and the first known in the Communist bloc.

In 1965, Karpiński designed the KAR-65, a computer capable of performing 100,000 operations per second, which was the fastest Polish computer at the time. Despite its technical excellence, the computer was not widely adopted due to political and administrative resistance.

In 1970, Karpiński founded the Microcomputers' Construction Plant (Zakład Budowy Mikrokomputerów) to develop a minicomputer, the K-202. Though the project was technically innovative and included features like memory segmentation and paging, it failed to reach mass production due to political opposition, financial constraints, and lack of institutional support. Only 30 units were produced.

After the failure of K-202, Karpiński moved to the countryside near Olsztyn and later worked on Nagra tape recorders in Switzerland. In the 1990s, he served as an advisor on computer science to Andrzej Olechowski and Leszek Balcerowicz. He received the Officer's Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2009 and was posthumously awarded the Commander's Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2010.

Karpiński is also known for his controversial work as a secret informant for the Służba Bezpieczeństwa, the Polish intelligence agency, from 1961 onward, where he provided extensive technological data, a practice he claimed was motivated by the need to serve national interests. His work has been debated, with some claiming it was a significant contribution to Poland's technological development, while others argue it was a political misstep.

Karpiński died on 21 February 2010 in Wrocław, Poland.
