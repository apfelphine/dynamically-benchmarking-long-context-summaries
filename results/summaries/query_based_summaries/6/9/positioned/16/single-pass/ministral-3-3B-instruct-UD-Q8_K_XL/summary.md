Jacek Karpiński was a pioneering Polish engineer and computer scientist, born in 1927 in Turin, Italy, into a family of intellectuals and mountaineers. His father, Adam Karpiński, was an aeronautic engineer and inventor, while his mother, Wanda Czarnocka-Karpińska, was a physician and winter mountaineering pioneer. Karpiński’s early life was marked by tragedy, including the death of his father during an expedition in the Himalayas.

During World War II, Karpiński joined the Polish underground resistance, serving in the Gray Ranks and later in the Home Army, where he earned the Cross of Valour three times. After the war, he attended university in Warsaw, graduating in 1951. His career in engineering began with contributions to scientific research, including his work on the AAH (Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics), a vacuum-tube-based computer used for weather forecasting, which improved forecast accuracy by 10%.

Karpiński’s most notable achievements include the development of **AKAT-1**, the world’s first differential equations analyzer based on transistors, completed in 1959. This project earned him a UNESCO scholarship to study in the United States, where he met influential figures like John Eckert and Claude Shannon. Upon returning, he designed the **perceptron**, one of the first artificial neural network devices in the Eastern Bloc.

His most ambitious project was **KAR-65**, a transistor-based computer built in 1968 for analyzing data from CERN experiments. Though technically successful, its publication was censored, and Karpiński faced bureaucratic obstacles. His later work, **K-202**, a modular minicomputer using Intel’s 4004 microchips, was innovative but never reached mass production due to political and economic pressures.

Karpiński’s legacy includes controversies surrounding his collaboration with Polish intelligence, particularly during his time in the United States. Despite these challenges, he was posthumously honored with the **Commander’s Cross of Polonia Restituta** (2010) and remains a revered figure in Polish computer science history. His contributions to early computing and neural networks reflect his visionary approach to technology and innovation.
