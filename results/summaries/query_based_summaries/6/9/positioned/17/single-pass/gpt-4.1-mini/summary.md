## Summary: Jacek Karpiński

Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer known for pioneering work in computer design during the mid-20th century. Born into a family of Polish intellectuals and alpinists, he endured wartime hardships including injury during resistance efforts in World War II. After the war, he rapidly advanced academically and professionally, graduating from Warsaw University of Technology in 1951. Karpiński led early projects such as the Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics (AAH) in 1957 and constructed AKAT-1 in 1959, the world's first transistor-based differential equations analyzer, noted for both technical innovation and aesthetic design.

Karpiński gained international recognition through a UNESCO scholarship that allowed him to visit major U.S. technological centers, where he interacted with leading scientists. Inspired by developments abroad, he built one of the first perceptrons in 1964 and developed scanners and computing machines such as KAR-65 (1968) and most notably the K-202 minicomputer (early 1970s). The K-202 was a modular, highly innovative minicomputer using Intel 4004 microchips and featuring advanced technologies like memory segmentation with paging and asynchronous operation, making it faster than many contemporaries. Despite its technical excellence, K-202 never reached mass production, largely due to political and institutional opposition in communist Poland, as well as internal conflicts and economic constraints.

Later in life, Karpiński moved away from large-scale computer engineering to work in Switzerland and attempted entrepreneurial ventures. He was posthumously honored with high Polish state orders recognizing his contributions. Controversially, it was revealed that since 1961 he had been a secret informant for Polish intelligence, mainly providing technological information. His legacy is debated: while some hail him as a visionary whose talents were stifled by politics, others question the full extent of his achievements. His work is preserved in Polish technology museums, and he remains a notable figure in the country's computing history.
