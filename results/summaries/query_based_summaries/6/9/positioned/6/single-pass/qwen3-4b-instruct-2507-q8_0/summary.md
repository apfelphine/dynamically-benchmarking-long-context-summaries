Jacek Karpiński was born in 1927 in Turin, Italy, into a family of Polish intellectuals and alpinists. His father, Adam Karpiński, was a prominent aeronautic engineer and inventor, and his mother, Wanda Czarnocka-Karpińska, was a respected physician and later Dean of the University of Physical Education in Warsaw. Both were pioneers in winter mountaineering in the Tatra Mountains.

During World War II, Karpiński joined the Gray Ranks, a Polish underground paramilitary organization, and participated in numerous resistance operations, including the Warsaw Uprising. He was severely injured in 1943 while working on homemade bombs and lost sight in both eyes, though he recovered fully. After the war, he moved to Radomsko and later to Warsaw, where he completed his high school education and earned a degree from the Warsaw University of Technology.

Karpiński's early career included designing the AAH — Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics, a device to improve weather forecasting precision. His major breakthrough was the creation of AKAT-1, the world's first differential equations analyzer based on transistors, which was a pioneering achievement in computational engineering. This device was recognized for its innovative design and aesthetic appeal, with its panel designed by leading Polish artists.

Following the success of AKAT-1, Karpiński received a UNESCO scholarship to study in the United States, where he visited major technological centers and met influential computer scientists. After returning, he designed the perceptron, a device inspired by Frank Rosenblatt's ideas, one of the first of its kind in the world and the first known in the Communist bloc.

In 1965, he designed the KAR-65, a computer capable of performing 100,000 operations per second, which was the fastest Polish computer at the time. Despite financial and political challenges, the KAR-65 was built and used successfully in various institutions. Karpiński also led the development of the K-202 minicomputer, which, though not mass-produced due to political and economic obstacles, was technologically advanced and innovative.

Karpiński later moved to Switzerland to work on Nagra tape recorders and then returned to Poland in the 1990s to serve as an advisor on computer science. He was awarded the Officer's Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2009 and the Commander's Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2010 for his achievements in computer engineering.

He is also known for his controversial work as a secret informant for the Służba Bezpieczeństwa, where he provided intelligence on technological developments, which sparked debates about the scale and ethics of his engineering achievements. Despite this, his contributions to computer science and engineering remain significant, and he is widely recognized as a pioneering figure in the field.
