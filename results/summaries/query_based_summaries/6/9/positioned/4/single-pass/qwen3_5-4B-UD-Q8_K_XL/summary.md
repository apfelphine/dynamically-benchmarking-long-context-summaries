Jacek Karpiński was a Polish computer scientist born in 1927 whose pioneering work laid early foundations for modern computing. He is best known for designing the **AKAT-1** (1959), the world's first differential equations analyzer based on transistors, and the **KAR-65** (1968), a high-speed asynchronous computer that performed 100,000 operations per second. His most significant achievement, the **K-202** (1973), was an innovative minicomputer that utilized Intel 4004 microchips, floating-point representation, and memory segmentation with paging—features that made it faster than contemporary Western competitors like the PDP-11. Although only 30 units were produced due to political interference and a lack of industrial backing within the communist bloc, the K-202 remains a celebrated example of independent innovation.

Karpiński's career was marked by controversy, most notably his secret cooperation with the Polish secret police (Służba Bezpieczeństwa) starting in 1961, which involved reporting on American technological progress and was revealed decades later. Despite this, he received the Officer's Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2009 and was posthumously awarded the Commander's Cross in 2010 for his engineering achievements. He died in Wrocław on February 21, 2010. His legacy includes the preservation of his machines in the Museum of Technology in Warsaw and recognition as a visionary who created a computer architecture that anticipated future trends in microprocessor design.
