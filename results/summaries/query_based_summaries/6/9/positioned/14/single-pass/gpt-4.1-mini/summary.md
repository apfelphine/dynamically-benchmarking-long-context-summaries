**Jacek Karpiński** (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and innovator born into a family of intellectuals and pioneering alpinists. His father was a notable aeronautic engineer and inventor, and his mother a respected physician and academic. During World War II, Karpiński actively participated in the Polish resistance, sustaining injuries yet continuing his contributions to the Home Army and the Warsaw Uprising.

After the war, Karpiński quickly completed his education and graduated from Warsaw University of Technology in 1951. He embarked on pioneering work in computing and electronics, notably designing the AAH (Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics) in 1957 and the transistor-based differential equations analyzer AKAT-1 in 1959, recognized internationally. Awarded a UNESCO scholarship, he visited major U.S. technological centers and met leading computer scientists, including Claude Shannon.

Karpiński developed one of the earliest perceptrons in 1964 and designed significant Polish computers: KAR-65 (completed in 1968), which was the fastest Polish computer of its time, and the innovative K-202 minicomputer (early 1970s), which incorporated advanced features such as microchips and memory segmentation with paging. Despite technical excellence, K-202 faced political and institutional obstacles that prevented mass production, marking a lost opportunity for Poland’s technological advancement.

Later, Karpiński moved away from active engineering, engaging in business ventures and advising on computer science after the fall of communism. He was posthumously honored with high-level Polish state awards for his achievements. Controversially, he worked as a secret informant for Polish intelligence during the Cold War. His legacy remains as one of Poland’s foremost computer pioneers whose work was often hindered by the political climate of his time.
