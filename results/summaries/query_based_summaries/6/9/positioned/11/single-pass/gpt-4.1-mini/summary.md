**Jacek Karpiński** (1927–2010) was a Polish computer engineer and innovator born in Turin into a family of intellectuals and alpinists. Despite severe wartime injuries, including temporary blindness and paralysis during WWII, he actively participated in the Polish resistance and later resumed his education, graduating from Warsaw University of Technology in 1951. Karpiński made significant contributions to computing, notably designing early analog and transistor-based devices such as the Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics (AAH, 1957) and the transistor-based differential equations analyzer AKAT-1 (1959), considered pioneering works in their fields.

After receiving a UNESCO scholarship to study in the United States, where he met leading computer scientists like John Eckert and Claude Shannon, Karpiński returned to Poland and built one of the first perceptrons in the communist bloc (1964). He designed the scanner and computing machine KAR-65 (1968), the fastest Polish computer of its time, and led the development of the innovative modular minicomputer K-202 (early 1970s), which featured cutting-edge technology such as segmentation and paging memory. Despite its technological superiority over Western competitors and initial success, K-202 never entered mass production due to political opposition and systemic challenges within Poland's communist industry. Karpiński later worked in Switzerland and attempted various business ventures before returning to Poland, where he advised government officials. Posthumously, he was honored with high Polish state awards.

Karpiński's legacy is marked by both his technical innovations and the controversies surrounding his cooperation with Polish intelligence during the Cold War. His career exemplifies the difficulties faced by innovators working under restrictive political regimes, and he is remembered as a visionary whose achievements were often hindered by external forces.
