Jacek Karpiński was a pioneering Polish engineer and computer scientist, born in 1927 in Turin, Italy, into a family of Polish intellectuals and mountaineers. His father, Adam Karpiński, was an aeronautical engineer and inventor, while his mother, Wanda Czarnocka-Karpińska, was a physician and a prominent figure in winter mountaineering.

During World War II, Karpiński, then 14 years old, joined the Gray Ranks, a Polish underground paramilitary organization, and served in the Home Army. He was severely injured during an underground bomb-making operation, losing sight in both eyes and nearly losing a hand, but recovered fully. After the war, he continued his resistance activities and participated in the Warsaw Uprising, where he was wounded. Post-war, he attended high school in Radomsko, then studied at the University of Warsaw and the University of Łódź, graduating in 1951.

Karpiński’s early career included work at the Polish Academy of Sciences, where he developed the **AAH (Analytical Analyzer of Harmonics)**, a pioneering vacuum-tube-based computer used for weather forecasting. His most notable achievements include the **AKAT-1 (1959)**, the world’s first differential equations analyzer based on transistors, and the **KAR-65 (1968)**, a transistor-based mathematical computer that significantly advanced Polish computing capabilities.

Karpiński’s work was recognized internationally, leading to a UNESCO scholarship in the United States in 1960, where he visited major technological centers. He also designed a **perceptron**, one of the first known artificial neural networks in the Eastern Bloc. However, his most ambitious project, the **K-202 minicomputer**, was halted due to political and administrative opposition, despite its innovative features, including modular design and the use of Intel’s 4004 microchips.

Karpiński later worked on military and scientific applications, including advancements in radar and communication systems. He was awarded the Officer’s Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2009 and the Commander’s Cross of Polonia Restituta posthumously in 2010. His legacy includes contributions to early computer science, particularly in the development of transistor-based computing and neural networks, though his career was marked by political and institutional challenges.
