Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a pioneering Polish computer engineer and inventor whose career spanned the resistance during World War II and decades of post-war scientific achievement. Born into a family of intellectuals and alpinists, he joined the Home Army at age 14 but was severely injured during the Warsaw Uprising in 1944, losing vision in both eyes and suffering paralysis that left him unable to walk for the rest of his life. Despite his disabilities, he excelled academically, completing high school in one year and earning a degree in electro-mechanical sciences. His post-war career began with work on the AAH harmonic analyzer in 1957, followed by the construction of AKAT-1 in 1959, which became the world's first differential equations analyzer based on transistors. This breakthrough earned him a UNESCO scholarship in 1960, during which he met leading scientists like Claude Shannon and Edward Moore before deciding to return to Poland.

In the United States, Karpiński also served as a secret informant for Polish intelligence, reporting on American technological progress. Upon his return, he implemented the first perceptron in the Communist bloc in 1964. He subsequently designed the KAR-65 computer in 1968, a high-speed machine capable of 100,000 operations per second, and the innovative K-202 minicomputer in 1973, which utilized Intel 4004 chips and floating-point arithmetic. Although the K-202 was technically superior to its Western competitors like the PDP-11 and Modular One, it faced political opposition and bureaucratic sabotage, limiting production to only 30 units. Despite controversies regarding his cooperation with intelligence services and criticism from state officials, Karpiński was awarded the Officer's Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2009 and posthumously received the Commander's Cross in 2010 for his remarkable contributions to computer engineering.
