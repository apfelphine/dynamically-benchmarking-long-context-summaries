Jacek Karpiński was born in 1927 in Turin, Italy, into a family of Polish intellectuals and alpinists. His father, Adam Karpiński, was a prominent aeronautic engineer and inventor, while his mother, Wanda Czarnocka-Karpińska, was a physician. Karpiński’s early life was marked by tragedy, including the death of his father during an expedition in the Himalayas in 1939.

During World War II, Karpiński served in the Polish underground resistance, including the Home Army, where he faced severe injuries and near-paralysis after an explosion. Despite these hardships, he continued his involvement in the resistance, earning the Cross of Valour three times.

Post-war, Karpiński faced political and professional challenges, including accusations from communist security services (Urząd Bezpieczeństwa) due to his past in the Home Army. He was forced to change jobs frequently and eventually worked on designing a mini-submarine, which he abandoned after the Polish thaw. In 1955, he was offered a position at the Polish Academy of Sciences’ Institute of Basic Problems, where he worked on the design of an ultrasonography device.

Karpiński’s most notable achievements include the construction of **AKAT-1**, the world’s first differential equations analyzer based on transistors, completed in 1959. This innovation was recognized for its aesthetic design, featuring artwork by leading Polish artists, and its technical significance. AKAT-1 was well-received domestically and showcased Poland’s advancements in computing.

Later, Karpiński was awarded a UNESCO scholarship in the United States, where he visited major technological centers, including Harvard, Caltech, UCLA, and Los Alamos National Laboratory. Though he received offers to stay in the U.S., he returned to Poland, where he continued his work in computer science. He also implemented a **perceptron**, one of the first such devices in the world and the first known in the Communist Bloc.

Karpiński’s career was marked by controversy, including allegations of working as a secret informant for the communist intelligence services (Służba Bezpieczeństwa) from 1961 onward. Despite these claims, he was awarded the Officer’s Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2009 and posthumously the Commander’s Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2010 for his contributions to computer engineering. He died in Wrocław in 2010. His legacy remains a subject of historical debate, with critics and supporters offering differing interpretations of his achievements and motivations.
