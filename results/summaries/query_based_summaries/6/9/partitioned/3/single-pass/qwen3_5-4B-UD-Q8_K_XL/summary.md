# Jacek Karpiński

Jacek Karpiński (1927–2010) was a pioneering Polish computer scientist whose career was marked by significant technological achievements and deep involvement with state security services. Born into a family of intellectuals, he survived the Holocaust and served in the Polish Home Army during World War II, earning the Cross of Valour three times. Post-war, he worked on early computing projects, including the vacuum-tube AAH and the transistor-based AKAT-1 differential equations analyzer in 1959. His international reputation was solidified in 1960 when he won a UNESCO scholarship to the United States, where he met leading figures like Claude Shannon and Edward F. Moore. However, it was revealed that he had been a secret informant for the Polish secret service (Służba Bezpieczeństwa) since 1961, collecting extensive data on American technological centers and personalities in exchange for financial rewards and travel.

Karpiński returned to Poland to lead ambitious projects, most notably the K-202 minicomputer, which utilized the Intel 4004 chip and featured innovative architecture like memory segmentation. Despite its technical superiority over contemporary Western models, the project failed to achieve mass production due to political interference, administrative incompetence, and a shift in state priorities toward the "Riad" project. Disappointed by this outcome, he later worked on the KAR-65 computer and served as an advisor to Polish economic reformers in the 1990s. His contributions to computer engineering were recognized posthumously with the Commander's Cross of Polonia Restituta in 2010, while his legacy remains a subject of historical debate regarding the interplay between individual genius, state ideology, and the evolution of computing in Eastern Europe.
