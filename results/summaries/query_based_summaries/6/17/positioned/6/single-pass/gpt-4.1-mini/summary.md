## Summary of Lynn Conway

Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and electrical engineer born in Mount Vernon, New York. She experienced gender dysphoria early, beginning a gender transition attempt at MIT in 1957 but withdrew amid lack of support and detransitioned before later completing gender transition in 1968. Conway earned her B.S. and M.S.E.E. from Columbia University and was recruited by IBM Research in 1964, where she contributed to the Advanced Computing Systems project, inventing multiple-issue out-of-order dynamic instruction scheduling—considered the first superscalar architecture. After her transition, she was fired by IBM in 1968 but later received a lifetime achievement award and public apology from IBM in 2020.

Conway’s post-transition career included key roles at Memorex and Xerox PARC, where she led the LSI Systems group and co-developed the revolutionary Mead–Conway VLSI chip design methodology. She founded multiproject wafer (MPW) technology and contributed to the Metal Oxide Semiconductor Implementation Service (MOSIS) system, enabling rapid prototyping and fabrication of chip designs. She also introduced dimensionless, scalable design rules simplifying chip design and tools, profoundly influencing VLSI design education worldwide, including a celebrated course at MIT in 1978. She later joined DARPA as a principal architect of the Strategic Computing Initiative and became a professor and associate dean at the University of Michigan, retiring in 1998.

Conway’s legacy includes revolutionizing VLSI system design, fostering the growth of electronic design automation, and impacting the semiconductor industry and startups from the 1980s onward. She coined the "Conway effect," describing how marginalized innovators are often overlooked in historical accounts. In later years, Conway became a prominent transgender rights activist, raising awareness, providing resources, and advocating for equal opportunities and protections. She publicly shared her transition story from 1999 onward and was recognized as a trailblazer breaking both gender and transgender barriers in engineering. Conway passed away in June 2024 at age 86, leaving a lasting impact on computing, engineering, and transgender advocacy.

Her numerous honors include IEEE Fellow status (1985), membership in the National Academy of Engineering (1989), several honorary doctorates, the IEEE Computer Society’s Computer Pioneer Award (2009), the IEEE/RSE James Clerk Maxwell Medal (2015), and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame (2023). Conway’s life and work continue to inspire both technological innovation and social progress.
