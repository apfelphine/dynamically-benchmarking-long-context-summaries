## Lynn Conway

Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and electrical engineer, notable for her groundbreaking work in very-large-scale integration (VLSI) design methodology and computer architecture. Born in Mount Vernon, New York, she experienced gender dysphoria early on and began gender transition attempts during her education but faced social and medical obstacles. After completing her B.S. and M.S.E.E. degrees at Columbia University, Conway joined IBM Research in 1964, where she contributed to the architecture of an advanced supercomputer project and invented multiple-issue out-of-order dynamic instruction scheduling, considered one of the first superscalar designs.

Conway completed her gender transition in 1968, after which IBM fired her due to her plans to transition; the company formally apologized decades later. She resumed her career stealthily as a programmer and designer, later joining Xerox PARC in 1973. There, she led the LSI Systems group and, in collaboration with Carver Mead and Ivan Sutherland, co-authored the influential textbook *Introduction to VLSI Systems*, which catalyzed the "Mead–Conway revolution" in chip design education and methodology. She founded multiproject wafer (MPW) technology and pioneered dimensionless, scalable design rules that significantly simplified chip design and production. Her innovations led to the creation of MOSIS, an infrastructure for rapid prototyping and fabrication of integrated circuits.

Conway taught at MIT and was involved in DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative before serving as a professor and associate dean at the University of Michigan until her retirement in 1998. Her work had a lasting impact on semiconductor manufacturing, electronic design automation, and the semiconductor startup ecosystem. Beyond her technical contributions, Conway was a vocal transgender rights activist, advocating for awareness, acceptance, and medical and employment protections for transgender people. She documented and spoke openly about the "Conway effect," highlighting how contributions by marginalized groups are often overlooked in history. Conway passed away in 2024, leaving a legacy as both an engineering pioneer and an advocate for transgender rights.
