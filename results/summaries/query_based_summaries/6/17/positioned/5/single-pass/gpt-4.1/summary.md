## Lynn Conway

Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering computer scientist and electrical engineer whose groundbreaking contributions propelled the development of modern integrated circuit design. After earning degrees from Columbia University, Conway joined IBM in 1964, where she invented multiple-issue out-of-order dynamic instruction scheduling, laying the foundation for superscalar computer architecture. In 1968, after revealing her intention to transition genders, she was fired by IBM—a decision for which IBM formally apologized in 2020. After transitioning, Conway restarted her career in “stealth mode” and later joined Xerox PARC in 1973, where, in collaboration with Carver Mead, she co-developed the VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) design methodology. Their textbook, *Introduction to VLSI Systems*, revolutionized chip design, bringing scalable design rules and accessible prototyping infrastructure (MOSIS) to universities and small companies. Conway's methodologies fostered the rapid growth of the electronic design automation industry, the foundry model of chip manufacturing, and numerous technology startups.

Beyond technical innovation, Conway was a trailblazer for transgender rights and visibility. Following her public coming out in 1999, she became an advocate for transgender inclusion, employment protection, and equal opportunity in academia and high-tech industries. Through activism and educational resources, she supported other trans individuals and worked to advance policies within organizations such as the IEEE. Conway’s concept of the “Conway effect” called attention to how marginalized innovators are overlooked historically. Her legacy includes prestigious awards such as membership in the National Academy of Engineering, the IEEE/RSE James Clerk Maxwell Medal, the Computer Pioneer Award, and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. Lynn Conway passed away in June 2024, leaving indelible marks on computer science and the fight for transgender equality.
