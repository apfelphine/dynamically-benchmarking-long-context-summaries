Lynn Conway, born in 1938 in Mount Vernon, New York, is a pioneering computer scientist and a trailblazer in the field of Very-Large-Scale Integration (VLSI) design. She earned a B.S. and M.S.E.E. from Columbia University and worked at IBM, where she contributed to the development of advanced supercomputing systems. In 1968, after facing professional challenges due to her gender identity, she transitioned and began a new career in stealth mode, working as a digital system designer and computer architect at Memorex. 

At Xerox PARC, she co-founded the multiproject wafers (MPW) technology with Ivan Sutherland and Carver Mead, and co-authored the influential *Introduction to VLSI Systems*, which became a standard textbook in chip design. Her work introduced dimensionless, scalable design rules and developed the Metal Oxide Semiconductor Implementation Service (MOSIS), a prototype for rapid prototyping and short-run fabrication of chip designs. These innovations helped establish the foundry model for chip design and manufacturing and significantly advanced the VLSI design industry.

Conway is widely recognized for her contributions to computer science, receiving numerous awards, including the Electronics 1981 Award with Carver Mead, the IEEE EAB Major Educational Innovation Award, and the IEEE/RSE James Clerk Maxwell Medal. She was elected a Fellow of the IEEE, the National Academy of Engineering, and the Computer History Museum. In 2023, she was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame.

Beyond her technical achievements, Conway is also known for her advocacy in transgender rights, co-founding the "Conway Effect" to describe the phenomenon of marginalized individuals being overlooked in historical accounts of innovation. She has been a prominent figure in transgender rights activism, promoting understanding and equal opportunities for transgender people in the technology industry.

Her legacy extends to her personal life and resilience, including overcoming professional setbacks due to her gender identity and persisting in her work despite societal barriers. She is celebrated as a revolutionary pioneer in VLSI systems and a courageous advocate for inclusion and equity in science and technology.
