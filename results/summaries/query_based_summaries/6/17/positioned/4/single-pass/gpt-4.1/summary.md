## Lynn Conway: Summary

Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering American computer scientist and engineer whose innovations in microelectronics transformed the computing industry. After earning degrees from Columbia University, Conway joined IBM Research in 1964, where she was instrumental in developing the superscalar computer architecture, including dynamic instruction scheduling—a precursor to out-of-order execution in modern processors. IBM dismissed Conway in 1968 after she revealed plans to transition genders; decades later, IBM apologized and honored her contributions. Post-transition, Conway rebuilt her career in stealth, working at Memorex and later at Xerox PARC, where she led the LSI Systems group. Conway, collaborating with Carver Mead, co-authored "Introduction to VLSI Systems," a foundational textbook in chip design, and invented scalable design rules and the multiproject wafers (MPW) technology. She also institutionalized MOSIS, a critical chip prototyping infrastructure, catalyzing the VLSI design revolution and directly fostering the rise of the electronic design automation industry and the foundry model in chip manufacturing.

Conway joined DARPA in 1983 as a key architect of the Strategic Computing Initiative and later served as a professor and associate dean at the University of Michigan, specializing in visual communications and control systems. Throughout her career, Conway received numerous honors, including induction into the National Academy of Engineering, multiple honorary doctorates, the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award, and the IBM Lifetime Achievement Award. Approaching retirement, Conway publicly shared her gender transition, becoming an influential transgender rights activist and advocating for LGBT inclusion in tech and engineering communities. She maintained a website supporting transgender individuals and critiqued pathologizing frameworks within psychology. Conway died in 2024 at age 86, leaving a legacy as both a technical innovator and a trailblazer for transgender visibility and rights.
