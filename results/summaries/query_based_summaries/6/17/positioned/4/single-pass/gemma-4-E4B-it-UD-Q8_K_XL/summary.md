Lynn Conway was a pioneering computer architect, known for her significant contributions to microelectronics and computer design. She began her education at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology but withdrew in 1959 after experiencing gender dysphoria and detransitioning. After working as an electronics technician, she earned her B.S. and M.S.E.E. degrees from Columbia University's School of Engineering and Applied Science in 1962 and 1963.

At IBM Research, Conway was part of the team designing the Advanced Computing Systems (ACS) project, where she invented multiple-issue out-of-order dynamic instruction scheduling, which the Computer History Museum notes appears to be the first "superscalar" design. Following her gender transition in 1968, she began a career as a contract programmer, then worked as a digital system designer and computer architect at Memorex. She later joined Xerox PARC in 1973, where she co-authored *Introduction to VLSI Systems* and founded the multiproject wafers (MPW) technology. In collaboration with Carver Mead, she developed dimensionless, scalable design rules, and institutionalized the Metal Oxide Semiconductor Implementation Service (MOSIS) system in 1981, driving the Mead–Conway VLSI design revolution. After Xerox, she served at DARPA before joining the University of Michigan in 1985, specializing in visual communications and designing control systems.
