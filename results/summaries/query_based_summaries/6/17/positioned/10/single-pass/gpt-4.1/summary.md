## Lynn Conway: Key Contributions and Biography

Lynn Conway (1938–2024) was a pioneering American computer scientist, transgender activist, and educator whose work profoundly impacted computer engineering. After early studies at MIT and Columbia, Conway joined IBM Research in 1964, where she invented multiple-issue out-of-order dynamic instruction scheduling—laying the groundwork for modern superscalar computer architectures. IBM's firing of Conway in 1968 due to her gender transition marked a turning point, both personally and professionally. After transitioning, she rebuilt her career (often in stealth) at Computer Applications Inc., Memorex, and then Xerox PARC, where she led the "LSI Systems" group. At PARC, Conway co-developed the multiproject wafers (MPW) technology and, with Carver Mead, created the revolutionary VLSI (Very-Large-Scale Integration) design methodology. Their co-authored textbook, *Introduction to VLSI Systems*, became a foundational text, sparking a global transformation in chip design. Conway also created scalable design rules and the MOSIS infrastructure for rapid prototyping, democratizing access to chip fabrication for universities and startups.

Conway’s legacy broadened further through her roles as visiting associate professor at MIT, professor and associate dean at the University of Michigan, and as a key architect of DARPA’s Strategic Computing Initiative. Her innovations propelled the rise of the electronic design automation industry, the foundry model for chip production, and countless technology startups. In later life, Conway became an influential transgender activist, sharing her story and advocating for equal rights and inclusion in STEM and society. She received numerous honors, including the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award, membership in the National Academy of Engineering, and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. Conway’s advocacy for diversity and visibility in engineering—articulated through the "Conway effect"—remains an enduring part of her impact on technology and culture.
