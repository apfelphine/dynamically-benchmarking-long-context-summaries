Lynn Conway was a pioneering computer scientist and engineer whose contributions significantly advanced the field of Very-Large-Scale Integration (VLSI) design. Born in 1938, Conway initially struggled with gender dysphoria and detransitioned after a failed attempt at transitioning during her time at MIT. After working as an electronics technician, she resumed her education at Columbia University, earning degrees in electrical engineering.

Conway’s career was marked by groundbreaking work at IBM, where she contributed to the design of the Advanced Computing Systems (ACS) project, pioneering multiple-issue out-of-order dynamic instruction scheduling. Despite IBM firing her in 1968 for her gender transition, she later became a key figure in VLSI design, founding the Metal Oxide Semiconductor Implementation Service (MOSIS) system in 1981, which democratized access to advanced digital technology for universities and small companies.

Her collaboration with Carver Mead led to the development of *Introduction to VLSI Systems*, a foundational textbook in chip design, and the Mead-Conway revolution in VLSI methodologies. Conway’s innovations included scalable design rules, internet-based rapid prototyping, and the introduction of multiproject wafers (MPW), which streamlined chip design and manufacturing. She also championed the concept of the "Conway effect," highlighting the historical underrepresentation of women and people of color in technological advancements.

Beyond her technical contributions, Conway was a prominent advocate for transgender rights, using her platform to raise awareness, challenge pathologization, and promote understanding of gender identity. She co-founded the Grace Hopper Celebration of Women in Computing and was recognized for her activism in transgender rights, including her role in the Stonewall 40 trans heroes initiative.

Conway retired from active teaching in 1998 but continued her impact through mentorship and advocacy. She passed away in 2024 at the age of 86, leaving behind a legacy of innovation, resilience, and advocacy for diversity and inclusion in technology. Her work remains foundational in VLSI design and continues to inspire efforts to address systemic barriers in computing.
