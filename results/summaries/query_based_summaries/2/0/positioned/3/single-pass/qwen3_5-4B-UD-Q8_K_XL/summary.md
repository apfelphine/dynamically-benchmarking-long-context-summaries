Based strictly on the provided text, **embodied cognition** is a research program rather than a unified theory, emphasizing that cognition is deeply dependent on the physical body and its interactions with the environment. It opposes the disembodied Cartesian model by asserting that mental phenomena such as perception, attention, memory, reasoning, and emotion are shaped by sensorimotor capacities and bodily experiences. Key aspects include the view that cognition emerges in the relationship between an agent and environmental affordances, rather than occurring solely within the brain. The theory posits that abstract concepts are often grounded in bodily experiences through mechanisms like conceptual metaphors, image schemas, and prototypes, and that cognitive processes involve active perception-action loops rather than passive information processing.

Empirical evidence supporting embodied cognition includes studies showing that bodily states influence perception (e.g., distance perception varies with fitness or glucose levels), memory (e.g., body position facilitates autobiographical recall), and reasoning (e.g., motor experts perform better on spatial tasks when movement is inhibited). In language, comprehension activates motor simulations via mirror neurons, linking semantics to action. In emotion, theories like James–Lange suggest physiological arousal generates emotional experience, and facial expressions can modulate affective processing. Applications of this theory extend to education (using kinesthetic and gesture-based learning), robotics (designing situated robots with sensory-motor skills), and clinical settings (using body-oriented therapies for psychological difficulties). Despite its growing acceptance, the field faces challenges such as the replication crisis, where findings like "power posing" have failed to reproduce, and methodological debates regarding the distinction between embodied and symbolic processing in pre-verbal infants.
