## Embodied Cognition: Key Concepts and Theory

Embodied cognition asserts that cognitive processes are deeply rooted in the physical body and its interactions with the surrounding environment. Rather than viewing cognition as the result of abstract, isolated brain activity, the embodied cognition thesis highlights how bodily experiences, including sensorimotor capacities and cultural context, actively shape perception, attention, memory, reasoning, language, and emotion. This perspective contrasts with Cartesian dualism, emphasizing that cognition emerges from the dynamic interplay between the brain, body, and environment, and should be seen as a research program encompassing multiple approaches, including situated and extended cognition.

Empirical evidence from philosophy, psychology, neuroscience, linguistics, artificial intelligence, and robotics supports the embodied cognition framework. Studies demonstrate that sensorimotor systems are involved in understanding language, remembering experiences, reasoning, and emotional processing—the body influences not only how individuals perceive and interact with the world, but also how they self-regulate, learn, and engage socially. Applications of embodied cognition span varied fields such as education (kinesthetic and gesture-based learning), AI and robotics (emphasizing sensory-motor integration), clinical practice, sport psychology, music cognition, and social psychology. Though widely supported, the approach faces methodological challenges, including the need for clear operational definitions and ongoing debates related to early cognitive development and replicability of certain findings.
