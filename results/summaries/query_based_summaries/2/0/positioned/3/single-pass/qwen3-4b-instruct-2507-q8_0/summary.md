**Embodied cognition** is a theoretical framework asserting that cognition is fundamentally shaped by the body and its interactions with the environment, challenging the traditional Cartesian view of the mind as a disembodied, abstract processor. It emphasizes that perception, memory, reasoning, language, emotion, and social cognition are deeply rooted in bodily experiences and sensorimotor processes. Key principles include the idea that cognition is not merely a product of internal representations but emerges from active engagement with the world, involving perception-action cycles, bodily states, and environmental context. 

The theory has roots in phenomenology, which highlights the body as the primary site of experience, and is supported by empirical research in neuroscience, psychology, and robotics. Evidence shows that bodily actions influence cognitive processes—such as memory recall, language comprehension, and decision-making—through sensorimotor simulations. For instance, motor actions can enhance memory, and language understanding involves the simulation of actions. 

Embodied cognition has broad applications in education, robotics, clinical psychology, and social interaction, promoting teaching methods that integrate physical movement and real-world interaction. In robotics, it supports the development of systems that interact dynamically with their environment. In social cognition, it explains how nonverbal synchrony and perspective-taking are grounded in bodily experiences. 

Despite its influence, the theory faces criticism, including the "binding problem" (how sensory inputs are integrated), concerns about preverbal infants' cognitive development, and the replication crisis in some findings. Critics argue that the theory may not fully account for the complexity of human cognition or the role of symbolic processing. Nevertheless, embodied cognition remains a foundational and evolving research program in cognitive science, emphasizing the inseparability of mind and body in understanding intelligence and behavior.
