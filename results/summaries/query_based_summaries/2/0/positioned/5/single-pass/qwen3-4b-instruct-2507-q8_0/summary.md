Embodied cognition is a theoretical framework in cognitive science that asserts cognition is deeply rooted in the body and its interactions with the environment, challenging the traditional Cartesian view of the mind as a disembodied, abstract processor. It emphasizes that perception, memory, language, reasoning, emotion, and self-regulation are not isolated mental processes but are shaped by sensorimotor experiences, bodily states, and dynamic interactions with the world. Key principles include the idea that cognition is situated, time-pressured, and involves offloading cognitive work to the environment. The theory is supported by empirical evidence from neuroscience, psychology, robotics, and linguistics, showing that bodily actions influence cognitive processes, such as perception, memory, and language comprehension. For instance, motor actions can enhance memory recall, and understanding language involves simulating the actions described in it. Embodied cognition has practical applications in education, robotics, clinical settings, sports, and music, promoting embodied learning methods, intelligent robots, and therapies. Despite its broad support, the theory faces challenges such as the binding problem, difficulties in studying preverbal infants, and replication crises, with critics questioning the extent to which bodily experiences are truly foundational to cognition.
