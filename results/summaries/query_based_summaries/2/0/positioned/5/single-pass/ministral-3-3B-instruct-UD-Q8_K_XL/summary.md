Embodied cognition posits that cognition is fundamentally shaped by an agent’s bodily experiences and interactions with the environment, rather than being solely a product of abstract mental processes. This perspective rejects the Cartesian dualism that separates mind from body, emphasizing that cognitive functions—such as perception, memory, reasoning, and emotion—are deeply intertwined with physical and sensorimotor capacities. The theory argues that the body’s sensory and motor systems actively influence cognitive processes, including how we interpret the world, make decisions, and engage socially.

Key aspects of embodied cognition include:
- **Sensorimotor contingencies**: The quality of perception is determined by the knowledge of how sensory information changes when an agent acts in the world. For example, bending down to look under an object involves specific sensorimotor interactions that shape visual perception.
- **Extended cognition**: Cognitive processes extend beyond the brain into the environment, where tools, artifacts, and social contexts play active roles in shaping thought and action.
- **Situated cognition**: Cognition is inherently tied to the context in which it occurs, including cultural, social, and physical environments.
- **Enactive cognition**: Organisms obtain knowledge through active perception-action interactions with their environments, rather than passively receiving information.

Research in embodied cognition spans disciplines like neuroscience, psychology, philosophy, and robotics, offering insights into how human cognition develops and functions. It challenges traditional computational models of cognition, which often treat the brain as an isolated information processor, and instead highlights the importance of bodily experience, embodiment, and environmental engagement in shaping mental processes. This perspective has significant implications for understanding human behavior, education, and even the development of artificial intelligence, emphasizing the need for systems that integrate sensory and motor capabilities with cognitive functions.
