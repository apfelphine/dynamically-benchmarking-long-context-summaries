Based strictly on the provided text, **Embodied cognition** is a research program rather than a unified theory, positing that cognition is not merely the product of abstract representations but is deeply shaped by the agent's body, sensorimotor capacities, and interactions with the environment. It opposes the disembodied Cartesian model by emphasizing that perception, memory, reasoning, and language are grounded in physical embodiment. Key aspects include the idea that cognition requires active exploration and modification of the environment, where the body plays a constitutive role in processing information. Research indicates that abstract concepts are often understood through bodily experiences and image schemas, and that motor systems are activated during language comprehension and memory recall. Furthermore, the theory suggests that cognitive processes are situated within cultural and social contexts, leading to the "4E" framework of embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended cognition.

The text outlines various applications and evidences supporting this thesis. In **linguistics**, studies show that conceptual metaphors and prototypes rely on bodily experiences, and that reading comprehension is enhanced when actions match the described content. In **memory**, body positions can facilitate the recall of autobiographical memories, and trauma is re-experienced somatically. In **reasoning**, motor experts outperform non-experts in mental rotation tasks, and gestures aid in mathematical and logical problem-solving. In **emotion**, physiological states and facial expressions influence how emotional information is processed, supporting the James–Lange theory. The theory also impacts **education** through kinesthetic learning and robotics, where embodied artificial intelligence aims to create systems that interact with the physical world rather than relying solely on symbolic computation. However, the text notes controversies regarding the replication of findings (e.g., power posing) and the challenge of distinguishing embodied processing from symbolic processing in preverbal infants.
