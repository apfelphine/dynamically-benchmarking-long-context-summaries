Embodied cognition posits that an agent's cognition is not merely an abstract product of the brain but is actively and significantly shaped by the body beyond the brain itself. This thesis challenges the disembodied Cartesian model by reintroducing the agent's bodily experiences into any account of cognition. The enactive approach specifies that "embodiment" highlights two key aspects: cognition depends on experiences derived from having a body with diverse sensorimotor capacities, and these capacities are embedded within a broader biological, psychological, and cultural context.

A narrower view specifies embodied cognition as features of cognition being deeply dependent upon the physical body, meaning the agent's "beyond-the-brain body" plays a significant causal or physically constitutive role in cognitive processing, influencing aspects like perception, memory, and reasoning. This perspective contrasts with extended cognition (which extends processing into the environment) and situated cognition (which emphasizes interaction with the world). This shift accounts for a more encompassing view of cognition, suggesting it emerges from the relationship between an agent and the environment's affordances, rather than being solely internal to the brain.
