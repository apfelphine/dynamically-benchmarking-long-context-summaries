## Embodied Cognition: Overview and Key Concepts

Embodied cognition is a theoretical perspective asserting that cognitive processes are deeply influenced by the body and its interactions with the environment. Unlike the traditional disembodied or Cartesian model—which regards the mind as separate from the body—embodied cognition holds that an agent’s cognitive capacities (such as perception, memory, reasoning, and language) depend not only on the brain but also on bodily experiences and sensorimotor systems. This approach emphasizes that cognition is shaped by the physical attributes of the body, the biological, psychological, and cultural contexts, and the organism’s ongoing actions in the world. As such, embodied cognition is seen as a broad research program rather than a unified theory, encompassing related ideas like situated and extended cognition.

Historically, embodied cognition arose in opposition to mechanistic and symbolic approaches to mind, drawing inspiration from phenomenology (e.g., Merleau-Ponty) and fields such as neuroscience, psychology, and robotics. It posits that cognition emerges from dynamic interactions between brain, body, and environment, and that features like language, learning, memory, and reasoning are tied to sensorimotor experiences. Empirical research supporting embodiment includes findings in linguistics, neuroscience, education, robotics, and social psychology, showing that bodily states and actions affect cognitive tasks from perception to emotion regulation. Applications span education (kinesthetic and gesture-based learning), AI and robotics (embodied and situated AI), clinical settings, sports, and music, while ongoing debates address methodological challenges and distinctions from purely symbolic computation.
