**Embodied Cognition** posits that cognition is fundamentally shaped by an agent’s bodily interactions with the environment, rather than being confined to purely abstract mental processes. This theory rejects the Cartesian view that cognition is independent of the body, emphasizing instead that sensory-motor experiences, bodily states, and environmental affordances are essential to understanding mental processes. The embodied cognition thesis is rooted in critiques of traditional computational models of cognition, which were deemed insufficient to explain human cognitive abilities, particularly those involving perception, memory, reasoning, and emotion.

Researchers in embodied cognition argue that cognition is not merely the manipulation of symbols but an active, dynamic process influenced by the body’s physical and social contexts. This perspective has been influenced by phenomenological traditions (e.g., Merleau-Ponty), enactivism (e.g., Varela, Thompson, and Rosch), and empirical studies demonstrating the role of sensorimotor experiences in cognitive functions. For instance, studies show that language comprehension involves motor simulations, memory retrieval is influenced by bodily positioning, and reasoning benefits from embodied gestures. The theory also extends to social cognition, where bodily interactions and nonverbal synchrony play a critical role in social perception and judgment.

Embodied cognition has broad implications across disciplines, including neuroscience, psychology, linguistics, and artificial intelligence. It challenges traditional views by advocating for a more holistic understanding of cognition, integrating the body, environment, and cultural context. This shift has led to the development of the 4E framework (embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended cognition), highlighting how cognition is not confined to the brain but is distributed across the body and its surroundings. The theory continues to inspire innovative educational, clinical, and technological applications, such as embodied learning methods in education and the design of more human-like artificial intelligence systems.
