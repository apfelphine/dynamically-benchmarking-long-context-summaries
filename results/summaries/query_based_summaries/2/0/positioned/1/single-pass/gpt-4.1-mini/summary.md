# Summary of Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition is a broad research program emphasizing that cognition is deeply influenced and shaped by the body's physical characteristics, sensorimotor capacities, and its interactions with the environment. Opposing the classical view that cognition is a product of disembodied abstract representations (Cartesian dualism), embodied cognition maintains that many cognitive functions—such as perception, memory, reasoning, language, and emotion—depend causally or constitutively on the body beyond the brain. The enactive approach further situates these capacities within biological, psychological, and cultural contexts, highlighting cognition as arising from dynamic body-environment engagements rather than solely internal brain processes.

Historically rooted in critiques of Cartesian dualism and inspired by phenomenology (notably Merleau-Ponty), ecological psychology (Gibson), and connectionism, embodied cognition has garnered broad interdisciplinary support from philosophy, cognitive science, neuroscience, psychology, AI, and robotics. Empirical evidence from neuroimaging, behavioral studies, and robotics underscores the interdependence of sensory, motor, and cognitive systems. Applications span education (embodied learning), AI and robotics (situated and embodied AI), clinical therapies (body-influenced cognitive interventions), sports, music cognition, social psychology, and more. Despite widespread acceptance, some controversies remain regarding operational definitions, the binding problem, and replication challenges. Overall, embodied cognition shifts the paradigm from an internalist brain-centric view to a distributed, action-oriented, and contextually embedded understanding of the mind-body-world nexus.
