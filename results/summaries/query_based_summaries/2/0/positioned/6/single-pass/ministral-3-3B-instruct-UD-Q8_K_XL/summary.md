Embodied cognition posits that cognition is fundamentally shaped by an agent’s bodily experiences, interactions with the environment, and sensorimotor processes rather than being confined to abstract mental representations. This perspective challenges traditional Cartesian views by emphasizing that the body, including its sensory and motor capacities, plays a crucial role in shaping cognitive functions such as perception, memory, language, reasoning, and emotion. Proponents argue that cognitive processes are not isolated from physical interactions but are deeply embedded in the agent’s embodied experiences and the dynamic relationships between the body, mind, and world.

Key aspects of embodied cognition include:
- **Sensorimotor contingencies**: The quality of perception is influenced by how sensory information changes when an agent acts in the world, highlighting the dynamic interplay between perception and action.
- **Extended cognition**: Cognitive processes extend beyond the brain into the environment, where tools, artifacts, and social interactions contribute to mental functioning.
- **Situated cognition**: Cognitive activities are inherently tied to the context in which they occur, including cultural, social, and physical environments.
- **Enactive cognition**: Cognition emerges through active engagement with the environment, where organisms interact with their surroundings to acquire knowledge and adapt.

Research in embodied cognition has expanded across disciplines, including neuroscience, psychology, linguistics, and robotics, to explore how bodily experiences influence cognitive development, learning, and problem-solving. For example, studies show that language comprehension involves motor simulations, memory is shaped by bodily states, and emotional processing is grounded in bodily responses. This perspective has implications for education, robotics, and clinical settings, where understanding the role of embodiment can enhance learning, interaction, and therapeutic interventions.
