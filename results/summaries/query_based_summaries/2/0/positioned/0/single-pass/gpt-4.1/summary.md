## Embodied Cognition: Summary

**Embodied cognition** is a broad research program claiming that human cognition is deeply influenced by the body's physical form and sensorimotor experiences, beyond what occurs in the brain alone. It opposes traditional disembodied, Cartesian views, emphasizing that cognition emerges from the dynamic interactions among brain, body, and environment. Cognition is not simply building internal representations, but actively shaped by bodily capacities, cultural context, and goal-directed actions. Variants of the theory range from “weak” accounts—where bodily characteristics constrain cognition—to “strong” embodiment, in which the body plays a constitutive, causal role in cognitive processing.

Empirical evidence supports embodied cognition across many domains: perception depends on bodily and environmental interactions; language comprehension involves motor simulations and sensorimotor areas of the brain; memory is facilitated by congruence between body posture and recalled events; learning is enhanced by physical actions and gestures; reasoning and problem solving are affected by motor experience; emotion and motivation are rooted in bodily states; and social cognition arises through sensorimotor synchrony in interactions. Applications include education (embodied learning strategies), robotics (emphasizing the role of the body in AI), clinical settings (therapy practices), sports (action-perception coupling), and music (bodily engagement in music perception). While widely accepted and supported in fields like neuroscience and psychology, embodied cognition remains a research program with ongoing debates and challenges regarding its exact boundaries and explanatory power.
