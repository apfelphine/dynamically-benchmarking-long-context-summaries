## Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition posits that an agent's cognition is significantly shaped by the agent's body and its sensorimotor capacities, rather than being solely the product of abstract, disembodied mental representations. This thesis challenges the Cartesian model by reintroducing bodily experiences into cognitive accounts. The enactive approach defines "embodiment" as cognition depending on the experiences derived from having a body with sensorimotor capacities, which are themselves embedded within broader biological, psychological, and cultural contexts. A narrower view specifies that many cognitive features are deeply dependent on the physical characteristics of an agent's body, giving the beyond-the-brain body a causal role in cognitive processing.

The influence of embodiment is evident across various cognitive domains. In perception, it suggests perception is an active process influenced by an individual's intentions, bodily states, and environment, rather than a passive reception of sensory input. Language comprehension involves motor simulations, and memory is viewed as integrated patterns of actions limited by the body. Furthermore, research demonstrates that bodily states can modulate emotional processing and that sensorimotor contingencies—how sensory information changes with action—determine the quality of perception. Ultimately, this perspective views cognition as emerging from the dynamic relationship between an agent and the affordances of the environment.
