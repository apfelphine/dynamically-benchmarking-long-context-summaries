**Embodied cognition** is a theoretical framework asserting that cognition is deeply shaped by the body and its interactions with the environment, challenging the traditional Cartesian view of the mind as a disembodied, abstract entity. It emphasizes that cognitive processes—such as perception, memory, reasoning, and language—are not isolated in the brain but emerge from the dynamic, sensorimotor, and embodied interactions between an agent and their world. Key principles include the idea that cognition is situated, time-pressured, and inherently tied to action, with the body playing a causal role in shaping thought.

The theory encompasses multiple related concepts, such as **situated cognition** (cognition as dependent on context), **extended cognition** (cognitive processes extending beyond the brain and body into the environment), and **enactivism**, which posits that cognition arises through the mutual shaping of an agent and their environment via perception-action cycles. Embodied cognition is considered a broad research program rather than a unified theory, integrating insights from philosophy, cognitive science, neuroscience, robotics, and artificial intelligence.

Empirical evidence supports the role of the body in cognition. For instance, studies show that bodily states influence perception, memory, and decision-making. Perceiving the world involves active engagement with the environment, not passive reception of stimuli. Language comprehension activates motor systems, and abstract concepts are understood through bodily simulations. In memory, bodily experiences facilitate recall, and in social cognition, bodily movements influence perspective-taking and emotional responses.

Applications of embodied cognition span education (e.g., embodied learning methods), robotics (e.g., embodied AI that interacts with the physical world), clinical practice (e.g., sensorimotor retraining), and social psychology (e.g., nonverbal synchrony). Despite its influence, the field faces methodological challenges, including the replication crisis and debates over definitions and boundaries. Critics argue that the theory may overstate the body's role or misattribute cognitive processes to embodiment without sufficient evidence. Nonetheless, embodied cognition has significantly reshaped how cognition is studied and applied across disciplines.
