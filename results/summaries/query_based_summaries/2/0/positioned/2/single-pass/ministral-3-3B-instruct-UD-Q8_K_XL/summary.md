Embodied cognition posits that cognition is fundamentally shaped by an agent’s bodily experiences and interactions with the environment, rather than being confined to abstract mental representations. This perspective rejects the Cartesian model, which isolates cognition from physical processes, emphasizing instead that the body—including sensory, motor, and affective capacities—plays a crucial role in shaping cognitive functions such as perception, memory, language, reasoning, and social cognition. The theory is rooted in the idea that cognition is not merely a product of internal mental processes but is deeply intertwined with the agent’s physical and environmental context.

Researchers in embodied cognition highlight that cognitive processes are situated, dynamic, and influenced by the body’s interactions with the world. For instance, perception is not passive but an active process where an agent’s intentions, memories, and bodily states interact with the environment to create coherent experiences. Similarly, language comprehension involves motor simulations, and memory is linked to bodily states and actions. This approach challenges traditional views of cognition as isolated within the brain, advocating instead for a holistic understanding where the body and its sensory-motor experiences are central to cognitive functioning. The theory has broad implications across cognitive science, neuroscience, robotics, and education, influencing how we conceptualize intelligence, learning, and even artificial intelligence.
