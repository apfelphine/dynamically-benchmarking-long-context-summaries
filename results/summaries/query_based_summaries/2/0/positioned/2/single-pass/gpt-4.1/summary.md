## Summary: Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition is a broad, interdisciplinary research program that emphasizes the integral role of the body in shaping cognitive processes. Rather than viewing cognition as merely abstract representation in the brain, the embodied cognition thesis asserts that mental functions such as perception, memory, reasoning, language, learning, and emotion are deeply influenced by an agent’s physical body, sensorimotor systems, and interactions with the environment. This perspective stands opposed to traditional Cartesian models, instead positing that cognition emerges from the dynamic interplay between brain, body, and physical/social context. Key aspects include the influence of sensorimotor contingencies, the grounding of abstract concepts in bodily experiences, and the way language, emotion, memory, and self-regulation are intertwined with bodily states.

Originating from philosophical traditions (especially phenomenology) and evolutionary theory, embodied cognition draws on evidence from neuroscience, psychology, linguistics, AI, and robotics. Applications span education (kinesthetic and gesture-based learning), AI–robotics (designing intelligent behavior through embodied interactions), clinical settings (somatic therapies), sports (motor skill development and judgment), music (embodied responses in cognition), and social psychology (bodily influence on social interaction and perspective-taking). Experimental and neuroimaging studies support the theory, demonstrating that cognition and neural activity are closely coupled with sensorimotor and bodily factors. While widely studied, debates and challenges remain regarding methodology, priming effects, the role of embodiment in early development, and the limits of the theory.
