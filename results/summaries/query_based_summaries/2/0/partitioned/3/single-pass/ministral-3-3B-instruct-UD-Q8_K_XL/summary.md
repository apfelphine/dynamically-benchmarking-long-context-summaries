Embodied cognition posits that cognition is fundamentally shaped by an agent’s bodily experiences and interactions with the environment. Central to this theory are **sensorimotor contingencies (SMCs)**, which describe how sensory information is governed by specific rules tied to an agent’s actions. For instance, visual SMCs involve the expansion of retinal flow when the body moves forward, while auditory SMCs are influenced by head rotations altering temporal asynchrony between ears. These contingencies highlight the body’s active role in shaping perception, memory, and reasoning, challenging the idea that cognition is purely abstract or disembodied.

Research in embodied cognition emphasizes that cognitive processes—such as language acquisition, problem-solving, and emotional processing—are grounded in sensorimotor experiences. Studies show that gestures, bodily movements, and even the physical act of writing enhance comprehension, while motor expertise (e.g., wrestlers’ mental rotation) influences spatial reasoning. Neuroscientific evidence further supports this by demonstrating that sensorimotor areas of the brain are activated during language comprehension and abstract thought. The theory also extends to memory, where bodily states (e.g., posture) and actions (e.g., reaching) influence recall, and learning, where perception-action loops facilitate skill acquisition. By integrating the body into cognitive processes, embodied cognition offers a dynamic framework for understanding how humans interact with and navigate their environments.
