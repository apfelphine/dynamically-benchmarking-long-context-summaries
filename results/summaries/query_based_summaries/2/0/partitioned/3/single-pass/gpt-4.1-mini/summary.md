# Summary of Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition posits that cognitive processes are deeply influenced by the body and its interactions with the environment, challenging traditional disembodied views of the mind. It emphasizes that cognition depends on sensorimotor capacities embedded within biological, psychological, and cultural contexts. This perspective highlights the significance of bodily experiences in shaping perception, attention, memory, reasoning, language, emotion, learning, and social cognition. The theory integrates findings from philosophy (notably phenomenology), cognitive science, neuroscience, robotics, and psychology, arguing that cognition emerges through dynamic interactions between brain, body, and environment rather than residing solely in the brain.

Research areas within embodied cognition include sensorimotor contingencies (how sensory information changes with action), the role of gestures in language acquisition and problem solving, and the neural basis of embodied experiences such as activation of motor regions during language comprehension or emotional re-experiencing. Evidence shows that bodily states—like posture or muscle activity—can influence cognition, learning, memory recall, reasoning strategies, and social interaction. Embodied cognition has practical applications in education (embodied learning methods), robotics (robots with sensory-motor integration), clinical therapies (sensorimotor retraining), sport psychology, music cognition, and social psychology (how bodily states affect social judgment and empathy). Overall, it reframes cognition as an active, bodily grounded process embedded in specific contexts and social interactions.
