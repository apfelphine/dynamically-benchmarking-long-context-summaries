**Embodied cognition** is a research program emphasizing that cognitive processes are not merely internal computations but are deeply rooted in the body's sensorimotor capacities and its dynamic interactions with the environment. The theory opposes the Cartesian "disembodied" view, proposing instead that cognition arises from the coupling of sensory inputs and motor actions, often described through concepts like sensorimotor contingencies, where perception is determined by how sensory information changes during action. This perspective has significant applications across multiple fields. In education, it supports embodied learning methods such as kinesthetic activities, gesture-based instruction, and the use of manipulatives to enhance conceptual understanding and retention. In artificial intelligence and robotics, it drives the shift from symbolic computing to "situated" or "embodied" AI, where systems like Shakey's successor models and modern drones utilize parallel activity producers and sensorimotor learning to function effectively in unpredictable, dynamic environments. In clinical settings, embodied principles inform therapies for autism, phantom limb pain, and mental health issues through somatic retraining and mindfulness. Additionally, the theory influences social psychology by demonstrating how bodily states, such as posture or temperature, influence social judgments and empathy, and impacts music cognition by highlighting the role of rhythmic entrainment and physical movement in musical meaning and emotional response.

Despite its broad acceptance as a framework for cognitive science, embodied cognition faces ongoing controversies and methodological challenges. Critics argue that the theory may not adequately explain early cognitive development or the "binding problem" of integrating disparate sensory features into a unified experience, suggesting that a complementary view is necessary. Furthermore, the field has experienced a replication crisis, with several studies claiming to demonstrate embodied effects (such as power posing or weight-induced moral judgments) failing to reproduce their original findings. These failures have led to debates regarding whether results are due to true embodied mechanisms or confounding factors like priming and facial muscle engagement. Nevertheless, the theory remains influential, particularly in linguistics where it posits that abstract concepts are grounded in bodily experiences, and in neuroscience where neuroimaging evidence continues to show that understanding language and reasoning activates motor and sensory regions of the brain.
