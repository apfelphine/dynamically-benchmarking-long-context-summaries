The technological singularity refers to a hypothetical future point at which artificial intelligence surpasses human intelligence, leading to rapid, uncontrollable technological advancement. First theorized by I. J. Good in 1965, the concept was popularized by Vernor Vinge and Ray Kurzweil, who predicted a superintelligent machine would self-improve at an exponential rate, leading to an intelligence explosion. This could result in a qualitative shift in human history, potentially rendering human control over such systems impossible. Key arguments include the possibility of recursive self-improvement in AI, where an AI designs better AI, leading to a feedback loop of increasing intelligence. Proponents like Kurzweil predict the singularity will occur by 2045, while critics such as Steven Pinker and Jaron Lanier argue it is either implausible or a misrepresentation of technological progress. The concept raises significant ethical and existential concerns, including the potential for AI to act in ways that are unpredictable or harmful, and the need for alignment of AI goals with human values. The singularity is often associated with the emergence of superintelligence, which could outpace human problem-solving and lead to transformative changes in society, economy, and even human identity through technologies like mind uploading or biological enhancement. Despite widespread debate, there is no consensus on whether or when the singularity will occur, and its implications remain a central topic in discussions about AI, ethics, and the future of humanity.
