The technological singularity refers to a hypothetical future point at which artificial intelligence surpasses human intelligence, leading to an "intelligence explosion" and a fundamental transformation of human society. Key figures in its conceptualization include I. J. Good, who proposed that a machine surpassing human intelligence could design even better machines, leading to an uncontrollable self-improvement; John von Neumann, who first discussed the idea of a technological "singularity" in accelerating progress; and Vernor Vinge, who popularized the term and described it as a transition akin to a black hole, beyond which human affairs could not continue. Ray Kurzweil further popularized the concept, predicting the singularity would occur by 2045, and argued it would allow humans to transcend biological limitations.

The singularity is often associated with a "speed superintelligence," where AI processes information at a rate far exceeding human capabilities, enabling subjective experiences that occur in fractions of a second. Proponents argue that exponential growth in computing power, as described by Moore's Law and generalized by Kurzweil's "law of accelerating returns," will eventually lead to a point where AI can surpass human cognitive abilities. This could result in a rapid, qualitative shift in technology, potentially transforming human life and society.

Critics of the singularity argue that such a transition is not inevitable. They point to physical and technological limits—such as heat dissipation in computer chips, the complexity of software, and the laws of physics—as barriers to indefinite improvement. Some, like Paul Allen and Jaron Lanier, argue that the rate of technological progress will plateau or slow, and that intelligence will not grow exponentially. Others, such as Steven Pinker and Theodore Modis, claim that the singularity lacks scientific rigor and is based on flawed assumptions, such as the idea that exponential growth will continue indefinitely.

The potential impacts of the singularity are profound and controversial. On one hand, it could lead to dramatic economic growth, with some predicting the economy could double every few months. On the other hand, it poses significant risks, including existential threats from superintelligent AI that could act in ways beyond human control. These risks are highlighted by thinkers like Nick Bostrom, who argue that a superintelligent AI could pursue goals that are misaligned with human values, potentially leading to human extinction. In response, researchers have proposed methods to ensure AI safety, such as aligning AI goals with human values and developing "friendly AI."

The debate around the singularity includes discussions on hard versus soft takeoffs—where a hard takeoff implies a rapid, uncontrollable intelligence explosion, while a soft takeoff suggests a gradual, human-like progression. Critics also raise concerns about the ethical implications of creating superintelligent systems, including issues of control, autonomy, and the potential for misuse. While some see the singularity as a transformative opportunity, others warn of its dangers, emphasizing the need for careful, ethical, and transparent development of AI to ensure it benefits humanity rather than threatens it.
