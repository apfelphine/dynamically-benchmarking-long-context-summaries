The concept of the **technological singularity** refers to a hypothetical future point at which artificial intelligence undergoes an "intelligence explosion," leading to superintelligence that vastly surpasses human capabilities and triggers rapid, uncontrollable technological and social change. Pioneered by figures such as I. J. Good (1965), Vernor Vinge (1983), and Ray Kurzweil (2005), the theory posits that a machine capable of designing better machines would recursively self-improve, accelerating at an incomprehensible rate. This process, often termed a "hard takeoff," could result in the end of the human era, where biological minds become obsolete or merged with machines, potentially solving existential problems or posing catastrophic risks to humanity.

Predictions regarding the timing of the singularity vary widely, ranging from the late 20th century to beyond 2100, with many experts anticipating the emergence of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) between 2040 and 2050. Proponents argue that exponential growth in computing power and algorithmic efficiency will inevitably lead to a singularity, while critics contend that human creativity is diminishing, hardware limits (such as heat and Moore's Law) will halt progress, or that the event is scientifically unfounded. The topic remains highly contentious, with significant debate surrounding the feasibility of superintelligence, the alignment of AI goals with human values, and the potential existential dangers or benefits of such a transition.
