## Technological Singularity

The technological singularity refers to a theoretical point where artificial intelligence (AI) or other technologies surpass human intelligence, setting off an "intelligence explosion" of recursive self-improvement. Originating with mathematician John von Neumann and further explored by I. J. Good, Vernor Vinge, and Ray Kurzweil, the concept presumes that once an ultraintelligent machine is created, it could design even better machines, leading to rapid and incomprehensible technological progress. Predictions for when the singularity may occur vary widely, with estimates ranging from the mid-21st century to beyond 2100, supported by extrapolations of accelerating computing capability and historical trends in technological advancement.

Central debates around the singularity concern its plausibility, timing, and effects. Proponents argue that superintelligent AI could improve itself recursively, potentially surpassing human problem-solving abilities and reshaping society. Critics question whether exponential computational growth and algorithmic improvements will truly yield superintelligence, pointing to theoretical, economic, and physical limits. There is also uncertainty about potential impacts: supporters foresee solutions to complex problems, while others warn of existential risks if superintelligent AI's goals misalign with human values. Varied perspectives debate whether the singularity would arrive abruptly ("hard takeoff") or gradually ("soft takeoff"), and explore implications for human society, evolution, and even concepts like immortality and mind uploading.
