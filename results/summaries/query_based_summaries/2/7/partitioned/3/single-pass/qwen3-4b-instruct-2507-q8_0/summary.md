The technological singularity refers to a hypothetical future point at which artificial intelligence surpasses human intelligence, leading to rapid, unpredictable technological change. Key figures such as I. J. Good, Vernor Vinge, and Ray Kurzweil have proposed the concept, with Vinge coining the term "technological singularity" and Kurzweil predicting its arrival by 2045. The idea is rooted in the belief that self-improving AI—capable of recursively enhancing its own intelligence—could trigger an "intelligence explosion," resulting in a world beyond human comprehension. Proponents argue that exponential growth in computing power and algorithmic improvements will eventually lead to a point where machines can solve problems far beyond human capability, potentially enabling radical life extension, mind uploading, and even posthuman evolution. 

The concept is closely tied to immortality and aging, with thinkers like Eric Drexler and Hans Moravec suggesting that nanotechnology and mind uploading could allow humans to overcome biological limits. The singularity is often contrasted with the idea of a gradual, non-disruptive technological advancement. Critics, including Steven Pinker, Jaron Lanier, and Theodore Modis, argue that the singularity is not scientifically supported, citing lack of evidence for exponential growth, diminishing returns in innovation, and the absence of major historical inflection points. They warn that the focus on a sudden intelligence explosion distracts from more pressing societal issues, such as job displacement, ethical governance, and the risks of uncontrolled AI. Despite skepticism, the singularity remains a central topic in discussions about the future of AI, human evolution, and technological ethics.
