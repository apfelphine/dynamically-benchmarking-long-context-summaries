The concept of the **technological singularity** refers to a hypothetical future point where technological growth becomes uncontrollable and irreversible, resulting in unforeseeable changes to human civilization. This phenomenon is most commonly associated with the emergence of artificial intelligence that surpasses human intelligence, triggering a recursive self-improvement cycle known as an "intelligence explosion." Pioneers such as I. J. Good (1965), Stanisław Ulam, Hans Moravec (1977), and Vernor Vinge (1983, 1988) theorized that once machines achieve superhuman capabilities, they would evolve at an incomprehensible rate, potentially leading to a "hard takeoff" where human affairs cease to be recognizable. Ray Kurzweil popularized the term in 2005, predicting a singularity around 2045 based on the "law of accelerating returns," where exponential growth in computing power, nanotechnology, and biotechnology allows for mind uploading and the transcendence of biological limitations.

Despite this optimistic trajectory, the plausibility and timing of the singularity remain heavily debated. Critics argue that exponential growth trends do not hold indefinitely, citing the end of Moore's Law, the "complexity brake" where increasing system complexity hinders further innovation, and the distinction between quantitative speed improvements and qualitative algorithmic leaps. Significant risks are associated with the event, including existential threats from unfriendly superintelligent AIs, loss of human control, and socioeconomic upheaval, prompting calls for AI alignment, regulation, and ethical frameworks. Conversely, proponents view the singularity as an inevitable or desirable evolution of humanity, offering solutions to disease, poverty, and aging, though concerns regarding inequality, the "genetic divide," and the potential for technological determinism persist.
