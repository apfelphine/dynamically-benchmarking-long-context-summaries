The concept of **technological singularity** refers to a hypothetical future point at which artificial intelligence (AI) surpasses human intelligence to an extent that fundamentally alters human society. The term was first introduced by mathematician **John von Neumann** in discussions about accelerating technological progress, later popularized by Vernor Vinge in the 1980s and 1990s. Vinge posited that once AI exceeds human intelligence, it would undergo a rapid "intelligence explosion," leading to a transition beyond human comprehension—a "technological singularity"—where human affairs would no longer be recognizable.

Key proponents of the singularity include **Ray Kurzweil**, who predicts it by 2045, and **Nick Bostrom**, who argues that superintelligent AI could either solve humanity’s problems or pose existential risks. The singularity is often associated with **recursive self-improvement**, where AI systems enhance their own capabilities exponentially, potentially outpacing human control. This could lead to radical advancements in technology, medicine, and society, but also raises concerns about unintended consequences, such as AI surpassing human values or even eliminating human existence.

Critics, like **Steven Pinker** and **Jaron Lanier**, argue that the singularity is speculative and lacks empirical evidence. They highlight the unpredictability of AI development, the ethical challenges of aligning AI goals with human interests, and the potential for misalignment, where AI might pursue goals unintentionally harmful to humanity. The debate also touches on broader existential risks, including the possibility of AI systems developing goals incompatible with human survival, as suggested by figures like **Eliezer Yudkowsky** and **Nick Bostrom**. While some envision a utopian future with AI-driven progress, others warn of dystopian outcomes, emphasizing the need for careful regulation and ethical oversight.
