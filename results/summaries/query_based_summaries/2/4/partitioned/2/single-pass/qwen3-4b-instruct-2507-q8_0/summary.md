The Chinese Room thought experiment, proposed by John Searle, challenges the idea that a computer running a program can have a mind or understanding. Searle argues that even if a machine simulates human behavior—such as passing the Turing test—it does not truly understand the meaning of the symbols it manipulates. He illustrates this by imagining himself in a room following a set of rules to process Chinese characters, which he does without understanding the language. Since he, as a human, lacks understanding, Searle concludes the machine, which operates the same way, also lacks true understanding or intentionality. This critique targets the "strong AI" hypothesis, which claims that a properly programmed computer would have a mind like humans. Searle opposes this, asserting that consciousness and understanding require specific biological processes found in brains, not just symbolic manipulation. He argues that the mind is not merely a product of information processing, but is dependent on the actual physical and chemical properties of biological brains. The argument is central to debates about the nature of mind, consciousness, and the limits of artificial intelligence, and it remains a foundational concept in philosophy of mind.
