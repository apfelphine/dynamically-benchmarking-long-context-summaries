# Chinese Room

The "Chinese Room" is a thought experiment proposed by philosopher John Searle in 1980 to challenge the "Strong AI" hypothesis, which claims that a suitably programmed computer would possess a mind and understanding. Searle imagines a person who does not speak Chinese confined in a room with a rulebook for manipulating Chinese symbols. When Chinese characters are input, the person follows the instructions to produce correct Chinese outputs, making it impossible for an outside observer to distinguish the room from a Chinese speaker (passing the Turing test). However, Searle argues that the person inside understands neither the input nor the output; they are merely manipulating symbols based on syntax without any grasp of their semantics or meaning. Consequently, Searle concludes that syntax alone is insufficient to generate semantics, and therefore, a computer running a program simulates understanding but does not actually possess a mind or consciousness.

This argument directly opposes the computational theory of mind, which holds that mental states are defined by their functional roles and information processing. Searle contends that while computers can effectively model the functional relationships of the mind, they lack the specific biological machinery found in brains that he believes is necessary for genuine understanding. Critics of Searle's argument have offered various rebuttals, including the "systems reply" (arguing that the man plus the paper and rules form a system that understands), the "virtual mind" reply (suggesting the mind is a virtual construct within the system), and modifications involving robots or brain simulations. Searle maintains that regardless of the system's complexity, speed, or connection to the real world, the internal manipulation of symbols remains syntactic and devoid of the semantic grounding required for true mental states.
