The Chinese room thought experiment, primarily developed by John Searle, is a philosophical challenge to the strong AI hypothesis and the computational theory of mind. It posits a scenario where a person, who only understands English, follows a program (symbol manipulation) to process Chinese characters as input and produces Chinese characters as output, making the room's behavior indistinguishable from that of a Chinese speaker (passing a Turing test). Searle argues that because the person inside does not understand the symbols, the system, despite perfectly simulating understanding, lacks genuine semantics or "intentionality" and therefore does not possess a mind in the human sense.

Critics of the Chinese room raise several counterarguments. Some argue that the understanding resides in the "whole system" (the system reply), not just the person. Others focus on the limits of Searle's intuition, pointing to issues of speed and complexity, or question the premise that subjective consciousness can be determined externally (other minds reply). Searle counters these by asserting his view of "biological naturalism," maintaining that genuine mental phenomena depend on the specific physical-chemical properties of actual human brains, not merely on running a formal program.
