The Chinese room thought experiment, developed by John Searle, posits a scenario where a person in a room, using an instruction manual (program) and symbols (Chinese characters) without understanding their meaning, can correctly manipulate the symbols to produce Chinese output. Searle argues that this demonstrates that symbol manipulation, which is syntactic, is insufficient to generate semantics (meaning); therefore, the computer, despite passing a Turing test, does not possess genuine understanding or a "mind" in the human sense. Searle concludes that the strong AI hypothesis—that a correctly programmed computer has a mind identical to a human's—is false.

Critics of the Chinese room have proposed several replies to challenge Searle's conclusion. These include the "systems reply," which argues that the entire system (including the person, program, and external components) understands Chinese; the "robot reply," which suggests connecting the program to a robot to create a causal link to the physical world; and the "brain simulator reply," which suggests that a detailed simulation of a human brain would account for understanding. Searle counters these replies by maintaining that understanding requires the specific causal and intentional states found in actual human brains, not just the formal program or simulation.
