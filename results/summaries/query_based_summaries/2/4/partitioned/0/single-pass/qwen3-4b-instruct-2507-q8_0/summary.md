The Chinese Room thought experiment, proposed by John Searle, challenges the idea that a computer running a program can have a mind or understanding. The experiment imagines a person in a room who follows a set of rules to manipulate Chinese symbols, producing responses that appear to be meaningful to a Chinese speaker. Searle argues that the person, despite the room's behavior resembling understanding, does not comprehend the Chinese symbols—only follows syntactic rules. He concludes that syntax alone is insufficient for semantics (meaning), and thus a computer, which operates purely on syntax, cannot have intentionality or consciousness. This refutes the "strong AI" hypothesis, which claims that a properly programmed computer with the right inputs and outputs would have a mind in the same sense as humans. Searle maintains that consciousness and understanding require specific biological processes found in human brains, a position he calls "biological naturalism." While critics offer replies—such as the system reply, which claims the entire system (including the program and environment) understands Chinese, or the robot reply, which argues that physical interaction with the world grounds meaning—Searle contends these fail to prove that understanding arises from computation. The argument remains a central point in the philosophy of mind, challenging functionalism and the computational theory of mind.
