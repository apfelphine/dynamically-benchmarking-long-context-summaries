The **Chinese room** is a thought experiment proposed by philosopher John Searle in 1980 to challenge the claim that a computer running the right program would possess a mind or understanding (Strong AI). In the scenario, a person who does not speak Chinese is locked in a room with a rulebook in English. When Chinese characters are slipped under the door, the person follows the rules to manipulate symbols and produce appropriate Chinese responses, making the interaction indistinguishable from a native speaker. Searle argues that the person in the room understands nothing; they are merely manipulating syntax without grasping semantics. Therefore, he concludes that the computer performing the same operations also lacks understanding and intentionality, proving that mere symbol manipulation is insufficient for true thinking.

The argument targets the **computational theory of mind** and **functionalism**, asserting that mental states require specific biological causal powers ("brains cause minds") rather than just functional organization. Searle distinguishes between "syntax" (symbol manipulation) and "semantics" (meaning), positing that syntax alone cannot generate semantics. While critics propose "system replies" (arguing the whole system understands) or "robot replies" (connecting the machine to the physical world to ground meaning), Searle maintains that without biological substrates, simulation is not realization. Consequently, the Chinese room demonstrates that a machine can pass the Turing test without having a mind, consciousness, or genuine understanding, effectively refuting the idea that intelligence is purely a matter of information processing.
