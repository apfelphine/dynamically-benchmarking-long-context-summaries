The Chinese room thought experiment, proposed by John Searle in his 1980 paper "Minds, Brains, and Programs," challenges the sufficiency of the Turing test to determine if a machine truly thinks. The experiment posits a person (Searle) in a room who follows a program to manipulate Chinese characters received as input and produces Chinese characters as output, perfectly mimicking a native speaker. Searle argues that even though the system's behavior appears intelligent (passing the Turing test), the person inside the room, who only follows the rules step-by-step, does not understand the conversation.

Searle concludes that without genuine "understanding," or "intentionality"—the property of thoughts being "about" something—the machine cannot be described as "thinking" in the human sense. This argument is presented as a direct refutation of the strong AI hypothesis, which claims that a properly programmed computer with the right inputs and outputs would have a mind equivalent to a human's. The experiment highlights the distinction between syntactic manipulation of symbols and semantic understanding.
