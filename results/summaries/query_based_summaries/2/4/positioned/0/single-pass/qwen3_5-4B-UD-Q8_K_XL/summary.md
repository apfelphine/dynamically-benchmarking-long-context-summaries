The "Chinese Room" is a thought experiment proposed by philosopher John Searle to argue that a computer running a program cannot possess a mind or understanding, even if it behaves indistinguishably from one. Searle imagines a person following a set of rules in a room to manipulate Chinese symbols without understanding the language; similarly, a computer manipulates symbols syntactically without grasping their semantics. Searle concludes that this demonstrates the failure of the "strong AI" hypothesis, which posits that appropriately programmed computers would have minds identical to human ones. He argues that syntax alone is insufficient for semantics and that consciousness requires specific biological machinery, a view known as biological naturalism.

The Chinese Room argument challenges functionalism and the computational theory of mind, asserting that external behavior cannot prove internal understanding. It has become one of the most influential and debated arguments in the philosophy of mind, generating extensive literature attempting to refute it. Critics offer various responses, such as the "system reply" (claiming the whole system understands, not just the individual), the "robot reply" (arguing connection to the physical world grounds meaning), and claims that Searle's intuition relies on the limitations of his specific scenario. While Searle maintains that no digital machine can truly think, proponents of artificial intelligence and computationalism often argue that the argument misunderstands how meaning arises through functional structure or that the distinction between simulation and reality is less rigid than Searle suggests.
