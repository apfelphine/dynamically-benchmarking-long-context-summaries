The "Chinese room" is a thought experiment proposed by philosopher John Searle in his 1980 paper *Minds, Brains, and Programs* to argue that the Turing test is insufficient for determining whether a machine possesses a mind or understands language. Searle posits that a person who does not speak Chinese, but follows a rulebook to manipulate Chinese symbols, can simulate understanding Chinese perfectly to an external observer without actually comprehending the meaning of the symbols. This demonstrates that a system can exhibit the *behavior* of thinking or understanding (syntax) without possessing *semantics* or intentionality. Consequently, Searle concludes that strong AI—the hypothesis that an appropriately programmed computer would have a mind in the same sense humans do—is false, as running a program merely simulates mental states rather than creating them.

The argument challenges computationalism and functionalism, which suggest that mental phenomena are defined by their functional roles and can be replicated by any physical system running the correct software. Searle counters that syntax alone is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics, asserting that biological naturalism is required for consciousness, where specific causal powers inherent to biological brains produce mental phenomena. While critics offer various replies, such as the "system reply" (arguing the whole system understands) or the "robot reply" (arguing physical interaction grants meaning), Searle maintains that without the specific machinery of the brain, no artifact can truly think or be conscious, regardless of its ability to pass behavioral tests like the Turing test.
