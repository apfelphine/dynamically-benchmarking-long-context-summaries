The Chinese Room thought experiment, proposed by John Searle, challenges the idea that a computer can truly understand or have a mind, even if it can simulate human-like behavior. Searle argues that a machine following a set of rules to produce human-like responses—such as in a language game—does not possess understanding or intentionality. He claims that without "understanding" (or "intentionality"), a machine cannot be said to "think" or have a mind in the same way humans do. The experiment involves a person in a room who follows a program to respond to Chinese characters, but does not understand the language. Searle contends that the person, and by extension the machine, lacks semantic understanding, despite producing behavior that appears intelligent. This argument is a central critique of strong AI, which holds that a computer running the right program could have a mind. Searle's argument has sparked extensive debate, with critics offering various responses such as the "system reply" (the mind is in the system, not the individual), the "robot reply" (a robot body could provide meaning), and the "virtual mind" reply (a virtual mind could exist in a simulation). Despite these rebuttals, the Chinese Room remains a foundational argument in philosophy of mind, highlighting the distinction between syntactic manipulation and semantic understanding.
