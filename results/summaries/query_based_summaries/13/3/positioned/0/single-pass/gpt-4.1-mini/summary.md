## Summary: University of Edinburgh

The University of Edinburgh, founded in 1582 as Tounis College through a royal charter granted by King James VI, was the first university in Scotland established by secular authorities. Its origins trace back to a bequest by Bishop Robert Reid in 1557, though the founding was delayed by the Reformation. Initially focusing on liberal arts and divinity, the university later expanded to include faculties of law, medicine, and arts. It played a central role in the Scottish Enlightenment, attracting prominent scholars like Adam Smith, David Hume, and Joseph Black, and became renowned for its medical school, anatomy, and surgery. The university has evolved through significant expansions, mergers, and restructurings, including admitting women from the late 19th century, merging Edinburgh College of Art in 2011, and establishing satellite campuses like King's Buildings and BioQuarter.

Today, the University of Edinburgh operates across five main campuses in Edinburgh, housing varied academic disciplines from arts and humanities to medicine and veterinary studies. It is governed by the University Court, General Council, and Senatus Academicus, with a structure organized into three colleges: Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences; Science and Engineering; and Medicine and Veterinary Medicine. The university is a leading research institution, a member of the Russell Group, and internationally ranked for its academic programs. It has a rich student life with a historic students' association, diverse clubs, and traditions, and is known for influential alumni in science, literature, and politics, including multiple Nobel laureates.
