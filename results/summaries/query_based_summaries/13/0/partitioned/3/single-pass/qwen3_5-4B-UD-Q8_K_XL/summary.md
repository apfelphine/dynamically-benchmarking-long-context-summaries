The University of Cambridge is a historic collegiate research university in the United Kingdom, founded in 1209, and is widely regarded as one of Britain's most prestigious institutions. Its academic structure consists of 31 self-governing colleges and numerous faculties, schools, and departments, with a strong emphasis on research, particularly in mathematics, natural sciences, and medicine. The university has produced a vast array of notable alumni and academics, including 70 Nobel Prize winners, prominent figures in mathematics such as Isaac Newton and Alan Turing, and leaders in politics, including 14 British Prime Ministers and several monarchs. The university's unique teaching system features small-group sessions known as "supervisions," which are credited with fostering exceptional academic development.

The university operates within the city of Cambridge, where students traditionally live within three miles of the River Cam during term time. It has faced significant challenges regarding women's education, finally admitting women to full degree programs in 1948, though female-only colleges like Girton and Newnham still exist. In recent years, the institution has faced scrutiny over issues including sexual harassment complaints and its historical ties to certain political figures and organizations. Despite these challenges, Cambridge remains a central hub for innovation, often cited as the birthplace of the computer, and is a key component of the "Golden Triangle" of British research universities alongside Oxford and Imperial College London.
