## University of Cambridge Summary

The University of Cambridge, founded in 1209, originated from a scholarly community in Cambridge and scholars fleeing Oxford conflicts. It consists of 31 self-governing colleges, with the oldest being Peterhouse (1284) and the newest full college status granted to Homerton College in 2010. The university transitioned from religious teachings to a broader curriculum after the dissolution of monasteries, with a strong history in mathematics and sciences, highlighted by its rigorous Mathematical Tripos and notable mathematicians like Isaac Newton, G. H. Hardy, and Srinivasa Ramanujan.

Cambridge is characterized by its collegiate system combining small-group teaching (supervisions) with university-wide lectures, and has a distributed academic year of three terms. The university holds extensive libraries and museums such as the Fitzwilliam Museum and prominent collections at individual colleges. It is globally recognized as a prestigious research institution, ranked consistently in the world’s top universities, and part of the UK’s elite group alongside Oxford. Its alumni include numerous Nobel laureates, Fields Medalists, Turing Award winners, British Prime Ministers, foreign heads of state, monarchs, and significant contributors to fields ranging from science and literature to politics and technology. The university fosters a vibrant student life with formal halls, May balls, numerous societies, and traditions like the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols by the King’s College Choir, broadcast worldwide annually.
