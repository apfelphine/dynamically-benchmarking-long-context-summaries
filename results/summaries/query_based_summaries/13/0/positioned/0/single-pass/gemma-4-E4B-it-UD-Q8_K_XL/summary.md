The University of Cambridge was founded in 1209, spurred by scholars leaving Oxford amidst local conflicts. By 1225, a chancellor was appointed, and in 1231, writs from King Henry III established rules regarding rents, while Pope Gregory IX granted the university *ius non trahi extra* (the right not to be drawn out) for three years. It was described as a *studium generale* by Pope Nicholas IV in 1290 and confirmed as such in 1318, attracting researchers from other European medieval universities.

The university structure includes 31 colleges, none of which is as old as the university itself. The focus of colleges shifted from canon law to classics, the Bible, and mathematics following the dissolution of the monasteries in 1536. Cambridge quickly became a global leader in mathematics, evidenced by the competitive Mathematical Tripos examination. The Cambridge University Act 1856 formalized its structure and introduced subjects like history and modern languages. Furthermore, the university has a strong history in producing notable alumni in fields ranging from science (like Charles Darwin and Isaac Newton) to politics and literature.
