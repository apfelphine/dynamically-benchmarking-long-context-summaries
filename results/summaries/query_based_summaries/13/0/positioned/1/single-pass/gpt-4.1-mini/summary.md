The University of Cambridge, founded in 1209 by scholars migrating from Oxford, developed a collegiate system with its first college, Peterhouse, established in 1284. The university quickly gained a global reputation, particularly in mathematics and mathematical physics via its rigorous Mathematical Tripos examination. Expansion in the 19th and 20th centuries broadened its curriculum and reformed its governance, integrating modern subjects and formalising academic structures. Despite initially being male-only, Cambridge admitted women to degrees in 1948, with several women’s colleges still operating. The university is known for its historic buildings in Cambridge, extensive libraries, museums such as the Fitzwilliam, and a strong emphasis on small-group teaching called supervisions.

Cambridge is a collegiate university with 31 self-governing colleges that provide housing, welfare, and social functions, while faculties conduct research and lectures. It admits highly competitive student cohorts, supported by a rigorous interview and pooling admission process. The university is renowned globally, ranking consistently in top positions for arts, sciences, and social sciences. Its notable alumni include many Nobel laureates, renowned scientists like Isaac Newton and Stephen Hawking, prominent political leaders, and influential writers. Annual traditions, a vibrant student union, and a rich cultural and sport life complement its academic excellence.
