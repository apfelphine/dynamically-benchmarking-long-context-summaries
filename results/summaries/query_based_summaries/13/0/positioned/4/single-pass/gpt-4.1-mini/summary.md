## University of Cambridge

The University of Cambridge, founded in 1209, originated from scholars fleeing conflicts in Oxford. It quickly gained recognition through royal writs and papal bulls, evolving into a major medieval studium generale attracting scholars across Europe. The 31 constituent colleges, starting with Peterhouse in 1284, began as endowed fellowships, gradually expanding to include women’s colleges and adapting curricula from canon law to classics, mathematics, and sciences, shaped by religious and social movements such as Puritanism. Cambridge became a global leader in mathematics and mathematical physics, notably through the Mathematical Tripos and figures like Isaac Newton, G. H. Hardy, and Srinivasa Ramanujan. The university underwent significant modernization in the 19th century, expanded its faculties, and contributed substantially during the World Wars.

Cambridge operates as a collegiate university with self-governing colleges responsible for undergraduate support and housing, alongside a broad central academic structure composed of faculties, schools, and departments. Its governance includes the Chancellor, Vice-Chancellor, Senate, Council, and Regent House. The university has a substantial endowment (£3.7 billion) supporting extensive research and student scholarships, including the prestigious Gates Cambridge Scholarships. Admission is highly competitive and involves rigorous selection procedures including interviews and over-subscription clauses. Cambridge maintains over 150 academic departments, world-class libraries, and museums, including the historic Fitzwilliam Museum. Its academic reputation is among the world’s highest, consistently ranked in the global top six by QS and other major rankings, excelling in subjects across the arts, sciences, and social sciences. Student life is rich with traditions such as Formal Halls, May Balls, numerous societies, and competitive rowing, exemplified by its iconic Boat Race against Oxford. Cambridge alumni include 70 Nobel laureates, Fields Medalists, Turing Award winners, numerous heads of state, leading scientists, philosophers, writers, and artists, reflecting its broad and profound impact on global academia, culture, and technology.
