## University of Cambridge

The University of Cambridge, founded in 1209, originated from scholars fleeing conflicts at Oxford. Its early reputation was tied to scholastic and ecclesiastical scholarship linked to Ely Cathedral. The university evolved with a cluster of colleges—initially fellowships of scholars—most notably starting with Peterhouse in 1284. Over centuries, Cambridge shifted from religious-based studies to classics, mathematics, and sciences, becoming a global leader in mathematics and mathematical physics, exemplified by the prestigious Mathematical Tripos exam producing notable scientists such as Isaac Newton and G. H. Hardy. The university expanded its curriculum and infrastructure through the 19th and 20th centuries, formalizing its structure via the Cambridge University Act 1856 and broadening academic disciplines. Women’s admission progressed from the late 19th century, with full degree conferral beginning in 1948 and the gradual inclusion of women in colleges over subsequent decades.

Cambridge’s collegiate system consists of 31 autonomous colleges responsible for student housing, welfare, and smaller-group teaching, while faculties oversee lectures and research. Its academic year is divided into three terms with intensive teaching via lectures and small-group supervisions. The university houses extensive facilities, including 116 libraries, specialized museums (e.g., Fitzwilliam Museum), and the renowned Cambridge University Press & Assessment. Highly selective admissions involve interviews and an over-subscription clause to manage quotas. The university is a member of prestigious academic groups like the Russell Group and maintains strong ties to the regional economy through Silicon Fen. Cambridge holds a worldwide reputation for excellence, consistently ranking among the top global universities, and has produced numerous distinguished alumni including Nobel laureates, Fields Medalists, Turing Award winners, prime ministers, and notable figures across sciences, humanities, and arts. Its traditions continue through formal dinners, May Balls, vibrant student societies, and a competitive sports culture highlighted by the historic Boat Race against Oxford.
