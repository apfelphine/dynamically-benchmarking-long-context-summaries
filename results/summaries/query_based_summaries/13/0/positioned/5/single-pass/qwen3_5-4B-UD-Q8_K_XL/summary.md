The University of Cambridge, founded in 1209, is a collegiate research university in Cambridge, England, renowned for its history of producing over 70 Nobel Prize laureates and significant contributions to mathematics, science, and literature. Its structure consists of 31 self-governing colleges that provide housing, welfare, and undergraduate teaching, alongside the university's central administration which manages research, degrees, and facilities. The institution is a member of the Russell Group and the G5, and it is considered one of Britain's most prestigious elite universities, often ranking in the top 10 globally in major league tables.

Cambridge's academic profile is defined by its highly competitive admissions process, which typically requires A-level grades of A*AA, and its unique pedagogical methods, particularly the "supervision" system where small groups of students meet weekly with a tutor. The university operates across several sites, including Addenbrooke's Hospital, Downing Site, and the West Cambridge Development, and is a major driver of the local "Cambridge Phenomenon," characterized by high-tech and biotech growth. While it maintains a strong tradition of male-only colleges for women, such as Girton and Newnham, it has admitted female students to its undergraduate degrees since 1948, achieving a nearly balanced gender ratio by the 2019–2020 academic year.
