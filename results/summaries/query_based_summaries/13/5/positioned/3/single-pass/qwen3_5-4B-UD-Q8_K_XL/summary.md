University College London (UCL) was founded on February 11, 1826, as the joint-stock company "London University," established as a secular alternative to the Anglican universities of Oxford and Cambridge. Although Jeremy Bentham is often regarded as its "spiritual father," he only purchased share No. 633; the institution's actual founders included James Mill and Henry Brougham. After a decade of attempting to secure royal recognition, the proprietors accepted the government's proposal to form the University of London as an examining body in 1836, incorporating UCL as the first affiliated college. Following its reconstitution in 1909, UCL surrendered its legal independence in 1907 to become a constituent "school" of the University of London, though it retained its informal name.

In 1976, a new charter restored UCL's legal independence, and in 1993, it gained the power to confer its own degrees, effectively becoming a de facto university. In 2005, UCL was granted full degree-awarding powers and adopted a corporate branding that uses the initialism "UCL" in all external communications. The university is organized into eleven faculties, including Arts and Humanities, Bartlett (Built Environment), Brain Sciences, Engineering Sciences, and Medical Sciences. Its main campus is located in Bloomsbury, Central London, with additional sites in UCL East, Stratford, and various hospital campuses. UCL is a founding member of the Russell Group and the League of European Research Universities, and it holds the distinction of being the first British university to offer a campus in Australia.
