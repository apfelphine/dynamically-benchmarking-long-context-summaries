## University of Chicago Summary

The University of Chicago, founded in 1890, is a major research university located on a 217-acre campus in Hyde Park and Woodlawn, Chicago. The university features Victorian and Collegiate Gothic architecture with notable modern additions like the Regenstein Library and Joe and Rika Mansueto Library. Its academic structure comprises an undergraduate college offering 51 majors and 33 minors across four divisions, alongside four graduate divisions and seven professional schools, such as the Booth School of Business and the Law School, both consistently ranked among the top professional schools nationally. The university is renowned for pioneering the liberal arts core curriculum, the Socratic method, and the "Great Books" program. Its rigorous quarter system encourages interdisciplinary scholarship, supported by over 140 research centers and close affiliations with major laboratories such as Argonne National Laboratory and Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory.

The University of Chicago has a highly selective admissions process, with undergraduate acceptance rates dropping to 4.7% in recent years. In fall 2024, enrollment included 7,569 undergraduates and 10,968 graduate students, with a diverse student body and need-blind admissions for domestic applicants. The university employs over 3,400 academic staff, has an endowment of $10.1 billion, and faces financial challenges, including a $288 million budget deficit in 2024. Notable traditions and student life include a distinctive house system, more than 400 student organizations, and events such as the annual Scavenger Hunt. The university is home to 101 Nobel laureates, prominent faculty, and alumni who have led in academia, government, and industry. Reflecting its global reach, the university has expanded overseas, opening centers in Paris, London, Hong Kong, Beijing, and Delhi. In leading university rankings, the University of Chicago consistently places among the world's top institutions.
