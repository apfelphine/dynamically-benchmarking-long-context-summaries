The University of Chicago, founded in 1890 by the American Baptist Education Society, is a prestigious research university located in Hyde Park, Chicago. Its history spans from the closure of the first University of Chicago in 1886 to its evolution into a major research institution under various presidents, including William Rainey Harper, Robert Maynard Hutchins, and Paul Alivisatos. The university is known for its rigorous core curriculum, Socratic teaching methods, and diverse academic divisions. It operates on a quarter system and has historically maintained a high standard of academic freedom and independence, despite facing financial challenges and budget deficits that have required significant fundraising and austerity measures over the decades.

The University of Chicago is organized into an undergraduate College and four graduate divisions (Biological Sciences, Physical Sciences, Social Sciences, and Humanities), alongside seven professional schools, including the Booth School of Business, Pritzker School of Medicine, and Law School, which consistently rank among the top in the United States. The campus features distinctive Gothic and modern architecture, notably the Main Quadrangles designed in the Collegiate Gothic style. The university is a major research hub, managing or co-managing facilities such as the Argonne National Laboratory and Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory (Fermilab), and it is a founding member of the Association of American Universities. With a substantial endowment and a strong alumni network, the university continues to be a leader in science, economics, and the humanities, while navigating contemporary issues such as campus safety, student activism, and financial sustainability.
