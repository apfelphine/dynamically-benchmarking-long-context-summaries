## University of Chicago Summary

The University of Chicago, established in 1890 and located in Hyde Park, Chicago, is a prestigious private research university with a significant global reputation. It operates on a quarter system and comprises an undergraduate college, four graduate research divisions, seven professional schools, and multiple research centers and institutes, including the Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory and Argonne National Laboratory. The main campus spans 217 acres and is architecturally notable for its mix of Collegiate Gothic and modernist buildings; important landmarks include the site of Chicago Pile-1 (the first controlled nuclear chain reaction), Robie House, and Rockefeller Chapel. The university also maintains satellite campuses and centers in global cities such as Paris, London, Hong Kong, and Beijing.

Academically, the University of Chicago is renowned for its rigorous core curriculum and highly selective admissions, with an undergraduate acceptance rate of 4.7% in 2023. It is well known for its economics department (home of the "Chicago School") and professional schools, particularly the Booth School of Business and the Law School, both consistently ranked among the top in the nation. The university offers undergraduate and graduate degrees across broad disciplines, supports over 400 student organizations, and has a distinctive residential house system. Chicago faculty and alumni have earned 101 Nobel Prizes—the most affiliated prizes of any university—including prominent figures in physics, economics, literature, and medicine. The university, governed by a board of trustees and an endowment of roughly $10.1 billion (2024), continues to exert major influence in academia and public life. Notable alumni and faculty include CEOs, heads of state, Nobel laureates, Supreme Court justices, and influential scholars.
