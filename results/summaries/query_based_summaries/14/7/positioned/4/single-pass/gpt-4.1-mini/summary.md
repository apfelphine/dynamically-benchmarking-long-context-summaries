The University of Chicago was reestablished in 1890 with financial backing from John D. Rockefeller and land donated by Marshall Field, building on the legacy of an earlier institution founded in 1857. Under founding president William Rainey Harper, the university quickly developed a strong faculty and established a rigorous academic structure, including an undergraduate division split between an Academic College and University College. It fostered early adoption of the quarter system, instituted the nation’s first graduate professional school (Divinity School), and supported intercollegiate athletics. Despite financial challenges in its early decades, substantial philanthropic support stabilized its budget and enabled expansion.

Throughout the 20th century, the University of Chicago advanced innovative educational reforms such as Robert Maynard Hutchins' core curriculum and maintained a focus on both undergraduate liberal arts education and graduate research. It played a significant role in major scientific developments, including contributions to the Manhattan Project, and cultivated notable academic movements like the Chicago School of economics and sociology. The Hyde Park campus features architecturally significant Gothic and modern buildings. Today, it enrolls over 7,500 undergraduates and nearly 11,000 graduate students, operates seven professional schools including law, business, and medicine, and sustains world-renowned research centers like Fermilab and Argonne National Laboratory. The university is highly selective, emphasizing a rigorous liberal arts education and extensive research activity, and maintains a robust student life with a distinctive house system and long-standing traditions.
