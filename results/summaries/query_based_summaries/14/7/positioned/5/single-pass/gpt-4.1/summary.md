## University of Chicago – Concise Summary

The University of Chicago, founded in 1890 through donations from the American Baptist Education Society and Standard Oil co-founder John D. Rockefeller, is a private, coeducational research university located in the Hyde Park and Woodlawn neighborhoods of Chicago. The campus spans 217 acres and is renowned for its Collegiate Gothic architecture and historic buildings, such as Mitchell Tower and the Main Quadrangles, patterned after the colleges of Oxford. Over its history, the university established a pioneering core curriculum, fostered the birth of the Chicago Schools of thought in economics and sociology, and made significant contributions to science, including the Manhattan Project and the first controlled nuclear chain reaction led by Enrico Fermi.

Academically, the university is organized into an undergraduate college, four graduate divisions, and seven professional schools, including highly ranked law, business, medicine, and public policy programs. The institution operates on a quarter system and offers 51 majors and 33 minors at the undergraduate level. The University of Chicago is classified as an "R1: Doctoral Universities – Very high research activity" institution, manages national laboratories (Argonne and Fermilab), and maintains a vast library system with over 11 million volumes. It is consistently ranked among the top ten universities in global rankings and boasts affiliations with 101 Nobel laureates, numerous MacArthur Fellows, Rhodes Scholars, and other distinguished alumni and faculty. The university supports vibrant student life, athletics (NCAA Division III Maroons), and more than 400 student organizations, maintaining strong traditions and a commitment to academic freedom and rigorous inquiry.
