# University of Chicago

Founded in 1890 by the American Baptist Education Society, the University of Chicago began as a coeducational institution in Hyde Park with significant endowments from John D. Rockefeller and Marshall Field. The university was established to offer a rigorous academic environment, distinguishing itself early with a unique "Great Books" curriculum and a focus on liberal arts. Key historical figures included President William Rainey Harper, who expanded the faculty and introduced intercollegiate athletics, and later Presidents like Robert Maynard Hutchins, who reorganized the curriculum under the "New Plan" and emphasized core general education. The university is renowned for its research output, hosting the Enrico Fermi Institute and the James Franck Institute, and serving as the site of the Manhattan Project's Metallurgical Laboratory, where Enrico Fermi achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction.

The University of Chicago is classified as an "R1: Doctoral Universities – Very High Research Activity" institution, ranking among the top globally in the Academic Ranking of World Universities. It is home to seven professional schools, including the Booth School of Business and the Pritzker School of Medicine, and operates major research facilities such as Fermilab and the Marine Biological Laboratory. The campus features the iconic Main Quadrangles designed in the Gothic style, alongside modern structures like the Mansueto Library. While the university has faced financial challenges, including deficits requiring debt accumulation and budget cuts, it remains a prestigious center for scholarship, producing numerous Nobel laureates and influential alumni in fields ranging from economics and physics to law and business.
