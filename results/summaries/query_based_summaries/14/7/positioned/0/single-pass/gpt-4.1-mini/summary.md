The University of Chicago, established in 1890 with significant funding from John D. Rockefeller, evolved from the Old University of Chicago (founded 1857 and closed 1886). It quickly distinguished itself as a major research university with a comprehensive undergraduate program divided into an Academic College and a University College, and introduced a quarter system and a core curriculum under President Robert Maynard Hutchins in the early 20th century. The Hyde Park campus, characterized by Collegiate Gothic architecture inspired by Oxford, spans 217 acres and hosts numerous historically significant buildings and research institutes such as the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures and the Enrico Fermi Institute. The university has consistently emphasized interdisciplinary research and maintains satellite campuses globally, including locations in Hong Kong, London, and Paris.

Academically, the University of Chicago comprises an undergraduate college, four graduate divisions, seven professional schools (including the Booth School of Business, Law School, and Pritzker School of Medicine), and a broad library system holding over 11 million volumes. It is renowned for pioneering the liberal arts program, the Socratic method, and research excellence, notably in economics, sociology, physics (home to Chicago Pile-1, the first controlled nuclear chain reaction), and molecular engineering. The university is highly selective, need-blind for domestic applicants, and maintains a vibrant student body with extensive extracurriculars and Greek life. Its faculty and alumni include numerous Nobel laureates, Fields Medalists, and leaders in various sectors, emphasizing its prestigious reputation and rich academic tradition.
