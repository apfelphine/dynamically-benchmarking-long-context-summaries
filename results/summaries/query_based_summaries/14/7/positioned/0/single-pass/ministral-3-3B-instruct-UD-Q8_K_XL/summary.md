The **University of Chicago** was founded in 1890 by a group of Baptist educators and incorporated after a land endowment from Senator Stephen A. Douglas and contributions from John D. Rockefeller. Initially struggling financially, the university transitioned into a coeducational institution in 1890, with Rockefeller’s support stabilizing its finances. Key milestones include the establishment of the Divinity School in 1891, the adoption of maroon as its official color in 1894, and the creation of the University of Chicago Press and extension programs.

Under the leadership of William Rainey Harper, the university expanded its curriculum, emphasizing research and liberal arts education. Notable figures like Harry Pratt Judson and Robert Maynard Hutchins further shaped its academic and administrative development. The university played a pivotal role in scientific advancements, including the discovery of the Higgs boson, the Manhattan Project, and the development of radiocarbon dating. It also fostered the Chicago School of Economics and sociology.

The campus, located in Hyde Park and Woodlawn, Chicago, features a mix of Gothic and modern architectural styles, including iconic buildings like Rockefeller Chapel and the Joe and Rika Mansueto Library. The university operates on a quarter system and offers a wide range of undergraduate and graduate programs across its seven divisions, seven professional schools, and the Graham School of Continuing Liberal and Professional Studies.

The University of Chicago is renowned for its academic rigor, research output, and global reputation, consistently ranking among the top universities in the world. It is governed by a board of trustees and has a substantial endowment, supporting extensive research initiatives and educational programs. The university’s commitment to interdisciplinary scholarship and innovation continues to make it a leading institution in higher education.
