The University of Chicago was founded in 1890 by a group of Baptist educators with financial support from Senator Stephen A. Douglas and a $400,000 donation from John D. Rockefeller. It began operations in 1891 and was initially known as the "Old University of Chicago," which closed in 1886 due to financial struggles. The new University of Chicago was established in 1890 and operated on a quarter system, offering a general curriculum and a medical school. William Rainey Harper became its first president in 1891 and played a key role in establishing faculty, research, and a strong academic culture. The university adopted the motto "Crescat scientia; vita excolatur" (Let knowledge grow from more and more; and so be human life enriched) in 1911 and established the Divinity School in 1891. During the early 20th century, the university faced financial difficulties, leading to austerity measures and a reliance on Rockefeller donations. The university's medical school was significantly impacted by the Great Depression and World War I, with the medical school being closed in 1940 and later re-established. In 1942, the university's Metallurgical Laboratory contributed to the Manhattan Project, with Enrico Fermi engineering the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction at Stagg Field. The university faced public scrutiny during the 1930s and 1940s for alleged communist influence. In the 1950s, the university experienced financial strain and underwent restructuring, including the merger of the University of Chicago with the University of Chicago's medical school. In 1961, George Beadle was elected chancellor, and the university launched a major fundraising campaign in 1964 to rebuild after financial cuts. In 1967, the university's provost Edward Levi became president, and during his tenure, sit-ins and protests over student policies occurred. In 1975, John Wilson became president and balanced the budget through cuts. In 1978, Hanna Holborn Gray became the first woman to serve as president of a major research university in the United States. She modernized financial systems, increased undergraduate college size, and invested in student life. In 1992, Hugo F. Sonnenschein became president and initiated a major fundraising campaign that helped alleviate financial problems. In 1996, Sonnenschein proposed reducing the general education curriculum from four years to two years and reducing the number of required courses in the core curriculum from 21 to 15–18. In 2000, Don Michael Randel became president and focused on arts and outreach. In 2006, Robert J. Zimmer was appointed president and oversaw a major expansion of campus facilities, including the construction of the Mansueto Library, the Logan Center for the Arts, and the Keller Center. The university also expanded its international presence with the opening of campuses in Hong Kong, London, and Paris. In 2020, the university faced criticism for allowing an ethnic minority student convicted of rape to remain enrolled, leading to a public review and eventual expulsion of the student. The university's main campus is located in Hyde Park, Chicago, and includes the Main Quadrangles, the Robie House, the Henry Hinds Laboratory for Geophysical Sciences, and the Gerald Ratner Athletics Center. The university has a total of 10,546 graduate students and 7,569 undergraduate students. The university's endowment is the 21st largest among American educational institutions, valued at approximately $10.1 billion. The university's academic departments include the College of the University of Chicago, the Divinity School, the Booth School of Business, the Law School, the Crown Family School of Social Work, Policy, and Practice, the Pritzker School of Medicine, and the Harris School of Public Policy. The university is known for its strong research programs, with a focus on economics, law, and medicine. The university is considered one of the most prestigious research universities in the United States and has produced numerous Nobel laureates, including physicist Peter Higgs, economist Milton Friedman, and Nobel laureate John Maynard Keynes. The university is also known for its strong emphasis on liberal arts education and its commitment to academic freedom and intellectual inquiry.
