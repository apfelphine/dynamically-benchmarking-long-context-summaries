# University of Chicago

The University of Chicago was founded as a new institution in 1890 by the American Baptist Education Society, supported by a substantial donation from John D. Rockefeller and other Chicago benefactors. Opening its Hyde Park campus in 1892 under the leadership of President William Rainey Harper, it quickly developed rigorous academic programs, a core curriculum, and professional schools, including its Divinity School. The university played a pivotal role in research, notably contributing to the Manhattan Project during World War II, with key faculty like Enrico Fermi engineering the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction on campus. Despite financial challenges through its history, strategic leadership and fundraising, particularly under Harry Pratt Judson and Robert Maynard Hutchins, stabilized and shaped its academic environment.

The campus spans 217 acres in Chicago's Hyde Park and Woodlawn neighborhoods, known for its Collegiate Gothic architecture and modern expansions like the Joe and Rika Mansueto Library. It maintains extensive transit access and a comprehensive security system. The university comprises an undergraduate college with 51 majors organized into four divisions, multiple graduate divisions, seven professional schools (including renowned Business, Law, and Medicine schools), and numerous research institutes. It operates significant national laboratories such as Fermilab and Argonne National Laboratory. The university is globally recognized for its research output, rigorous academics, and faculty and alumni achievements, including 101 Nobel laureates. The academic culture emphasizes a core curriculum rooted in the Socratic method, and the university fosters a rich arts community. Its varsity athletics compete in NCAA Division III as the Maroons. Student life includes over 400 clubs, a dedicated student government, and a house system for undergraduates.
