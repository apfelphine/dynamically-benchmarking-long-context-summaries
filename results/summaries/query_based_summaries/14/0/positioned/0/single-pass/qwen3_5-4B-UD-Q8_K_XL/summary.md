King's College London, founded in 1829 as a rival to the secular London University, was established in response to theological controversy and the Industrial Revolution, with patronage from King George IV. Initially Anglican-only, it evolved to become a coeducational institution with diverse religious participation. The college underwent significant structural changes, including a 1908 merger with the University of London that ended its legal independence, and regained autonomy in 1980. A major milestone occurred in 2006 when it was granted independent degree-awarding powers, a status it began exercising in 2007. Today, King's operates on five campuses in central London, including the Strand, Waterloo, Guy's, St Thomas', and Denmark Hill sites, and comprises nine academic faculties covering arts, business, dentistry, law, life sciences, medicine, nursing, psychiatry, and social sciences.

The university is a leading research institution with a substantial endowment, ranked highly in global university rankings, particularly in psychology, health, and law. It boasts a distinguished alumni network including Nobel laureates such as Peter Higgs and Michael Levitt, as well as prominent figures in politics, arts, and science. King's College London is also a founding member of the Francis Crick Institute and a key partner in the London Centre for Nanotechnology. Its academic profile includes rigorous admission standards, a focus on STEM and medical research, and a vibrant student life supported by a dedicated union, numerous sports clubs, and a rich cultural tradition.
