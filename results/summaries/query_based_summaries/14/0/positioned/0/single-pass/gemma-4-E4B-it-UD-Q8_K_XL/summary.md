# King's College London Summary

King's College London was founded in 1829, named in recognition of King George IV's patronage, partly in response to the secular nature of London University. Its establishment represented a Tory response aiming to reaffirm established educational values. The college opened in October 1831, and initially, the prospectus permitted nonconformists, although governors and professors were required to be members of the Church of England. In 1835, both King's and "London University" became affiliated colleges as the government established an examining board, leading to the formation of the University of London in 1836, with King's students receiving the first University of London degrees in 1839.

Key developments throughout the 19th and 20th centuries include the opening of a hospital in 1840, the appointment of Joseph Lister in 1877, and the establishment of a Military Department in 1845. The King's College London Act 1882 changed the corporation's name and extended education to include women, with the Ladies' Department opening in 1885. In 1910, King's merged into the University of London, losing its legal independence, though it later regained legal independence in 1980. Crucial scientific research occurred at King's, including contributions to the discovery of the DNA double helix structure in 1953. Today, King's is based across five central London campuses.
