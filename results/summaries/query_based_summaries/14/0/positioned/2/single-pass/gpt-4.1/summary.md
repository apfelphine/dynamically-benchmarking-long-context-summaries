## King's College London: Key Facts and Profile

King's College London, established in 1829, is a major research university located in central London. Originally founded with the patronage of King George IV as a response to UCL's secularism, King's maintains a unique historical connection with the monarchy and the Anglican Church. Its academic and medical roots stretch back to the 16th century, and the institution played significant roles in 19th- and 20th-century higher education reforms. King's comprises nine academic faculties covering humanities, sciences, law, medicine, dentistry, nursing, engineering, social science, business, and psychiatry. It offers a wide range of undergraduate and postgraduate programs, with independent degree-awarding powers since 2007.

King's operates across five urban campuses (Strand, Guy's, Waterloo, St Thomas', and Denmark Hill), as well as additional sites for sports and specialist training. As of 2024, it boasts a research income of £256.9 million and an endowment of £324.8 million, positioning it among the UK's wealthiest universities. King's is a founding member of the University of London and part of the elite "golden triangle" of research universities, with strong international partnerships and collaborations, including the Francis Crick Institute, King's Health Partners, and joint ventures in mathematics and nanotechnology. Renowned for its contributions to science (most notably the discovery of the DNA double helix), medicine, law, and the arts, King's consistently ranks among the top global and UK universities for both employability and academic reputation. The student community is diverse, actively engaged in more than 300 clubs and societies, with vibrant traditions, media, and sports—including a historical rivalry with University College London. King's distinguished alumni and staff include Nobel laureates, artists, politicians, scientists, writers, and leaders in various sectors.
