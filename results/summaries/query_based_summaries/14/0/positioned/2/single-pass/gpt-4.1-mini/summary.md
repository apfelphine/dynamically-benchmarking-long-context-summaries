# Summary of King's College London

King's College London (KCL) was founded in 1829 under the patronage of King George IV as a response to the secular London University, emphasizing Anglican educational values. Its origins trace back to a medical school with roots in the 16th century. Notable early events include the 1829 duel between the Duke of Wellington and the Earl of Winchilsea, symbolizing political tensions at the college's founding. KCL evolved through the 19th and 20th centuries, expanding its departments and merging with institutions such as Queen Elizabeth College, Chelsea College, and the Institute of Psychiatry. The college gained independent degree-awarding powers in 2006 and was a founding partner in the Francis Crick Institute in 2011. Its campuses are located primarily in central London, including the historic Strand Campus and medical-related sites at Guy’s and St Thomas’, Waterloo, and Denmark Hill, with additional facilities like the recent Bush House acquisition.

Academically, KCL is organized into nine faculties covering arts, sciences, health, law, business, and social sciences, including the renowned Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology and Neuroscience and the Dickson Poon School of Law. It operates as a constituent college of the University of London and is part of elite groups like the Russell Group and the "golden triangle" of UK research universities. King’s has an annual income exceeding £1.2 billion with significant funding from tuition, research grants, and endowments. The student body is diverse, with substantial British ethnic minorities and international students, and the university maintains a broad array of student activities, including a strong students’ union, media outlets, and over 60 sports clubs. Notable alumni include 14 Nobel laureates such as Peter Higgs, distinguished figures in politics, law, literature, and the arts, reflecting the college’s historic and ongoing impact.
