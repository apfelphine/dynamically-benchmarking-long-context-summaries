King's College London (KCL) was founded in 1829 as King's College, originally named in honor of King George IV, in response to the establishment of London University. The college was created as a rival to London University, aiming to provide a secular education while maintaining Anglican traditions. The founding was contentious, involving a duel between supporters of King's and the Earl of Winchilsea, who opposed the college's secular nature.

Initially, King's College operated under a religiously restrictive charter, but it gradually expanded its educational scope. In 1882, the King's College London Act transformed the college into a non-profit institution, extending its educational opportunities to women. The college merged with other institutions over time, including Queen Elizabeth College and Chelsea College in 1985, and the Institute of Psychiatry in 1997, forming a comprehensive university.

King's College London gained independence from the University of London in 2006, allowing it to award its own degrees. The university is renowned for its diverse academic programs across nine faculties, including arts and humanities, dentistry, law, life sciences, natural sciences, nursing, psychiatry, social sciences, and business. It is a member of the Russell Group and has a strong reputation for research, particularly in health sciences and social sciences.

The university operates across five main campuses in central London, with the Strand Campus being the central hub. It houses notable institutions like the Maughan Library, the Gordon Museum of Pathology, and the Science Gallery London. King's College London is known for its contributions to medical research, including pivotal roles in the discovery of the structure of DNA, and its commitment to interdisciplinary studies and global engagement. The university emphasizes inclusivity, with a significant international student body and a diverse faculty.
