King's College London was founded in 1829 by King George IV as a rival to the secular London University, establishing itself as a Tory institution rooted in the Church of England. Initially a constituent college of the University of London, it gained independent degree-awarding powers in 2006. Today, it operates across five campuses in central and South London, including the Strand Campus, Guy's Campus, and the Denmark Hill Campus, housing nine faculties such as Life Sciences and Medicine, Law, and the Institute of Psychiatry. The university is a member of the Russell Group and a founding partner of the Francis Crick Institute, renowned for its medical research, historical contributions like the discovery of DNA, and a student body with a significant international presence.

The administration is governed by a Council with a Vice-Chancellor and Principal, Ellen Clark-King serving as the Dean, who maintains the university's Anglican traditions. Financially, King's reported total income of £1.271 billion in the fiscal year ending July 2024. It holds the fourth-largest endowment of any UK university and is a key participant in King's Health Partners. The institution is highly ranked globally, particularly in subjects like Health Studies, Law, and Psychology, and is notable for its rich history of notable alumni including Nobel laureates, Heads of State, and prominent cultural figures.
