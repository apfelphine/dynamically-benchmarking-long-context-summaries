**Education in Finland**  

Finland's education system emphasizes equity, student well-being, and holistic development. Compulsory education spans from age 7 to 18, comprising a nine-year comprehensive school (peruskoulu) divided into primary (grades 1–6) and middle school (grades 7–9). Small class sizes (typically 20 students), minimal homework, and relaxed, informal learning environments are hallmarks. Students learn two languages (Finnish/Swedish) alongside core subjects, with arts, music, physical education, and practical skills integrated into the curriculum.  

Teachers are highly autonomous, following state guidelines but choosing their own methods and textbooks. Grading is primarily verbal in early years, transitioning to a 4–10 scale with half-grades and quarter-grades; failing grades require improvement exams, and repeating a year is rare and only considered after extensive support. Pupils receive free meals, books, and school trips, with strong emphasis on reading for pleasure and outdoor activities.  

Upper secondary education (lukio/gymnasium or ammattikoulu/yrkesskola) is now compulsory from age 15–18. Students can choose between academic tracks (leading to university) or vocational tracks (leading to applied sciences or skilled work). Admission to academic schools is based on grades and performance, with a national matriculation exam (ylioppilastutkinto) serving as a key gateway to higher education. Vocational graduates can access university of applied sciences, and vice versa.  

Adult education includes "Free Education" (vapaa sivistystyö) through independent institutes offering diverse, non-degree courses focused on personal development, community engagement, and lifelong learning.  

Finland's system is renowned globally for its success, with a focus on phenomenon-based learning (introduced in 2016), collaboration, creativity, and critical thinking. The system is also recognized for its high-quality, equitable access to education, with strong international ties through programs like "Education Finland," promoting Finnish pedagogical models abroad.  

Tuition is free at all levels, and the system is largely publicly funded, with private schools strictly limited to faith-based or Waldorf models and required to offer equal access to education and social benefits as public schools.
