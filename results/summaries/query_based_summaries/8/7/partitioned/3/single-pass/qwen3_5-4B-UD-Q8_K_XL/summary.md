Finland's education system is characterized by a strong emphasis on equity, small class sizes, and high teacher autonomy, with schools primarily funded and administered by municipalities. The compulsory system consists of a nine-year comprehensive school (grades 1–9) followed by three to four years of upper secondary education, which became compulsory in September 2021. This upper secondary level bifurcates into academic tracks preparing students for universities and vocational tracks for applied sciences, though pathways between them are fluid. The curriculum prioritizes phenomenon-based learning to foster 21st-century skills, and while grading is formalized, the system avoids high-stakes standardized testing in favor of continuous assessment and teacher-led evaluation. Finland also boasts extensive free early childhood education and adult learning options, such as "Free Education" institutes, to support lifelong development and social inclusion.

The tertiary sector is divided into traditional universities focusing on theoretical research and universities of applied sciences focusing on practical vocational training. Both sectors are merit-based and transparent, with admissions determined by GPA and final exams rather than essays or extracurriculars. The system has undergone structural reforms, including the merger of institutions and the adoption of the Bologna Process, which introduced a three-cycle degree structure (bachelor's, master's, doctorate). While historically free for all, non-EEA students must pay tuition fees since 2017. Future prospects face challenges regarding rising academic unemployment and the need to balance student place expansion with economic demands, prompting discussions on shifting focus toward vocational training and addressing demographic shifts in the workforce.
