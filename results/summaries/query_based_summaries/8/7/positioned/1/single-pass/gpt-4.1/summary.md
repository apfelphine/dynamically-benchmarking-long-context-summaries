## Education in Finland – Key Features

Education in Finland is characterized by universal access, high quality, and equity at all stages. Early childhood education is widely accessible and focused on play-based, child-centered development until age seven, when children begin formal schooling. Comprehensive education, which became mandatory nationwide in 1921, currently requires participation from ages 7 to 18 and includes a nine-year comprehensive school (grades 1–9). This system is administered and funded primarily by municipalities, with private schools being rare, non-selective, and also publicly funded. Students are offered free daily lunches, free learning materials, and social entitlements such as healthcare. Classes are small, teachers are highly trained (requiring a master’s degree), and instruction emphasizes autonomy, minimal homework, and minimal high-stakes testing.

After completing compulsory education, students proceed to upper secondary education, which since 2021 has become mandatory unless the student is 18, and is divided into vocational and academic (lukio) tracks. Both tracks are tuition-free and include access to school healthcare, free books, and transportation. Vocational and academic secondary graduates can qualify for higher education; universities focus on research and theoretical subjects, while universities of applied sciences emphasize professional skills and industry partnerships. Tertiary admissions are transparent and merit-based, relying mainly on GPA, examinations, and entrance tests. Tuition is free for Finnish and EU/EEA students, with fees applying to non-EU/EEA students from 2017. Lifelong learning is supported through adult education centers, open university courses, and "Free Education" institutes accessible to the public.

Finland’s curriculum emphasizes phenomenon-based learning and 21st-century skills such as collaboration, communication, and critical thinking. All students learn multiple languages, with Finnish or Swedish as a mother tongue, and additional language study up to high school level. Internationally, Finland’s educational model and expertise are exported through various programs and “Finnish-inspired” schools abroad. System restructuring, such as university mergers, is ongoing to improve cooperation and efficiency. Challenges include aligning education supply with labor market needs and addressing academic unemployment; recent reforms continue to place Finland as a model for inclusive, equitable, and future-oriented education.
