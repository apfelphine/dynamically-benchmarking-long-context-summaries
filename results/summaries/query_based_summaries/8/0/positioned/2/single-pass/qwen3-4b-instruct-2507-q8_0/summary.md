Education in the United States began with the establishment of free public schools in New England, led by Horace Mann, emphasizing the three Rs and teacher training. After the Civil War, high schools emerged in cities, while the South lagged behind. The 1823 founding of the Columbian School in Vermont marked the start of formal teacher training. The U.S. Office of Education was created in the 1860s to standardize education, though it lacked enforcement power. States passed compulsory education laws from 1852 onward, with Massachusetts being the first. The Committee of Ten (1892) recommended a 12-year education with eight years of elementary and four years of high school. 

The 1940s saw a surge in high school enrollment, and the 1954 Brown v. Board of Education decision mandated racial desegregation. The 1965 Elementary and Secondary Education Act (ESEA) provided federal funding, and the 1975 Education for All Handicapped Children Act established special education funding. The No Child Left Behind Act (2001) introduced standardized testing and accountability, later replaced by the Every Student Succeeds Act (2015). The 21st century saw increased emphasis on standardized testing, curriculum standardization, and school safety, with ongoing debates over equity, teacher pay, and curriculum content. 

The U.S. education system is decentralized, with states managing most policies. Public education is free from kindergarten through 12th grade, with tuition fees required in private schools. The U.S. ranks 10th in the world for college completion rates and has a literacy rate of 99% among those over 15. However, it faces challenges including socioeconomic disparities, racial achievement gaps, and a significant student loan debt. The pandemic disrupted education, leading to learning loss and mental health issues. The system is marked by a strong emphasis on standardized testing, teacher shortages, and debates over curriculum content and equity.
