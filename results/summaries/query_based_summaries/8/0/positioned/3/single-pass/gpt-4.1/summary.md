## Education in the United States

Education in the United States is overseen by federal, state, and local governments, with substantial decentralization resulting in significant variation in policies and curriculum. Schooling is compulsory, typically beginning at age five or six and continuing to age sixteen or eighteen depending on the state. The system is structured into early childhood education (preschool/kindergarten), primary (elementary) education, secondary (middle and high school), and tertiary (college/university) education. Public education is provided free from kindergarten through 12th grade, funded primarily through local, state, and federal sources, with substantial disparities in spending between wealthy and poor districts due largely to property tax-based funding. Private schools, including religious and independent institutions, form a significant part of the educational landscape, while homeschooling is also recognized.

The U.S. has high rates of high school and college graduation, though achievement gaps persist by race, socioeconomic status, and region. Education since the 19th century progressed from basic compulsory schooling to widespread access to high school and college by the 20th and 21st centuries, marked by numerous federal and state initiatives including the No Child Left Behind Act, the Common Core Standards, Title I funding for low-income students, affirmative action policies, and recent efforts to address teacher shortages and student debt. Higher education is largely deregulated, offering diverse programs and institutions, but faces rising costs and student loan debt. Major issues include disparities in school funding, standardized testing debates, teacher compensation, curriculum differences, the school-to-prison pipeline, and ongoing challenges around educational equity and adequacy. U.S. students generally achieve high literacy rates but score below average internationally in math and science, with ongoing concerns about the quality of learning outcomes.
