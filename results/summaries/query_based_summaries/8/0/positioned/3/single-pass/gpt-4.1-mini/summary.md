## Education in the United States

The U.S. education system has evolved from early public schooling efforts in New England during the 19th century, expanding through compulsory education laws adopted progressively by states from 1642 to as late as 2019 in some territories. Initially focused on elementary education, high schools and universities grew rapidly by the mid-20th century, with significant federal legislation shaping education access and funding, such as the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965), the Higher Education Act, and the No Child Left Behind Act (2001), replaced by Every Student Succeeds Act (2015). The system is decentralized, with significant state and local control over policy and funding, although the federal government provides about 8.5% of K-12 funds, primarily targeting disadvantaged students and those with disabilities.

Public education is compulsory generally from ages 5 or 6 to 16 to 18, depending on the state. The K-12 structure covers elementary, middle, and high school levels, with high school graduates optionally pursuing higher education at over 4,400 institutions, including universities, colleges, community colleges, and vocational schools. College admissions rely on a mix of GPA, standardized tests (SAT/ACT), extracurriculars, and other criteria. Higher education shows diversity in institution types, costs, and student debt levels, with student loans topping $1.5 trillion (2018). Despite high graduation rates, issues persist, including widespread remediation needs, inequities based on socioeconomic and racial backgrounds, achievement gaps, and rising tuition vs. income. The COVID-19 pandemic further stressed the system, accelerating technology use but highlighting disparities in access.

Educational funding relies heavily on local property taxes, causing disparities between wealthy and poor areas and resulting in legal challenges around equity and adequacy. Teacher shortages and turnover are increasing, with issues linked to low pay and burnout despite teachers holding higher degrees. Curricula and standardized testing vary widely, with debates over their appropriateness and effectiveness amid calls for greater educational equity and quality. The U.S. also faces challenges such as the school-to-prison pipeline, racial achievement gaps, and controversies over affirmative action and LGBT-related curricula. Reading and literacy remain concerns, with significant portions of adults exhibiting low proficiency linked to social outcomes such as crime.
