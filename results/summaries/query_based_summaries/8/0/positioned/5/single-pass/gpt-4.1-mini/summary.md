## Education in the United States

Education in the United States evolved from early 19th-century reforms focusing on free public elementary schools, largely influenced by figures like Horace Mann. Compulsory education laws spread across states between 1852 and 1917, with public high schools and universities expanding throughout the 20th century. The system is decentralized, with governance shared primarily between state and local authorities, and overseen federally by the Department of Education. Education stages include preschool, elementary, middle, and high school (K–12), followed by optional higher education through colleges and universities, vocational schools, and community colleges. Education is typically mandatory until ages 16 to 18 depending on the state.

Public education is primarily funded through local, state, and federal sources, but disparities exist, especially between wealthy and poor districts. Tuition fees at the higher education level vary widely, with significant student loan debts posing social challenges. Standardized testing has been emphasized since laws like No Child Left Behind, though it remains controversial due to inequities and teaching to the test. The United States faces issues such as racial and economic achievement gaps, teacher shortages, variations in curriculum and quality, school safety concerns, and debates over affirmative action. Despite high spending per student, academic performance has stagnated comparatively in recent decades. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated online learning but widened educational inequities. US higher education is diverse and extensive, with thousands of institutions offering varying degrees, but faces challenges in cost, credential inflation, and adjunctification.
