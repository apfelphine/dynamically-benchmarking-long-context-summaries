Education in the United States is a publicly funded, decentralized system governed by federal, state, and local authorities. Compulsory education begins at age six, covering primary (K–6) and secondary (7–12) education, with graduation making students eligible for higher education. The system emphasizes standardized testing, with federal policies like the **No Child Left Behind Act** and **Every Student Succeeds Act** mandating accountability measures.

**Early Childhood Education** includes preschool programs like Head Start, though only 69% of 4-year-olds attend. **K–12 Education** is divided into elementary (grades K–6), middle/junior high (7–9), and high school (10–12). High school graduation rates have improved, but disparities persist based on socioeconomic status, race, and geography. **Higher Education** is largely voluntary, with over 7,000 post-secondary institutions offering degrees from community colleges to elite universities. Tuition costs vary widely, with public institutions generally more affordable than private ones, though student loan debt remains a significant issue.

Key challenges include **achievement gaps**, **teacher shortages**, and **affordability**, with debates over standardized testing, curriculum standards, and equity in funding. The U.S. ranks below average in international assessments like PISA but remains a leader in higher education, with a high percentage of adults holding college degrees. The system’s flexibility allows diverse educational paths, though it also faces criticism for uneven quality and access.
