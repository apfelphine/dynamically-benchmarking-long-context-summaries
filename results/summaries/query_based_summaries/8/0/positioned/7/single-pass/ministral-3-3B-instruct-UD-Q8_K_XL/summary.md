Education in the United States is a publicly funded system with significant federal, state, and local governance. Compulsory education begins at age 5–6 (kindergarten) and lasts through 12th grade, with an average of 12 years of schooling. The system is divided into **K–12 education** (elementary through high school) and **higher education**, including universities, colleges, and vocational programs.

### **K–12 Education**
- **Structure**: Public schools are primarily funded by local and state taxes, with federal support for disadvantaged students (e.g., Title I funding). Private schools exist but are not federally regulated.
- **Curriculum**: Standards vary by state, with recent reforms emphasizing **Common Core** and standardized testing (e.g., No Child Left Behind, later replaced by the **Every Student Succeeds Act**). Core subjects include **English, mathematics, science, and social studies**, though curricula can differ widely.
- **Compulsory Attendance**: Mandatory from age 5–6 (kindergarten) to 16–18 (varies by state), with graduation requirements tied to standardized tests.
- **Challenges**: Disparities in funding, achievement gaps between racial/ethnic groups, and socioeconomic backgrounds persist. Issues include **teacher shortages**, **underfunded schools**, and debates over **standardized testing** and **curriculum content**.

### **Higher Education**
- **Structure**: Over **4,495 colleges and universities**, including public, private, and for-profit institutions. Public universities are state-funded, while private institutions rely on tuition and endowments.
- **Admissions**: Competitive, with standardized tests (SAT/ACT) and high school transcripts as primary criteria. Many universities offer merit-based and need-based scholarships.
- **Costs**: Tuition varies widely—public universities average **$7,020/year** for in-state students, while private institutions can exceed **$50,000/year**. Student loan debt has surged, reaching **$1.5 trillion** by 2018.
- **Outcomes**: About **89.8% of adults** have a high school diploma, and **34% hold a bachelor’s degree or higher**. However, disparities exist, with wealthier students more likely to attend and complete college.

### **Key Issues**
- **Funding**: Local property taxes fund most public schools, leading to inequities between wealthy and poor districts. Federal funding (e.g., Pell Grants) supports low-income students.
- **Equity**: Racial and socioeconomic achievement gaps remain significant, with Black and Hispanic students often underperforming compared to White and Asian students.
- **Teacher Shortages**: Low pay, burnout, and lack of support contribute to high turnover rates, particularly in rural and low-income areas.
- **Standardized Testing**: Debates continue over its role in evaluating students and teachers, with critics arguing it overemphasizes test scores at the expense of creativity and critical thinking.

The U.S. education system balances **local control** with **federal oversight**, reflecting its decentralized structure. While it offers broad access to education, challenges in **equity, funding, and teacher quality** persist.
