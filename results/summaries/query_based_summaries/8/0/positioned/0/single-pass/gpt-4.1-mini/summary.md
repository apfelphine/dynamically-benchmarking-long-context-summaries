# Education in the United States

Education in the United States has evolved significantly since the 19th century, beginning with Massachusetts’ pioneering of free public elementary schools and teacher training in normal schools. Post-Civil War efforts expanded education, including high schools and land-grant colleges, but the South lagged behind due to segregation and unequal funding. Compulsory education laws were widely adopted by 1917, and the 20th century saw rapid increases in high school and college graduation rates, development of private and public higher education institutions, and landmark policies such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954) mandating school desegregation. The federal government has influenced education through legislation like the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965) and No Child Left Behind (2001), emphasizing standardized testing and accountability. The COVID-19 pandemic posed challenges including transitions to virtual learning and financial strains on institutions.

Education is compulsory until ages 14–18 depending on the state, with free public education from kindergarten to 12th grade. The system is typically divided into elementary, middle, and high school levels, followed by optional higher education at over 4,000 colleges and universities. Higher education is varied, with public and private universities, community colleges, and vocational schools offering associate, bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees. Challenges include educational inequality, achievement gaps linked to socioeconomic and racial factors, teacher shortages with low retention due to pay and burnout, debates over affirmative action, and critiques of standardized testing. Funding largely comes from state and local sources, supplemented federally, with ongoing court cases addressing equity and adequacy. Despite high literacy rates, concerns remain regarding science and math proficiency, student loan debt, and adapting curricula to diverse student needs and 21st-century skills.
