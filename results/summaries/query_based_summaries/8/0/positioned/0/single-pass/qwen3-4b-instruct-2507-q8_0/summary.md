**Education in the United States**

The U.S. education system is decentralized, with states holding primary control over public education, while the federal government provides oversight and funding. Compulsory education began in the 1800s, with states adopting laws from 1852 onward, and by 1910, 72% of children were enrolled in school. The 20th century saw expansion of high school enrollment, with the 1946 National School Lunch Act and the 1965 Elementary and Secondary Education Act (ESEA) introducing federal support and targeted funding for disadvantaged students. The 1975 Education for All Handicapped Children Act established special education, and the 1983 "A Nation at Risk" report spurred widespread reform efforts.

The system is divided into K–12 and higher education. K–12 includes early childhood (preschool), elementary (K–5), middle (6–8), and high school (9–12), with a traditional 12-year path. The Common Core State Standards were adopted to standardize curriculum, though criticized for emphasizing test preparation. In the 21st century, the No Child Left Behind Act (2001) mandated standardized testing, and the Every Student Succeeds Act (2015) replaced it. The pandemic disrupted learning, leading to a 3% drop in enrollment and mental health challenges. Student loan debt reached $1.5 trillion by 2018, contributing to financial strain.

Higher education is largely deregulated, with over 4,400 institutions offering diverse programs. Public universities are state-funded, while private institutions charge higher tuition. The average college graduate earns over $51,000 annually, significantly above the median for high school graduates. However, disparities persist in racial, socioeconomic, and geographic outcomes, with minority and low-income students facing systemic disadvantages. The system faces ongoing challenges including teacher shortages, inadequate funding, and the impact of policy decisions on equity.

Key statistics show that 89% of adults have completed high school, and 34% hold a bachelor’s degree or higher. The U.S. ranks 10th in college completion among industrialized nations. Despite high educational attainment, the U.S. ranks below average in science and mathematics in international assessments. A 2018 study found that 40% of students in grades 1–3 were not proficient in reading, and literacy remains a concern. The system is marked by significant socioeconomic disparities, with access to quality education, resources, and opportunities unevenly distributed.
