# Education in the United States

The history of education in the United States is characterized by the 19th-century shift toward free, tax-funded public schooling, pioneered in Massachusetts and later standardized through state-level common schools. While the federal government established the Office of Education during Reconstruction to track data, it lacked enforcement power, leaving policy development primarily to individual states. This decentralized system faced significant regional disparities, particularly regarding segregated education in the South and the development of parochial schools, which were largely prohibited by the Blaine Amendment. Compulsory education laws were gradually enacted across states between 1852 and 1918, driven partly by mass immigration and economic needs.

In the 20th century, the system expanded rapidly, with high school enrollment surging and the percentage of adults with college degrees rising significantly by the turn of the millennium. Key legislative milestones included the 1954 *Brown v. Board of Education* decision mandating desegregation, the 1965 Elementary and Secondary Education Act (ESEA) providing federal aid, and the 1975 Education for All Handicapped Children Act ensuring special education access. The 21st century saw reforms such as the No Child Left Behind Act (2001), which mandated standardized testing, and the Every Student Succeeds Act (2015). Despite these efforts, challenges persist, including widening achievement gaps based on race, socioeconomic status, and geography, as well as issues regarding teacher shortages, rising student loan debt, and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on educational equity.

The U.S. education structure divides into distinct stages: preschool, elementary, middle, and high school (K–12), followed by higher education. Funding is predominantly a shared responsibility among local, state, and federal governments, with local property taxes playing a significant role, often creating disparities between wealthy and poor districts. Higher education comprises a vast array of public and private institutions, including universities, colleges, and community colleges, offering diverse pathways from vocational training to research-focused degrees. While the U.S. ranks highly in reading literacy and has a large number of college graduates, international assessments often show lower performance in mathematics and science compared to other developed nations. Issues such as corporal punishment in some states, the school-to-prison pipeline, and debates over curriculum content (including sex education and history textbooks) continue to shape the ongoing discourse on educational policy.
