**Education in the United States**

The U.S. education system has evolved since the 19th century with roots in free public schooling funded by taxation, led by figures like Horace Mann. Compulsory education laws spread across states from the mid-1800s to the early 20th century, with the system expanding from basic elementary schooling to include high schools and universities. Public education is primarily funded and regulated at state and local levels, with federal involvement through initiatives like the Department of Education, and legislation including the Elementary and Secondary Education Act and No Child Left Behind. The system comprises preschool (non-compulsory), K–12 education (compulsory until ages 14–18 depending on state), and higher education, including universities, colleges, community colleges, and vocational schools. The U.S. has a decentralized curriculum and a mix of public, private, and charter schools, with standardized testing playing a significant role in assessment.

Higher education is diverse and largely deregulated, offering myriad programs across thousands of institutions, with admissions based on a combination of academic records, standardized tests (like SAT and ACT), essays, and recommendations. Costs vary widely, with public universities typically less expensive than private, but rising tuition and student loan debt remain critical issues. Teacher education requires a master’s degree, with teaching considered a respected yet challenging profession facing shortages and turnover. Key challenges include disparities in achievement linked to race and socioeconomic status, debates over affirmative action, standardized testing, curriculum content, and school safety. The COVID-19 pandemic underscored inequalities, prompted a shift to online learning, and posed financial threats to many educational institutions, amplifying existing gaps and prompting ongoing reforms.
