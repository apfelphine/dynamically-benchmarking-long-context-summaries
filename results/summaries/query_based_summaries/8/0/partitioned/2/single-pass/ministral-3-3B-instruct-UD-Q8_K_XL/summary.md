Education in the United States is structured into **K–12 schooling** and **higher education**, with key features including:

### **K–12 Education**
- **Compulsory through age 16 (or 18 in some states)**. Public education is free, though many schools charge voluntary fees for supplies, uniforms, or extracurricular activities.
- **Curriculum**: Primarily taught in English, with some bilingual programs for non-native speakers. Core subjects include math, science, history, and English, with standardized testing (e.g., NAPLAN) assessing proficiency.
- **School Structure**: Divided into **elementary (K–6)**, **middle/junior high (7–9)**, and **senior high (10–12)**. High school graduation is required for higher education.
- **Funding**: Primarily state-funded, with federal support for disadvantaged students (e.g., Title I programs). Funding disparities exist between affluent and low-income districts.
- **Issues**: Persistent achievement gaps by race, socioeconomic status, and geography. High teacher turnover and low salaries contribute to a **teacher shortage**, while standardized testing and curriculum debates (e.g., Common Core) remain contentious.

### **Higher Education**
- **Universities and colleges**: Over 4,500 institutions, including public, private, and for-profit schools. Public universities are more affordable for in-state students, while private institutions charge higher tuition.
- **Costs**: Undergraduate tuition averages **$10,000–$30,000/year** (public) and **$30,000–$60,000/year** (private). Student loan debt has reached **$1.7 trillion**, with 40 million borrowers.
- **Admissions**: Competitive, with standardized tests (SAT/ACT) and high school transcripts as key factors. Many universities offer merit-based scholarships.
- **Graduation Rates**: Only **32% of students graduate in four years** at public universities, with disparities between wealthy and low-income students. The U.S. ranks **10th** globally in college completion rates.
- **Issues**: Rising costs, brain drain (graduates leaving for better-paying jobs), and debates over curriculum content (e.g., critical race theory, gender inclusivity).

### **Key Trends**
- **Diversity**: Over **58% of students** are non-white, reflecting the nation’s demographic shifts.
- **Technology**: Digital learning tools and online courses are expanding access, though inequities persist (e.g., rural students lacking internet).
- **Policy**: Federal initiatives like the **Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA)** aim to reduce achievement gaps, while debates continue over standardized testing, teacher pay, and curriculum standards.

The U.S. education system balances **accessibility** with **rigorous standards**, though systemic inequities remain a major challenge.
