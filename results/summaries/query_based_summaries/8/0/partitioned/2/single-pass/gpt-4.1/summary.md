## Education in the United States

Education in the United States is primarily divided into three formal stages: early childhood education, K–12 education, and higher education. Early childhood education consists of preschools and kindergartens, functioning largely as non-compulsory programs preparing children for elementary schooling. K–12 education, which is mandatory until ages 16–18 depending on the state, encompasses elementary (grades K/1–5/6), middle (grades 6–8), and high school (grades 9–12). Most students attend public schools funded by local, state, and federal governments, while private and home schooling are also prevalent. The school year traditionally runs from late August or early September through late May or early June. Graduation from high school is required for entry into higher education, which includes options such as vocational schools, associate, bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral degrees at colleges and universities.

The U.S. education system has evolved over time due to various reforms and legislative acts—such as the Morrill Land-Grant Acts, Brown v. Board of Education, the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, No Child Left Behind, and Every Student Succeeds Act—aimed at expanding access and improving educational outcomes. Despite high rates of high school completion and college enrollment, significant disparities in achievement persist across racial, economic, and geographic lines. Notable issues include inequality in funding, achievement gaps, standardized testing challenges, teacher shortages, and the rising cost of higher education and student debt. International assessments often place U.S. students below many developed countries in math, science, and reading, though the country remains home to globally prominent universities.
