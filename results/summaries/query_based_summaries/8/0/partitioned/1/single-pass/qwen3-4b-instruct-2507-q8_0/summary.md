Education in the United States features a decentralized system with significant variation in quality and access across regions and socioeconomic groups. Public education is compulsory until age 16 (or 18 in some states) and includes a nine-year K–12 structure, with students progressing through elementary, middle, and high school. The system emphasizes standardized testing, with high-stakes assessments used to evaluate school performance, though critics argue these tests reflect systemic inequities rather than true academic ability. A major issue is the racial and economic achievement gap, with Black and Hispanic students often scoring lower on standardized tests and facing higher dropout rates and lower college enrollment. Teacher shortages are exacerbated by low salaries, high turnover, and burnout, with many teachers working over 50 hours per week and taking on second jobs to supplement income. The cost of higher education is a significant barrier, with student loan debt exceeding $1.5 trillion and graduates earning significantly more than those with only a high school diploma. Remedial education is common, with over 40% of students in remedial courses failing to complete them. The system also faces challenges related to curriculum content, including debates over the inclusion of subjects like astronomy and geography, and controversies over sex education, LGBT curriculum, and textbook bias. Despite these issues, the U.S. ranks among the top nations in the world for the number of college graduates and has a strong emphasis on higher education, with a high return on investment for top-tier institutions. The education system has also been impacted by the pandemic, with widespread disruptions and learning loss, especially among students from low-income backgrounds. Efforts to improve equity, such as expanding access to early childhood education and increasing teacher pay, are ongoing but face political and financial hurdles.
