Education in the United States is a decentralized system where state and local governments hold primary authority over public schooling, with federal funding playing a supporting role. The system is structured into elementary, middle, and high school stages, followed by optional higher education at colleges and universities. While compulsory education is mandated until age 16 or 18 in most states, significant disparities persist based on socioeconomic status, race, and geography. International assessments often show U.S. students ranking below developed nations in mathematics and science, though American universities are highly prestigious and globally ranked. Higher education is largely deregulated and expensive, characterized by rising tuition, high student loan debt, and a growing reliance on adjunct instructors. Recent legislative changes, such as the Every Student Succeeds Act, have shifted emphasis toward standardized testing and accountability, though debates continue regarding curriculum content, affirmative action, and the quality of teacher training and retention.

Issues facing the system include persistent achievement gaps, a teacher shortage driven by low pay and burnout, and challenges related to school funding inequities and safety. There is ongoing controversy surrounding standardized testing, the "school-to-prison pipeline," and the integration of diverse curricula including sex education and bilingual instruction for immigrant students. Despite these challenges, the U.S. maintains a robust higher education sector with over 4,000 institutions, offering diverse pathways from community colleges to research-intensive universities, though access and outcomes remain heavily influenced by family wealth and background.
