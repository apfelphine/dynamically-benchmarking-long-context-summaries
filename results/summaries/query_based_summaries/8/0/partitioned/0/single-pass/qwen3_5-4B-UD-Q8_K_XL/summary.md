Education in the United States is a decentralized system where states hold primary authority, though the federal government influences policy through funding and legislation. Historically, New England led the way in public education reform, establishing normal schools to train teachers and promoting free, compulsory schooling. The 19th and 20th centuries saw the expansion of high schools, the rise of land-grant colleges, and significant civil rights legislation such as *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), which mandated racial desegregation. Federal interventions include the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965), which provided Title I funding for low-income students, and the Higher Education Act (1965), which established the Pell Grant program.

In the 21st century, federal focus shifted toward accountability through the No Child Left Behind Act (2001), which mandated standardized testing, and the Every Student Succeeds Act (2015), which returned more control to states. Despite high literacy rates, the U.S. faces persistent challenges including a widening achievement gap based on race and socioeconomic status, teacher shortages driven by low pay and high burnout, and rising student loan debt. Higher education is characterized by a diverse mix of public and private institutions, with tuition costs rising significantly faster than inflation, creating a financial burden for families and contributing to credential inflation where the number of graduates exceeds the number of jobs requiring a degree.
